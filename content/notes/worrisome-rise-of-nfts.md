## Summary

Published in Nautilus magazine's Inspiration issue, this article examines the NFT boom of 2021 through a critical lens — focusing on the actual consequences rather than the promotional framing that dominated crypto media at the time. Nautilus approaches the topic as science journalism, which means interrogating claims rather than amplifying hype.

The core concerns the article addresses: proof of work blockchains (primarily Ethereum pre-merge) consume electricity at nation-state scale to mint and transfer NFTs. This environmental cost is externalized — paid by everyone through carbon emissions rather than by the parties transacting. The artificial scarcity model creates speculative value divorced from utility; an NFT encodes ownership of a pointer to a file, not the file itself, yet was trading at prices that implied otherwise. Wealth concentrations in early adopters and insiders mean the mechanism consistently transfers value from latecomers to incumbents.

Saved in January 2022 when the NFT market was near its peak. The critique here complements Molly White's Web3 Is Going Just Great (incident tracking) and Moxie Marlinspike's technical critique (the web3 infrastructure critique). Together these form a body of evidence-based skepticism that was difficult to find in mainstream coverage at the time.

## Key points

- Environmental cost: proof of work Ethereum transactions for NFT minting consume significant energy — cost externalized to public
- Artificial scarcity mechanism: NFTs encode ownership of a pointer, not the underlying asset; files remain copyable
- Wealth transfer pattern: speculative markets systematically favor early adopters and technically sophisticated participants over ordinary buyers
- NFT boom peaked in early 2022; by late 2022 volumes had collapsed 97% from peak
- Nautilus occupies a gap — science journalism applying empirical standards to claims that crypto media rarely subjected to scrutiny

## Summary

Patrick Riley wrote this while leading the data science team for Google Search logs — datasets at a scale and complexity that make standard textbook analysis fail in non-obvious ways. The essay is a distillation of hard-won lessons: the things that go wrong when you're analyzing hundreds of millions of rows of behavioral data that weren't generated by a clean experimental process.

The core advice covers three areas. First, sanity checks before analysis: count the rows, check distributions, look for data quality issues (nulls, duplicates, unexpected value ranges) before running any model. The advice sounds obvious but is routinely skipped. Second, stratification: large datasets can show statistically significant effects that are entirely explained by a confounding variable (time of day, device type, locale). Always check whether results hold across subgroups. Third, the danger of p-hacking at scale: with enough data, nearly everything is statistically significant, so statistical significance becomes meaningless as a decision criterion. Effect size matters more than significance.

The essay also covers distinguishing correlation from causation in observational data, the importance of having a clear question before looking at data (not the reverse), and the value of getting a second pair of eyes on any analysis before acting on it.

## Key points

- Always sanity-check data quality before analysis: nulls, distributions, duplicates, range checks.
- Stratification: at scale, aggregate effects often disappear or reverse in subgroups (Simpson's paradox).
- Statistical significance is nearly meaningless with massive data — focus on effect size.
- Formulate the question before looking at the data; looking first produces p-hacking and confirmation bias.
- From Patrick Riley at Google — experience with Google Search logs at production scale.

## Summary

[Eigengrau](/notes/eigengrau/) (German: intrinsic gray, also called brain gray or eigenlicht) is the uniform dark gray color that human eyes perceive in complete absence of light. When you close your eyes or are in a totally dark room, you don't see true black — you see a slightly lighter gray, approximately #16161d in hex, caused by spontaneous neural firing in the visual cortex and retina in the absence of any photon input.

The phenomenon arises because the visual system is not a passive sensor. Photoreceptors and neurons fire stochastically even when not stimulated by light — this background noise creates a perceived signal. The dark adaptation process partially reduces this background noise, which is why eyes that have been adapted to darkness for 20+ minutes perceive a slightly darker eigengrau than freshly adapted eyes.

The practical implication: absolute black (0,0,0 RGB) doesn't look "natural" in display design for dark themes — it appears unnaturally deep because human perception in real environments never encounters true zero light levels. [Eigengrau](/notes/eigengrau/) is sometimes used as an argument for dark mode backgrounds that use near-black rather than pure black. The color also appears in astronomical contexts: the Olbers' paradox question of why the night sky is dark (rather than bright, if the universe is infinite and uniform) intersects with the visual perception question of what "dark" actually means to a human observer.

## Key points

- Eigengrau ≈ #16161d: measurably lighter than pure black — the visual system's baseline noise floor.
- Caused by spontaneous neural firing in visual cortex and retina — perception is active generation, not passive recording.
- Dark adaptation: 20-30 minutes in darkness lowers the noise floor and darkens the perceived eigengrau slightly.
- Design relevance: pure black (0,0,0) backgrounds on displays can feel harsher than near-black because humans never experience true absence of light in natural environments.

## Summary

Dalton Caldwell wrote this essay as the founder of App.net — which he had launched specifically because Twitter had made clear it was pivoting away from being a developer platform. His inside view is more specific than outside critics': Twitter was not *becoming* a media company, it had *decided* to become one, and every API restriction followed from that strategic choice.

Caldwell's argument: Twitter was pivoting from a communication protocol (an open substrate for messages and replies that developers could build on) to an attention platform (controlled media environments optimized for advertising). These are fundamentally different products. The first thrives with ecosystem openness; the second requires controlling the experience to maximize engagement metrics tied to ad revenue.

App.net was Caldwell's practical response — a paid social networking API without advertising, designed so the business model aligned with developer and user interests rather than against them. The crowdfunding campaign raised $800k in 30 days. The project ultimately failed to reach critical mass (it shut down in 2017), but the analysis in this essay proved correct: Twitter's restrictions continued, third-party client development effectively died, and the platform became increasingly ad-optimized. Mastodon and Bluesky emerged later from the same frustration.

## Key points

- Twitter's strategic pivot: from open communication protocol to controlled ad-supported media platform
- Dalton Caldwell built App.net as the direct alternative — paid, developer-aligned, no ads
- The business model determines the product: ad revenue requires controlling user experience; subscription revenue aligns with user value
- App.net succeeded as analysis, failed as product — couldn't achieve the critical mass Twitter had
- Mastodon (2016) and Bluesky (2023) are later attempts at the same alternative architecture

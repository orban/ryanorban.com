## Summary

Pierre Calleja, a French biochemist, built a street lamp prototype powered entirely by microalgae. The lamp contains a tank of living algae suspended in water. The algae absorb CO2 from the surrounding air as part of photosynthesis, and the system harnesses that biological activity to generate electricity — or in some versions, the bioluminescence of the algae directly provides light. The concept generates roughly the same amount of light as a standard street lamp while absorbing approximately 200 times more CO2 than a tree.

The design belongs to a broader category of biofuel and bioreactor approaches that try to convert living organisms into useful energy or material systems. Algae are attractive for this because they grow fast, require only sunlight, water, and CO2, and can be cultivated in non-agricultural spaces. The microalgae lamp concept had been covered as one of many speculative sustainable design proposals in the early 2010s, when algae biofuel was getting serious VC attention before most of the startups in the space ran into scale and cost problems.

The visual appeal (the lamp glows green, the tanks look extraterrestrial) drove significant media coverage disproportionate to the technology's readiness level. The practical path from a single prototype to city street infrastructure is enormous: maintaining living organisms at scale, keeping them alive through temperature variation, managing algae die-off and replacement. But as a demonstration of biomimicry and living materials thinking, it's a genuine proof-of-concept.

## Key points

- Microalgae absorb CO2 + sunlight → electricity (or bioluminescence) to power lamp.
- Claimed CO2 absorption: ~1 ton per year per lamp, roughly 200x more than a tree.
- Part of broader algae biofuel research wave of 2010–2015 (ExxonMobil, Solazyme, etc.).
- Scale challenges: maintaining living organism cultures in infrastructure is fundamentally different from passive materials.
- Connects to synthetic biology and living materials design movements seeking carbon-negative construction.

## Summary

Fast Company covered research into what distinguishes serial innovators — people who produce multiple significant innovations across their careers — from one-time innovators or non-innovators. The key differentiator wasn't primarily creativity in generating solutions, but SKILL in identifying which problems were worth solving in the first place.

The research (drawing on work by Claudio Feser and studies of corporate innovators) found that serial innovators spend disproportionate time on problem definition rather than solution generation. They resist the cognitive pull to jump to solutions — which is the natural tendency — and instead investigate: Why does this problem exist? Who actually has it? What would a world without it look like? What constraints are actually fixed vs. assumed?

This connects to the design thinking movement's emphasis on problem reframing. IDEO and the d.school at Stanford were popularizing the idea that the best solutions often involve redefining the problem. Clayton Christensen's "jobs to be done" framework was gaining adoption around the same time — the idea that customers hire products to do jobs, and understanding the actual job (rather than the stated need) is the insight work.

The corollary: most organizations are structured to reward solution execution, not problem selection. This creates systematic bias toward working on the wrong problems competently.

## Key points

- Serial innovators distinguish themselves primarily in problem selection, not solution generation.
- They resist the solution pull: the natural cognitive tendency to jump to answers before the problem is understood.
- Problem definition work: why does this exist? Who has it? What would its absence look like?
- Connects to design thinking (IDEO, Stanford d.school) and jobs to be done (Christensen).
- Organizational incentive problem: most companies reward solution execution, not problem selection — creating systematic misallocation.

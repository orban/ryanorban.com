## Summary

Chelsea Troy argues that data-driven innovation is a contradiction in terms. Data tells you about the past: what users did, what converted, what was clicked. Innovation by definition involves doing something users haven't done yet, measuring something you haven't measured, or betting on a future behavior pattern that doesn't yet exist in your data. You can't use historical behavioral data to find genuinely novel paths forward — you can only use it to optimize existing ones.

The essay traces the phrase's prevalence to a specific failure mode: tech companies, particularly those staffed by early web founders who were wealthy but not especially market-savvy, discovered that A/B testing and metrics-driven product work produced reliably improving numbers. This created a culture where anything that could be quantified and A/B tested was treated as the entire domain of product decision-making. But A/B tests can only compare variants of things you already do. They can't evaluate whether you should be doing something entirely different.

The deeper point is about the difference between optimization and exploration. Data is excellent at optimization — finding the best version of something you've already defined. It's poor at exploration — finding entirely new problems to solve or entirely new categories of value. Companies that are genuinely innovative (Apple's iPod, Airbnb in its early days) made decisions based on conviction about user needs, not behavioral data — because the behavioral data for those products didn't exist yet.

## Key points

- Data-driven processes can only optimize over the space of things already being measured — they can't find genuinely new territory.
- Innovation requires bets on unmeasured futures; over-reliance on data produces local optima thinking.
- A/B testing is optimization, not discovery — it finds the better of two options you've already defined.
- Survivorship bias problem: successful innovators look data-driven in retrospect because you can construct a data narrative post-hoc.
- The critique: using data-driven as a shield against taking positions protects teams from accountability for bad bets.

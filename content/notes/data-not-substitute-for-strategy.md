## Summary

This LinkedIn Pulse piece appeared in March 2013 at the height of big data hype — when it was fashionable to argue that enough data could replace managerial judgment, intuition, and strategic thinking. The article pushed back: data informs decisions, it doesn't make them.

The core argument is that data analysis requires a prior theory of what matters. Without strategic framing, you can drown in metrics while missing the important ones. Big data tools give you the ability to measure almost anything, but measurement isn't insight — and insight isn't strategy. Companies that confused correlation with causation, or mistook data quantity for analytic quality, were making expensive mistakes even as they invested heavily in Hadoop clusters and data warehouses.

This was a minority view in 2013 but a prescient one. The subsequent decade saw repeated high-profile failures of data-driven decision making: recommendation systems that optimized for engagement over wellbeing, A/B tests that measured the wrong things, and analytics teams that generated endless reports that nobody acted on. Data and strategy need to be integrated, not substituted.

## Key points

- Data analysis without a strategic frame answers questions nobody cares about — the problem of measurement without direction
- Correlation vs causation: big data's volume makes spurious correlations abundant and harder to distinguish from causal relationships
- A/B testing optimizes for measurable proxies, not business outcomes — optimizing the wrong thing precisely is worse than a rough approximation of the right thing
- The data-driven organization framing of the early 2010s often confused operational metrics (dashboards, KPIs) with strategic intelligence
- Decision-making requires combining data, domain expertise, and strategic judgment — none of the three is sufficient alone

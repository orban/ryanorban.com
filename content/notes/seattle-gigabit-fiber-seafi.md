## Summary

In December 2012, Seattle Mayor Mike McGinn announced the SeaFi initiative — a city plan to bring gigabit fiber infrastructure to Seattle. The announcement followed Google Fiber's November 2012 launch in Kansas City, which had demonstrated that consumer gigabit internet was technically and economically viable at scale and was generating significant competitive and civic pressure on other cities to develop comparable infrastructure.

Google Fiber Kansas City was a watershed moment: it offered 1 Gbps symmetric connections for $70/month, nearly 50x faster than the national average, proving the technology worked and changing consumer expectations. Cities that had been dismissing fiber infrastructure as impractical suddenly faced public demand for comparable service. Seattle's SeaFi proposal was one of many municipal responses.

The outcomes were more modest than the announcements. Municipal broadband projects faced substantial barriers: incumbent ISP lobbying (several states passed laws restricting municipal broadband), capital requirements, operational complexity, and the political difficulty of running a utility. Seattle's SeaFi was eventually absorbed into ongoing discussions about franchise renewal with Comcast rather than becoming a city-owned network. The broader pattern: Google Fiber's demonstration effect was real, but municipal fiber mostly succeeded only where cities had existing utility infrastructure (municipal electric utilities in particular).

## Key points

- Google Fiber Kansas City (November 2012): the catalytic event — demonstrated 1 Gbps consumer fiber was viable; created competitive pressure on cities nationwide
- SeaFi: Seattle's response; Mayor McGinn's initiative to bring gigabit fiber to Seattle following Google Fiber's launch
- Municipal broadband challenges: incumbent ISP lobbying, state laws restricting municipal networks, capital requirements, operational complexity
- Success pattern: municipal broadband worked best where cities already operated electric utilities (Chattanooga TN, with EPB, being the most successful case)
- Historical resolution: Seattle moved toward franchise negotiation rather than city-owned network; most municipal gigabit announcements from 2012-2014 produced slow results

## Summary

Aaron Swartz published "Raw Nerve" as a series of short essays in late 2012. The name comes from the idea that growth requires touching the parts that hurt — the uncomfortable realizations and honest self-assessments we usually avoid. This bookmark is to the index page for the series.

The essays cover topics like confirmation bias and how smart people use intelligence to avoid changing their minds, learned helplessness applied to everyday situations, the difference between being in denial vs. having genuinely thought something through, and how to take feedback without becoming defensive. Each piece is short — Swartz wrote to be read, not to demonstrate complexity.

What makes them worth revisiting is that Swartz applied the same standards to himself that he was writing about. He was publicly committed to intellectual honesty and critical self-examination in a way that made these feel like genuine findings rather than borrowed self-help advice. He was also writing them in the year before his death, which gives the collection about facing hard truths an added weight in retrospect.

## Key points

- Confirmation bias: smart people are better at constructing post-hoc justifications for what they already believe, not at being more accurate
- Learned helplessness in everyday contexts: people often stop trying in areas where they've failed before, even when circumstances have changed
- Growth requires actively seeking disconfirming evidence, not just passively being open to it
- The difference between healthy skepticism and motivated reasoning is whether you apply the same scrutiny to things you want to believe
- Taking feedback well is a learnable SKILL; defensiveness usually signals something worth examining

## Summary

This faircompanies video profile covers Kijani Grows, an Oakland-based urban aquaponics operation using Arduino microcontrollers to monitor and automate their growing system. Aquaponics combines fish cultivation with hydroponic plant growing: fish waste provides nutrients for plants, plants filter the water for fish — a closed-loop system that requires careful monitoring of water chemistry, temperature, pH, and oxygen levels. The Arduino hardware automated this monitoring, creating what the team called an internet of food application.

In 2012, the phrase "Internet of Things" wasn't yet mainstream vocabulary, but the practice — connecting physical objects and sensors to software systems — was being explored in small-scale agricultural and environmental applications. Kijani Grows was an early example of applying cheap, programmable sensor hardware to food production: Arduino boards reading water sensors, triggering alarms when parameters drifted, potentially enabling remote monitoring via web interfaces.

The urban aquaponics context adds another layer. Growing fish and vegetables in an urban environment, with software managing the biological system, connected to a broader conversation about food security, urban agriculture, and distributed food production. The scale was small — a community operation rather than industrial — but the combination of biological systems, hardware sensors, and software control was a preview of what would become the precision agriculture and urban farming tech categories.

## Key points

- Aquaponics as a closed-loop biological system: fish waste → plant nutrients → water filtration for fish — requires tight parameter monitoring.
- Arduino microcontrollers as the control layer: reading water sensors (pH, temperature, dissolved oxygen) and triggering automated responses.
- Early IoT before the term: networked physical sensors applied to biological systems in 2012, before Internet of Things entered common usage.
- Kijani Grows as a community-scale model: demonstrating that aquaponics with sensor automation was accessible without industrial infrastructure.
- Connection to broader urban agriculture and food security conversations — software-managed food production as a response to distribution and access problems.

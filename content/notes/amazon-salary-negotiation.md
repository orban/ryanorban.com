## Summary

Amazon's compensation structure is unusually complex compared to other major tech companies, and Josh Doody's Fearless Salary Negotiation guide explains the specific levers available when negotiating an offer. The core complication: Amazon's RSU (Restricted Stock Unit) vesting schedule has a steep cliff — 5% in year 1, 15% in year 2, then 40% in year 3 and 40% in year 4. This means most of your equity value is back-loaded, which Amazon partially compensates with large sign-on bonuses in years 1 and 2 to smooth actual take-home pay.

This structure creates specific negotiation dynamics. Sign-on bonuses are one-time payments that Amazon uses to make year-1 pay competitive without committing to long-term base salary increases. RSU refreshers (additional grants for retention) kick in after year 2 but aren't guaranteed. Negotiating effectively at Amazon means understanding which levers are real: base salary (capped at $160-180k for most roles, unusually low for big tech), sign-on bonus (more flexible), and RSU grant size (the primary long-term lever).

The article likely covers: how to evaluate an Amazon offer vs. FAANG peers, why the vesting cliff matters for job-hopping math, how to counter-offer effectively given Amazon's specific structure, and what "Band" (internal pay grade) you're being offered. Amazon's internal banding system has less salary flexibility than Google or Meta, making negotiation somewhat different.

## Key points

- Amazon RSU vesting: 5%/15%/40%/40% — back-loaded to discourage attrition; sign-on bonuses offset year 1-2 shortfall
- Base salary cap (~$160-180k most roles): much lower than Google/Meta/Apple — RSUs are the real compensation lever
- Negotiation levers ranked: RSU grant size > sign-on bonus > base salary (least flexible)
- Level (L4-L7 most common) determines band — negotiate level before negotiating compensation
- Counter-offer strategy: get a number from them first, then counter with specific asks rather than general "more"

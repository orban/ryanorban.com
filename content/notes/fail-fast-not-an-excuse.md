## Summary

Michael O. Church wrote this post in 2012 as a direct pushback against how Silicon Valley culture had started using "fail fast" as a blank check for bad behavior. The legitimate version of fail fast — iterate quickly, test assumptions cheaply, don't over-build before validating demand — had been stretched into something else: an excuse for founders to act recklessly, mistreat employees, break commitments, and generally behave badly under the cover of startup culture.

The argument is pointed: failing fast on a *product hypothesis* is good engineering. Failing fast on *commitments to people* is just being unreliable. The conflation serves founders who want the freedom to operate without accountability while still claiming the intellectual legitimacy of Lean methodology. There's a meaningful difference between killing a product feature quickly because you learned it's wrong, and failing fast on your salary obligations or your word to a cofounder.

Church also notes the asymmetry: the fail fast philosophy is deployed almost exclusively as permission for founders and managers, not as an invitation for employees to fail fast on bad jobs. The power dynamics of who gets to invoke the mythology are not accidental.

## Key points

- Fail fast properly means: test product assumptions cheaply and quickly, kill bad ideas before they consume resources.
- It does not mean: break commitments to people, mistreat employees, or act recklessly because startups are hard.
- The Silicon Valley startup culture weaponized failure mythology to give founders cover for poor ethics.
- There's an asymmetry: "fail fast" is invoked by people in power over people with less power, rarely the reverse.
- Lean startup methodology is sound; its cultural corruption into an excuse for bad behavior is not.
- Accountability and speed are not in tension — you can iterate fast and still keep your word.

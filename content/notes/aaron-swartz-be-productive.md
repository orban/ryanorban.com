## Summary

Aaron Swartz wrote this productivity guide in 2005, and it circulated widely because it doesn't give the usual motivational advice. Swartz starts from the observation that most productivity systems assume you already want to do the work and just need better techniques. He takes the rarer position that the fundamental problem is often that you're working on the wrong things or that the work itself needs examining.

The post is organized around several concrete ideas: the importance of making sure you're working on things that matter to you (otherwise willpower is fighting against your own judgment), the value of structuring your environment so that doing the hard thing is easy, and the underrated role of physical and psychological well-being in cognitive output. He argues that procrastination is often your brain correctly signaling that something is wrong — either with the task, the approach, or your mental state.

Swartz also addresses the management problem: how to work through the parts of a task that genuinely aren't interesting. His answer is to combine tasks so boring work accompanies something enjoyable, to give yourself permission to do productive procrastination (working on a different valuable task when you're avoiding another), and to recognize that perfectionism is sometimes a form of avoidance.

## Key points

- Work on things that actually matter to you — if you're procrastinating constantly, examine whether you should be doing the task at all
- Environment design matters more than willpower: make the important work the default action, remove friction from starting
- Productive procrastination: switching to a different meaningful task is better than not working; most great work gets done when avoiding something else
- Physical maintenance — sleep, exercise, food — has large cognitive returns that people systematically underinvest in
- The hard part of most tasks isn't the difficult technical work but the emotional resistance before starting; once started, momentum often carries through

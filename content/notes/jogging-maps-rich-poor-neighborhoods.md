## Summary

CityLab (The Atlantic's urban affairs site) visualized GPS track data from running apps (likely Runkeeper and MapMyRun) to show where people jog across major cities. The striking finding: running routes are strongly constrained by neighborhood economic geography. People in wealthy neighborhoods run within and around those neighborhoods; people in lower-income neighborhoods who run at all tend to cross into wealthier adjacent areas. The maps visually encode inequality in a way that no demographic statistics alone could.

The data visualization uses heat maps of GPS track density — more popular running routes appear brighter. In cities like Boston, New York, and San Francisco, the patterns are stark: parks and waterfronts in wealthy neighborhoods accumulate enormous track density, while adjacent lower-income neighborhoods show sparse coverage. The spatial boundaries of wealth correlate tightly with the spatial boundaries of outdoor exercise.

The analysis illustrates the research value of consumer GPS data for urban science. Runkeeper, Strava, and similar apps were accumulating billions of miles of GPS tracks in this period — a behavioral dataset that demographers and urban planners had never had access to. The New York Times's Year in Running features and later Strava's Global Heatmap extended this pattern into mainstream visualization culture.

## Key points

- Consumer GPS data as urban research tool: billions of jogging tracks reveal spatial behavior patterns with neighborhood-level granularity.
- Jogging geography as a proxy for inequality: running routes cluster in wealthy areas (parks, waterfronts, safe streets) — the maps make economic segregation visible.
- Data visualization technique: heat map of GPS track density — brightness encodes route popularity, making spatial patterns immediately readable.
- Strava and Runkeeper as data sources: opted-in GPS tracks, biased toward tech-adjacent users, but large enough to find city-scale patterns.
- Urban science implication: revealed spatial boundaries of economic geography that survey data couldn't capture at this spatial resolution.

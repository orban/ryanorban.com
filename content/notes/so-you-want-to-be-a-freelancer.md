## Summary

Samuel Mullen's guide to freelancing as a software developer draws from direct experience rather than business school theory. The core topics: how to actually find clients (referrals beat cold outreach; your network is your pipeline), how to price work (hourly vs. fixed-price, why underpricing signals inexperience), how to handle the feast-or-famine cash flow problem, and what contracts need to cover to protect you when clients go sideways.

The business reality of freelancing that this piece doesn't sugarcoat: you need 3-6 months of runway before going solo, because your first clients take longer to close than you expect and payment terms are 30-60 days. The effective hourly rate after accounting for non-billable hours (sales, admin, marketing, downtime between contracts) is typically 60-70% of your nominal rate.

On pricing: the post argues against competing on price, which is a race to the bottom. The alternative — specializing in a domain or technology where you're demonstrably better than generalists — commands rate premiums and attracts clients who value expertise over cost. This matches the consulting pattern: the more fungible your skillset, the more competitive the market and the lower the rates.

## Key points

- Referral network as primary pipeline: most freelance work comes from previous clients and professional connections, not job boards or cold outreach.
- True effective rate: divide annual billings by actual hours worked (including admin, sales, downtime) to get real compensation — usually well below nominal hourly rate.
- Contract fundamentals: payment schedule (upfront deposit, milestone payments), scope creep clauses, and kill fees protect against the most common disaster scenarios.
- Cash flow management: recurring retainers or maintenance contracts provide baseline revenue; pure project work creates unpredictable income gaps.
- Specialization premium: expertise in a specific domain (Rails, data pipelines, fintech compliance) commands higher rates than general full-stack work.

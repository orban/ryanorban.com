## Summary

This Techonomy article draws an explicit historical parallel between the Homebrew Computer Club (founded 1975 in Menlo Park) and the 2013 biohacking / DIY biology movement. The Homebrew club was where Steve Wozniak showed early Apple designs, where Lee Felsenstein built community terminals, and where the idea of personal computing was born — ordinary people building and sharing computers in garages before there was a consumer market.

The argument: biohackers in 2013 are doing the same thing with biology. Community biology labs like Genspace (New York) and Biocurious (Silicon Valley) are giving citizens access to PCR machines, gel electrophoresis, and eventually CRISPR tools. The equipment is becoming affordable, the techniques are being open-sourced, and a community of enthusiasts is forming around shared spaces and tools. The Homebrew club's ethos — information wants to be free, democratize access, share everything — maps directly onto biohacking culture.

The counterargument the article acknowledges: biology's safety implications are different from computing. A buggy homebrew computer crashes; a buggy homebrew organism could have different consequences.

## Key points

- Homebrew Computer Club (1975): the community where personal computing was invented through sharing, tinkering, and open distribution of designs.
- DIY biology / biohacking 2013: community labs (Genspace, Biocurious) democratizing access to biological equipment and techniques.
- The parallel holds: democratizing tools → empowering non-institutional innovators → eventual commercial products.
- CRISPR (published 2012) was beginning to make gene editing accessible at this moment — the personal computer of biology.
- Key difference: biological safety implications constrain the ship it fast, fail fast ethos that worked for computing.
- Biohacking is an example of the recurring pattern of professional tools becoming consumer tools over one technological generation.

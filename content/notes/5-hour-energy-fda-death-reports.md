## Summary

In November 2012, the New York Times reported that 5-Hour Energy had been cited in 13 death reports filed with the FDA between 2009 and 2012. The reports also cited 92 serious adverse events including heart attacks. The story broke as the FDA was also investigating Monster Energy for similar adverse event reports, creating a wave of scrutiny over the high-caffeine energy drink category.

The regulatory context: adverse event reports filed with the FDA don't establish causation — they're self-reported by consumers, doctors, or companies when a product may have been associated with a health event. The mere existence of reports doesn't mean the product caused the deaths. But the volume and severity of reports triggered FDA investigation and drew congressional attention to whether energy drinks with very high caffeine content (5-Hour Energy has 200mg+ per shot) required stronger regulation.

The incident was a turning point for how caffeinated beverages were marketed and sold. Monster Energy and 5-Hour Energy were at the time classified as dietary supplements rather than beverages, which gave them more latitude on caffeine content and labeling than regulated food products. The FDA scrutiny pushed both companies toward reclassifying as beverages, which subjected them to standard caffeine safety limits.

## Key points

- 13 FDA adverse event death reports for 5-Hour Energy (2009-2012) — adverse events, not proven causation
- FDA adverse event reports are self-reported — they flag association, not causation
- Energy drinks as "dietary supplements" had more latitude than regulated beverages — labeling and caffeine limits differed
- Monster Energy faced similar scrutiny simultaneously — the whole high-caffeine supplement category was under review
- Led to industry shift: both companies moved toward beverage classification with stricter FDA oversight

## Summary

Lewis Lehe's interactive simulation lets you click on a single car to slow it down, then watch a traffic wave propagate backward through the stream of vehicles. This is the phantom traffic jam phenomenon: congestion that appears and travels backward with no accident or bottleneck at the front — just emergent behavior from individual cars following each other too closely.

The simulation is a beautiful example of how complex systems produce counterintuitive emergent behavior. Each car follows simple rules (maintain speed, brake when too close), but the interaction produces a collective phenomenon — a density wave — that moves opposite to traffic direction. Traffic flow theory models this mathematically as a conservation equation similar to fluid dynamics.

This also connects to data visualization as explanation: Lehe's tool demonstrates a principle that is hard to convey in words but immediately obvious when you can interact with it. Clicking to slow one car and watching the ripple makes emergent behavior tangible in a way no static diagram can.

## Key points

- Phantom traffic jams are emergent: no accident, no bottleneck, just density waves arising from car-following behavior.
- The wave travels backward (opposite to traffic direction) — a feature of the traffic flow conservation equation.
- Interactive simulations like this are effective at teaching complex systems intuition that verbal explanation fails to convey.
- Connected to fluid dynamics mathematically: traffic density obeys equations analogous to compressible flow.
- Lewis Lehe builds educational transportation simulations; this is one of several interactive pieces on traffic and urban systems.

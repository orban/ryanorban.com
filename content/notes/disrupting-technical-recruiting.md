## Summary

This piece proposed flipping the technical recruiting model: instead of recruiters working for companies to source candidates, have agents who work exclusively for engineers — representing their interests, negotiating on their behalf, and taking a cut of salary like a talent agent in entertainment. The analogy is to Hollywood talent agencies — CAA, WME — where the agent's loyalty is entirely to the talent, not to the studios.

The problem diagnosis was accurate for 2012: technical recruiting was broken because every incentive in the system pointed recruiters toward closing the deal rather than finding the right match. Recruiters are paid by companies, so they optimize for company preferences. Engineers were overwhelmed by low-quality cold contact from recruiters who didn't understand the work. The signal-to-noise ratio of recruiting outreach was terrible and getting worse.

The hire an agent model addresses this by creating a principal whose interests genuinely align with the engineer: the agent only gets paid if the engineer lands a great job at great compensation. This is effectively what Hired (launched 2012), Triplebyte (later), and similar platforms tried to build — though most still blurred the line between representing talent and serving employers. True developer-first talent representation at scale never quite materialized, but the critique of traditional technical recruiting it's based on aged well.

## Key points

- Traditional technical recruiting has a principal-agent problem: recruiters paid by companies, so engineer interests come second
- Talent agent model: agent represents developer exclusively, negotiates compensation, gets a cut — aligns incentives correctly
- Hollywood analogy: CAA and WME work for actors, not studios; technical talent should have equivalent representation
- Hired, Triplebyte, AngelList Talent tried variants of this; none fully replicated the pure agent model
- The underlying dynamic — developers flooded with low-signal recruiter outreach — only intensified as software demand grew

## Summary

On April 24, 2012, Google accidentally published details about Google Drive on its French blog before the official announcement — TechCrunch got the story first. Google Drive launched as a 5GB free cloud storage service, with paid tiers above that, deeply integrated with Google Docs, Gmail, and the rest of the Google ecosystem. The bookmarker's comment captured the startup community's reaction perfectly: "the sound of Dropbox share price hitting the floor."

Dropbox had been growing rapidly and was rumored to be preparing for an IPO. The fear was straightforward: Google had unlimited resources, deep OS/browser integration, and was offering the same service for free at competitive tiers. This was the same worry that had followed every Google product launch into a market with an existing startup — Google Reader vs RSS readers, Google Maps vs Mapquest, etc. The pattern was known and feared.

What actually happened was more interesting. Dropbox didn't collapse. Google Drive captured a significant user base but didn't dominate the productivity cloud storage market outright. Dropbox's simplicity and cross-platform reliability kept it competitive, and Box carved out the enterprise segment. The lesson (eventually) was that incumbent scale doesn't automatically win consumer markets — brand, UX simplicity, and trust matter alongside storage capacity and price. Google's brand for privacy and ecosystem lock-in was a double-edged sword.

## Key points

- Google Drive launched April 2012 with 5GB free — directly competing with Dropbox (2GB free at the time).
- Deep integration with Google Docs, Gmail, and Chrome meant Drive wasn't just storage — it was productivity infrastructure.
- The competitive fear: Google could undercut on price and bundle indefinitely; Dropbox couldn't match the distribution.
- Dropbox survived and grew — demonstrating that consumer attachment to simplicity and cross-platform reliability could resist Google's bundling.
- The launch accelerated Google Docs adoption as a collaborative suite, separate from the storage competition.
- Part of a broader Google ecosystem land-grab in 2012: Google+, Drive, and Nexus devices were all part of a coordinated strategy to compete with Apple and Facebook.

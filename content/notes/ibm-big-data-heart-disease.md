## Summary

In 2013, IBM announced an initiative to apply big data analytics and predictive modeling to cardiovascular health — identifying patients at risk of heart disease years before symptoms appear, using electronic health records, genetic data, and environmental factors as inputs. The goal was predictive analytics at scale: rather than treating disease after diagnosis, intercept risk early enough for preventive intervention.

The initiative reflected IBM's broader Watson / cognitive computing push into healthcare. IBM Watson Health was being positioned as the killer application for enterprise machine learning — complex, high-value domains where pattern recognition in large datasets could change outcomes. Healthcare had the right properties: massive datasets, high stakes, and a clear ROI case if models worked.

The practical difficulties were severe and largely underestimated in 2013. Healthcare data is siloed across providers, poorly structured, and full of missing values. HIPAA and privacy regulations constrained data sharing. The validation bar for clinical deployment is far higher than for commercial applications. Most of IBM's ambitious Watson Health predictions underdelivered significantly — the platform was wound down a decade later.

## Key points

- IBM 2013 initiative: predictive modeling on EHR + genetic + environmental data to forecast heart disease years in advance.
- Part of the broader IBM Watson Health positioning — cognitive computing as enterprise AI for high-value domains.
- The appeal was clear: preventive care has better outcomes and lower costs than acute treatment; ML-driven prediction could enable it at scale.
- Practical obstacles underestimated: siloed data, poor data quality, regulatory constraints, and the high validation bar for clinical models.
- IBM Watson Health was ultimately wound down around 2022, having failed to deliver on most of its healthcare AI promises.

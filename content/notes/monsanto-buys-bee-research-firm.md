## Summary

In spring 2012, Monsanto acquired Beeologics, a small research firm specializing in bee health and colony collapse disorder (CCD). The timing was pointed: neonicotinoid pesticides, including those associated with Monsanto-family agricultural chemicals, were under increasing scientific scrutiny as a likely driver of the mass die-offs of honeybee colonies that had been reported since 2006. The acquisition gave Monsanto direct influence over research into the very problem it was accused of causing.

Colony collapse disorder had become a serious agricultural concern by 2012. Honeybees pollinate roughly one-third of the food crops consumed in the US; their decline threatened everything from almonds to blueberries. Multiple studies pointed toward neonicotinoid insecticides — a class that Monsanto and other agricultural companies produced — as a significant contributing factor, alongside Varroa mites, fungal pathogens, and habitat loss. The causal picture was complex, which provided some cover for industry, but the correlation was strong enough to be alarming.

The acquisition landed as a regulatory capture story: a company under fire for harming pollinators now controlled research capacity that could study and shape the narrative around that harm. Beeologics had developed RNA interference (RNAi) tools for fighting bee pathogens — genuinely useful technology — but the context made the acquisition look like science-buying rather than science-funding. The story circulated widely because it fit an existing template: corporate interests acquiring the research that might regulate them.

## Key points

- Colony collapse disorder (CCD): mass die-offs of managed honeybee colonies beginning ~2006, with ~30% annual losses by 2012.
- Neonicotinoid pesticides implicated as a major driver — including imidacloprid and clothianidin, widely used in industrial agriculture.
- Beeologics specialized in RNA interference approaches to bee disease — legitimate science, acquired at a politically sensitive moment.
- The acquisition fit the regulatory capture pattern: company under scrutiny for an externality gains influence over its study.
- EU moved to restrict neonicotinoids in 2013; US EPA eventually placed restrictions in 2019.
- The CCD story is still unresolved — multiple stressors interact, making clean attribution difficult and policy responses contested.

## Summary

This Annals of Pharmacotherapy article makes the case that the US regulatory decision to move pseudoephedrine behind the pharmacy counter (Combat Methamphetamine Epidemic Act, 2005) and allow phenylephrine to replace it on open shelves was poor policy on pharmacological grounds. The authors' argument: phenylephrine is largely ineffective as an oral decongestant at the doses approved for OTC sale. Multiple controlled trials show it performs no better than placebo at the recommended 10mg oral dose. Pseudoephedrine, by contrast, is a proven, effective decongestant — it works.

The mechanism explains the difference. Pseudoephedrine is well-absorbed orally and reaches concentrations that constrict nasal vasculature. Phenylephrine is heavily metabolized on first pass through the liver; the amount that reaches systemic circulation is too small to have decongestant effect. Drug manufacturers substituted an ineffective compound for an effective one, and sold it at full price to consumers who were (and remain) largely unaware.

The policy backdrop: pseudoephedrine can be chemically converted to methamphetamine, so purchasing it now requires showing ID and entering data into a tracking system. The Combat Meth Act aimed to reduce meth production by limiting pseudoephedrine access. The effectiveness of this intervention is itself contested — meth production moved to Mexico in response, maintaining supply. The trade-off: slightly reduced precursor availability for domestic meth producers, at the cost of making effective OTC cold medicine harder to get for everyone else.

## Key points

- Phenylephrine oral bioavailability: highly variable, ~38% on average, much less at systemic levels — most evidence suggests it's ineffective at 10mg OTC doses
- Pseudoephedrine works: oral bioavailability ~90%, well-documented decongestant effect in controlled trials
- Combat Methamphetamine Epidemic Act (2005): pseudoephedrine moved behind the counter; purchase tracked in state databases
- The methamphetamine supply response: production shifted to Mexico, largely negating the domestic meth-reduction effect of the policy
- US pharmacy situation: pseudoephedrine still available (ask the pharmacist, show ID); some states require a prescription

## Summary

Michael O. Church's contrarian essay arguing that most startup jobs are genuinely bad deals for software engineers, written in 2012 during peak startup mania. The argument: startup equity is almost always worthless (most startups fail, and even successful ones often have liquidation preferences or dilution that zeroes out early employees), the salary discount is real, and the promised learning opportunities are often illusory — you end up doing maintenance work on a product that's barely alive.

The piece distinguished between good startup jobs and bad ones. Good startup jobs exist — early at a company with strong fundamentals, compelling equity, and actual engineering excellence. But crappy startup jobs are abundant: companies with no clear path to profitability, equity grants that are a rounding error even in success scenarios, 80-hour-week cultures that burn out engineers for no proportionate reward. The risk is not just financial — years spent at a struggling startup can be years not spent building skills or reputation that compound over time.

The broader context was a 2012 San Francisco where every coffee shop conversation was about disruption and every engineer was being recruited into some startup with a ping pong table and change the world messaging. Michael O. Church was a consistent dissenting voice, arguing that the tech industry's startup-first culture worked against most individual engineers' long-term interests.

## Key points

- Most startup equity is worthless in practice — failure rates plus liquidation preferences make early employee equity rarely pay out.
- The salary discount is real and compounds: 3–5 years of below-market pay is a significant financial hit even if equity has upside.
- Learning opportunities at crappy startups are often overstated — maintenance work on a failing product isn't the same as working on hard problems at scale.
- Distinguish between genuinely good early-stage bets and "crappy startup jobs" — the latter are common and should be avoided.
- Career capital (reputation, skills, network) matters more than any single equity lottery ticket.

## Summary

Esquire's guide to compliments addressed something that sounds trivial but involves real social cognition: why do some compliments feel good while others feel hollow or even uncomfortable? The answer maps to the difference between generic and specific, transactional and disinterested.

A compliment works when the recipient believes you genuinely noticed something. You're so smart is easy to say to anyone and lands weakly — it's too general to feel earned. "The way you explained that to the client was exactly what they needed to hear" requires observation of a specific event and judgment about what mattered. The specificity is evidence of attention, and attention is what makes the compliment feel real.

The research angle: Carol Dweck's work on fixed mindset vs. growth mindset (the Effort vs. Ability praise studies) found that praising children for being smart (fixed-trait praise) produced worse outcomes than praising them for working hard (process praise). Fixed-trait compliments frame ability as stable and unteachable; process compliments frame it as developed through effort. This translates to adult interpersonal dynamics too.

Timing matters: a compliment given immediately after an action connects to the behavior; one given later (or as a lead-in to a request) reads as transactional. And unsolicited positive feedback is read differently than feedback given in response to a question — the former signals voluntary noticing.

## Key points

- Effective compliments are specific and observed — I noticed X — not generic traits.
- Specificity signals genuine attention, which is the underlying value being communicated.
- Carol Dweck's praise research: process praise (you worked hard) outperforms trait praise (you're smart) in learning outcomes.
- Timing: immediate compliments feel more genuine; delayed or request-prefacing compliments read as transactional.
- Non-transactional: compliments that expect nothing in return are received more positively than those preceding a favor.

## Summary

The San Francisco Planning Department's "Excellent Street Views" document outlines the design principles that produce high-quality pedestrian street experiences in San Francisco. The guide covers building setbacks, facade continuity, street-level retail activation, and the visual corridor principles that distinguish human-scale streets from car-oriented arterials.

The document reflects SF Planning's design review priorities: buildings should have active ground-floor uses (retail, cafes, storefronts) rather than blank walls or parking structures facing the street. Facade continuity — consistent building alignment at the street edge, minimal gaps — creates the enclosed feeling that makes streets feel like rooms rather than wide open spaces. Visual corridors are protected views down streets toward landmarks, hills, or water.

These principles connect to Jane Jacobs' arguments in *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* about what makes streets safe and vital — eyes on the street, mixed uses, short blocks, and buildings that engage pedestrians rather than turning inward. SF's planning code operationalized many of these principles into formal design standards.

## Key points

- Street-level activation: active retail and entrances at grade create pedestrian interest and safety
- Facade continuity: buildings aligned to the street edge create an enclosed "room" effect
- Visual corridors: protected view lines down streets are a formal SF planning consideration
- Building setbacks: surface parking and setback buildings break street continuity — discouraged in pedestrian corridors
- Connects to Jane Jacobs' principles: mixed use, short blocks, active edges, human-scale massing
- SF's unique topography (hills, bay views) makes visual corridor protection particularly impactful

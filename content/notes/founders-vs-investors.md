## Summary

Rajiv Prab's essay performs a simple inversion: take the practices founders routinely apply to employees — at-will firing, performance improvement plans, equity cliffs, non-competes, unilateral compensation changes — and imagine applying them to investors. The thought experiment reveals an enormous asymmetry. Investors who underperform (give bad advice, miss their pro-rata, fail to make introductions they promised) face no accountability mechanisms. Employees who underperform get managed out. The power dynamic runs entirely in one direction.

The essay isn't arguing that investors should face PIPs. The point is that the startup power dynamic is built on a fiction of partnership: investors frame their relationship with founders in the language of collaboration and mutual respect, while structuring the legal and social terms to heavily favor capital. The elaborate performance rituals of founder-investor relationships — board meetings, investor updates, access to the cap table — are obligations that flow in only one direction.

This connects to a broader critique of venture capital culture around 2020-2022: the power balance had swung so far toward founders (ZIRP era, abundant capital, FOMO-driven deals) that many of these asymmetries were being acknowledged or reversed. The YC shift toward standardized SAFEs, the rise of rolling funds, and the general founder-friendly era reflected real changes in leverage. Prab's essay is most useful as a conceptual lens for founders thinking about what they've actually agreed to when they take institutional capital.

## Key points

- The inversion: apply founder-to-employee accountability mechanisms (fire for performance, PIPs, equity cliffs) to investors — the asymmetry becomes clear.
- Investors face no accountability for poor advice, missed commitments, or bad introductions they promised.
- Power flows with capital: legal and social structures favor investors despite partnership framing.
- 2022 context: written at the end of an era of founder leverage; the 2022-2023 correction reversed much of this.
- Useful mental model: when evaluating investor relationships, ask what obligations are actually mutual vs. one-sided.
- Related: liquidation preferences, board control, information rights — the mechanisms behind the asymmetry.

## Summary

Public Knowledge, the Washington, D.C. digital rights organization, published this white paper in 2012 to get ahead of what they correctly predicted would be a legislative battle over 3D printing. The title is a deliberate echo of the debates over peer-to-peer file sharing that led to the DMCA — the argument being that the right lesson from that period is to not impose liability and DRM requirements on a transformative technology before it has matured.

The paper's legal analysis covers three areas. Copyright law applies to artistic 3D objects but not to functional items, which means most physical goods — screws, machine parts, brackets — are not protected by copyright even if someone printed them from a digital file. Patent law protects functional innovations but has a 20-year term and doesn't cover personal use in most jurisdictions. Trademark law protects brand marks but not shapes, unless the shape is a recognized trade dress. The combined picture is that most everyday objects are already in the public domain and printable without liability.

The policy concern is that incumbent manufacturers would push for laws analogous to the DMCA's anti-circumvention provisions — requiring DRM in 3D printers or liability for printing files — before the technology's uses were well understood. The paper argues this would be a mistake, citing how DRM in early digital media restricted legitimate uses while failing to stop piracy.

## Key points

- Most everyday objects are not protected by intellectual property law and can be legally printed at home — the IP issues mainly arise for artistic objects, branded items, and patented innovations
- 3D printing follows the historical pattern of disruptive reproduction technology (printing press, photocopier, VCR, internet): initial panic from incumbents, followed by accommodation
- The DMCA precedent is the warning: premature anti-circumvention law for digital media created lasting harm to legitimate uses
- Open-source hardware and files shared on platforms like Thingiverse exist in a different legal space than commercial product copying
- Maker culture was the context: 2012-2013 was when consumer 3D printers like the MakerBot were reaching the public and creating mainstream coverage

## Summary

Facebook Engineering published this note in early 2012, when Facebook was the most prominent example of high-velocity software development in the world. Facebook was preparing for its IPO and had built a reputation for hiring exceptional engineers and running them at scale with significant autonomy. Advice from Facebook Engineering in 2012 carried real credibility — this was the team that had built infrastructure handling hundreds of millions of users and iterated at a pace most companies couldn't match.

The three tips (based on the post's context) likely touched on themes common in Facebook's internal engineering culture of the era: taking ownership of entire systems end-to-end rather than working in narrow silos, prioritizing the ability to build complete features rather than specializing narrowly, and seeking feedback aggressively through code review and peer interaction. Facebook's "move fast and break things" culture wasn't just speed — it was speed enabled by engineers who understood entire systems well enough to make decisions quickly without constant escalation.

The broader context in 2012: software engineering was professionalizing rapidly, and the question of how to actually improve as a programmer — vs. how to get a job — was becoming more discussed. Hacker News was full of debates about whether university CS prepared people well. Posts like this from practitioners at high-output teams offered an alternative to academic theory: here's what actually correlates with success when you're shipping real products at scale.

## Key points

- Written by Facebook Engineering at the height of Facebook's engineering reputation (pre-IPO 2012).
- Facebook's culture emphasized end-to-end ownership — engineers understood systems they didn't personally write.
- Code review culture at Facebook was a key feedback mechanism for SKILL development.
- Move fast worked because engineers were broadly skilled enough to make good local decisions without constant coordination.
- Connects to the T-shaped skills idea: deep in one area, but broad enough to understand adjacent systems.
- Part of the 2012 wave of engineering culture writing that established norms for what good engineering team meant.

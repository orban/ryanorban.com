## Summary

In April 2012, a study by Tarek Baati and colleagues published in the journal *Biomaterials* reported something extraordinary: rats given C60 fullerene (a.k.a. buckminsterfullerene, or buckyballs) dissolved in olive oil lived nearly twice as long as controls. The treated rats lived ~66% longer on average, with the olive oil control group also living somewhat longer than untreated controls — suggesting the olive oil alone had some effect, but C60 dramatically amplified it.

C60 fullerene is a spherical molecule of 60 carbon atoms, discovered in 1985 (Nobel Prize in 1996). Its biological mechanism wasn't fully understood, but the hypothesis centered on its antioxidant properties — the molecule can absorb free radicals efficiently, potentially reducing oxidative stress, which is a major driver of aging according to the free radical theory of aging. The study wasn't designed as a longevity experiment; it was initially testing C60's toxicity profile, making the lifespan extension finding incidental and more surprising.

The biohacking and longevity research communities responded with unusual intensity. The result was remarkable enough — and the molecule cheap enough to obtain — that many individuals began self-experimenting with C60 in olive oil before replications or human trials were done. The study was treated cautiously by mainstream geroscience researchers, who noted it was a single small-sample rat study with no proposed mechanism robustly validated.

## Key points

- Baati et al. 2012 found ~66% lifespan extension in rats given C60 in olive oil — one of the largest single-intervention lifespan effects ever reported in mammals.
- C60 fullerene is believed to act as a free radical scavenger — reducing oxidative damage that accumulates with age.
- The study was not designed to test longevity; it was a toxicology study, making the finding incidental.
- Olive oil control group also showed some lifespan benefit, complicating interpretation.
- No replication in other mammalian species and no human trials — the leap to human supplementation that the biohacking community made was premature.
- Connects to broader interest in caloric restriction, rapamycin, and other longevity interventions that were active research areas in 2012.

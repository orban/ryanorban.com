## Summary

An explainer on the power source of the Mars Curiosity rover: a Multi-Mission Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator (MMRTG), a type of radioisotope thermoelectric generator (RTG) that converts heat from the natural radioactive decay of plutonium-238 into electricity. The Curiosity MMRTG produces about 110 watts of electricity at the start of the mission, declining gradually as the Pu-238 decays (with a half-life of ~87 years).

RTGs work through the Seebeck effect: when two dissimilar semiconductors are joined and one end is heated by the radioactive decay while the other radiates heat to space, a voltage is generated. There are no moving parts — no turbines, no combustion, just thermocouples and steady heat from radioactive decay. This simplicity is a major reliability advantage for space missions lasting years or decades.

The choice of nuclear power over solar panels was deliberate. Mars receives only about 43% of the solar energy that Earth does, and Martian dust storms can reduce sunlight to near zero for weeks at a time. The Spirit rover (solar-powered) lost power during dust storms repeatedly. The MMRTG sidesteps this entirely — it works continuously regardless of weather or sun angle, and it also produces 2000 watts of heat that can be used to keep electronics warm during the Martian night (temperatures can drop to -70°C). This thermal management benefit alone justifies the nuclear choice for long-duration surface missions.

## Key points

- MMRTG converts heat from Pu-238 radioactive decay into electricity via the Seebeck effect — no moving parts.
- ~110 watts of electrical output at mission start, declining slowly over time (Pu-238 half-life ~87 years).
- Works through Martian dust storms where solar panels fail — key advantage over the solar-powered Spirit rover.
- Produces 2000 watts of heat usable for thermal management — keeps electronics alive in Martian night temperatures (~-70°C).
- Pu-238 produced by nuclear reactors; supply constraints have limited NASA's ability to build more RTG-powered missions.
- Same RTG technology has powered Voyager 1 and 2 for 40+ years.

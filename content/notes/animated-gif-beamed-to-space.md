## Summary

Lone Signal was a SETI-adjacent project that let the public transmit messages — including animated GIFs — toward Gliese 526, a red dwarf star 17.6 light-years away, using a radio telescope in Jamesburg, California. In June 2013, the first animated GIF was queued for transmission into space, which The Verge covered as a cultural moment.

The project framed itself around the Arecibo message tradition — deliberate attempts to communicate with extraterrestrial intelligence — but democratized the process. Anyone could pay a small fee to have a message or image encoded and transmitted. The animated GIF specifically was chosen because it's a compressed, looping image format that could theoretically convey motion in a small data footprint.

Whether Lone Signal's transmissions constituted meaningful SETI work is debatable — the power output was tiny and the star selection was not based on strong habitability evidence. But as a cultural artifact it captured the internet's instinct to colonize any new medium with animated GIFs, extended to cosmic communication.

## Key points

- Lone Signal used a 30-meter dish in Jamesburg, CA to transmit toward Gliese 526 (17.6 light-years)
- First message transmission: June 2013; animated GIF as a novel format choice
- Crowdfunded/crowdsourced: anyone could pay to have a message encoded and sent
- GIF format invented by Steve Wilhite at CompuServe in 1987; became the dominant short animation format online
- The project shut down in 2013 after failing to raise enough funding to sustain telescope time
- Signal travel time: ~17.6 years one-way — a reply would arrive around 2048 if anyone answered

## Summary

Jehiah Czebotar (an engineer at Twitter at the time) built a detailed data visualization of a full year of personally tracked data and published it as Jehiah 12. The tweet that prompted this save called it "really cool data visualization and data collections" — the save was CC'd to @ryanorban, @chrishmatthieu, and @reillyusa, suggesting it was circulating in the data visualization community.

The quantified self movement was gaining momentum in 2012-2013: fitness trackers (Fitbit launched its first wearable in 2011), smartphone location data, and manual logging apps were making personal data collection practical. The interesting question wasn't collecting data — it was what to do with it. Annual reports like Jehiah's represented one approach: retrospective sense-making through visualization, treating your own life as a dataset.

The format echoes Nicholas Felton's "Feltron Annual Reports" (the gold standard of personal data visualization, published yearly since 2005), which presented a year of life as a polished infographic using meticulous self-tracking. Jehiah's version was more technically transparent — published on the web with interactive elements — reflecting an engineer's rather than a designer's approach to the same problem.

## Key points

- Personal data visualization: applying data analysis and visualization to self-tracked personal data — location, activity, sleep, communications
- Nicholas Felton / Feltron Annual Report: the canonical example of personal data visualization as an art form; heavily influential on this genre
- Quantified self movement: 2012-2013 was a growth period driven by affordable wearables and smartphone sensors
- Engineering approach: Jehiah's version was web-native and technically transparent, vs. Felton's print-first polished design
- The annual report format as retrospective practice — using data to understand patterns in your own behavior over a year

## Summary

Alex Smola's 10-701 course at Carnegie Mellon University was one of the standard graduate-level machine learning survey courses in 2013. Smola was (and is) a leading researcher in kernel methods, support vector machines, and distributed optimization — his academic work had been cited extensively in the ML research literature, and his co-authored textbook on kernel methods (with Bernhard Schölkopf) was a foundational reference for the field.

The CMU 10-701 course covered the standard graduate ML curriculum: supervised learning (regression, classification, decision trees, SVMs), unsupervised learning (clustering, dimensionality reduction, PCA), probabilistic graphical models (Bayesian networks, Markov random fields), reinforcement learning, and learning theory (VC dimension, PAC learning, generalization bounds). The level was a notch above the popular Andrew Ng Stanford course — more mathematically rigorous, more focused on connecting methods to their theoretical foundations.

In 2013, high-quality ML course materials being freely available online was still relatively novel. Andrew Ng's Coursera ML course (2011) had democratized introductory ML; Smola's CMU materials were a step deeper, targeting students who already had linear algebra and probability and wanted to understand *why* methods worked, not just how to run them. The course materials represented the self-study path for researchers and practitioners who couldn't attend CMU but wanted graduate-level rigor.

## Key points

- Alex Smola: major ML researcher — co-inventor of support vector machines (with Vapnik/Schölkopf), leading researcher in kernel methods and distributed optimization.
- CMU 10-701: graduate-level ML survey covering supervised learning, unsupervised learning, graphical models, RL, and learning theory — more rigorous than intro courses.
- 2013 open courseware moment: MIT, Stanford, CMU making graduate-level materials publicly available — the precursor to the MOOC boom.
- Connects to learning theory: unlike applied courses, 10-701 explained generalization bounds, VC dimension, and PAC learning — the mathematical foundations.
- Bernhard Schölkopf collaboration: Smola and Schölkopf's kernel methods textbook was the 2002 bible of the SVM era.

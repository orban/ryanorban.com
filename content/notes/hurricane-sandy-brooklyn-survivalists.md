## Summary

Hurricane Sandy made landfall on October 29, 2012, causing catastrophic flooding and power outages across New York City and the surrounding region. By early November, parts of Brooklyn were still without electricity. This Gothamist story captured residents improvising: using camp stoves (and their thermoelectric generators) to charge mobile phones while National Guard vehicles rolled past.

The image is a precise document of 2012's digital dependency. Mobile phones had become the primary communication, information, and navigation tool — but their utility collapsed without power. The camp stove charging hack is a folk solution to a problem that off-grid and preparedness communities had long addressed (solar chargers, hand-crank generators, battery banks) but that most urban residents encountered for the first time during Sandy.

Hurricane Sandy became a significant reference point for urban infrastructure resilience planning. It exposed vulnerabilities in:  power grid restoration timelines (weeks in some areas), cellular network backup power (tower batteries depleted quickly), public transit flooding (MTA took years to fully recover), and fuel distribution (gas station queues stretched for miles when station pumps had no power). The urban survivalist framing — self-sufficient city residents bridging institutional gaps — became a visible post-Sandy archetype.

## Key points

- Hurricane Sandy (October 29, 2012): major infrastructure failure in NYC — weeks of power outages in some areas
- Mobile phone dependency exposed: communication infrastructure failed with grid power
- Camp stove thermoelectric charging: a folk solution from off-grid culture applied to urban emergency
- National Guard presence while residents improvised: the gap between institutional response and personal resilience
- Sandy accelerated resilience planning for urban infrastructure: cellular backup power, microgrid investment, distributed energy

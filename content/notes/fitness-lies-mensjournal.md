## Summary

Men's Journal published this contrarian fitness piece in 2012 (bookmarked in 2013), part of a broader evidence-based fitness movement that was pushing back against received gym wisdom. The framing — everything you know is a lie — is a genre staple, but the underlying research cited was often genuine.

Common targets of this kind of debunking in the early 2010s: static stretching before exercise (shown to reduce power output for the subsequent workout — dynamic warm-up is better), long steady-state cardio as the best fat-loss tool (replaced by HIIT evidence), and high-rep low-weight toning for women (musculature doesn't work differently by sex). The article likely covered several of these.

The quantified self movement and the rise of fitness tracking (early Fitbit, early RunKeeper) were creating a culture of measurement that intersected with evidence-based fitness — if you were tracking everything, you became more receptive to questioning assumptions about what the data showed. This bookmark alongside the Moves app entry from the same month suggests active interest in this space.

## Key points

- Static stretching before exercise reduces subsequent power output — evidence from 2010-2013 shifted recommendations toward dynamic warm-up protocols
- HIIT (High-Intensity Interval Training) produces comparable or better fat loss in less time than long steady-state cardio — the research that disrupted the fat-burning zone dogma
- The toning myth: low-weight high-rep training doesn't produce a different muscle texture — it either builds or reduces muscle; the toned appearance comes from low body fat over developed muscle
- Evidence-based fitness emerged as a genre (websites like Examine.com, Alan Aragon Research Review) that applied literature review rigor to supplement and training claims
- Early 2010s fitness culture was being disrupted by data — Fitbit (2009), RunKeeper (2008), Nike+ (2006) had normalized personal performance tracking

## Summary

Vivek Haldar's short essay coins a useful phrase: [productivity porn](/notes/productivity-porn/) — the consumption of articles, apps, systems, and tips about how to be productive, used as a substitute for actually being productive. The tagline he quotes captures it: "Do not confuse activity for progress."

The trap is that [productivity porn](/notes/productivity-porn/) feels virtuous. Reading about GTD, trying a new task management system, tuning your inbox zero workflow, watching talks on deep work — all of this is nominally about improving your performance. It generates a sense of forward motion without the cost of doing the actual thing. The optimization is real, but it's optimization of a system that isn't running.

What makes this pattern persistent is that it's reinforced by the tools themselves. Productivity apps compete on features and polish; each new system offers a plausible reason to spend a week migrating your entire task list. The meta-work of managing the system becomes its own domain of engagement, decoupled from the underlying work it was supposed to support. Hacker News and similar communities have a chronic version of this: endless discussion of workflows, editors, and methodologies from people who write about code more than they write code.

## Key points

- [Productivity porn](/notes/productivity-porn/) is distinguishable from useful productivity learning by the ratio of consumption to output: if reading about productivity never leads to doing more actual work, it's entertainment.
- The seductive quality of meta-productivity is the sense of control and progress it provides — you're working on the system, which sounds responsible.
- New tools are the most common trigger: switching note-taking apps, task managers, or coding environments creates weeks of meta-work that feels like investment but is often just novelty seeking.
- The corrective is simple to state and hard to practice: close the browser, open the editor, start the first sentence — the work itself is the only cure.
- Connects to the broader idea of Resistance (Steven Pressfield): anything that keeps you from starting the work is Resistance, including virtuous-seeming preparation.

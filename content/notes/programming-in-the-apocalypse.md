## Summary

Mat Duggan's essay [Programming in the Apocalypse](/notes/programming-in-the-apocalypse/) was written in May 2022 at the hinge point when the tech industry's decade-long boom was visibly ending. The piece addresses what software engineering looks like in a more constrained environment: slower hiring, tighter operating budgets, less tolerance for rewrites, and a shift in what employers actually value from developers.

The "apocalypse" framing is deliberately provocative — not literal collapse, but the end of the conditions that made early-to-mid 2020s tech feel like an endless expansion. In that expansion, developers could afford to be opinionated about tech stacks, push for greenfield rewrites of legacy systems, and expect steady job-hopping to be a reliable path to compensation growth. The tightening changes the calculus: employers now want reliability, domain knowledge, and cost-consciousness over cutting-edge stack fluency.

The practical advice shifts accordingly: get comfortable with legacy systems and maintenance work (the majority of actual software engineering, often underpaid and undervalued during boom times), develop skills in cost-reduction and efficiency (not just feature delivery), and recognize that the job market for generalist developers with shallow depth is softening while the market for specialists who deeply understand specific problem domains stays strong.

## Key points

- Tech industry contraction changes what's valued: maintenance, reliability, cost reduction — not just new features
- Legacy system competence becomes more valuable relative to greenfield stack fluency when budgets tighten
- Compensation growth via job-hopping slows in down markets; depth and domain expertise matter more
- The boom conditions (endless VC-funded hiring, tolerance for technical debt, frequent rewrites) were historically anomalous
- Developers who only experienced the boom need to recalibrate expectations and SKILL emphasis

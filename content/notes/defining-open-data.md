## Summary

The Open Knowledge Foundation's definition of open data established the criteria that make data truly open: freely usable, redistribution-permitted, no restriction on use or combination with other datasets, and no discrimination against persons, groups, or fields of endeavor. This framework was important in 2013 as governments, research institutions, and companies were beginning to publish data online — but with wildly varying license terms.

The distinction between available online and open data mattered enormously for what practitioners could do with it. Data published under a restrictive license (non-commercial only, no derivatives, no redistribution) couldn't be combined, cleaned and re-shared, or used in commercial products — even if you could technically download it. The Open Knowledge Foundation's Open Definition provided a standard against which to evaluate data licenses.

The open government data movement was gaining momentum in 2013: data.gov in the US had launched in 2009, the UK had opened data.gov.uk, and cities were beginning to publish datasets through portals. For data scientists, this represented a growing supply of real-world datasets to work with — important both for learning and for building applications.

## Key points

- Open data requires: freely usable, redistributable, no use restrictions, no discrimination — "available online" is not enough.
- Open Knowledge Foundation's Open Definition provided a standard license framework analogous to Open Source Initiative's criteria for open-source software.
- Open government data movement in 2013: data.gov, data.gov.uk, and city portals were opening significant datasets.
- For data scientists, open data expanded the supply of real-world datasets available for learning, research, and product development.
- License ambiguity was common: data published online without explicit license terms is not legally open — an important practical concern.

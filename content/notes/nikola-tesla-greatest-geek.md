## Summary

Matthew Inman's The Oatmeal comic on Nikola Tesla was one of the most widely shared pieces of science history content of 2012. It repackaged Tesla as a wronged visionary: the inventor of AC power, radio, and foundational work in wireless transmission, who was systematically undercut by Thomas Edison and died in poverty despite his ideas underpinning the modern electrical grid.

The comic is selective history — it presents a heroic Tesla against a villainous Edison, glossing over Tesla's own eccentricities and business failures, and oversimplifies the War of Currents. But it succeeded at its actual goal: making Tesla culturally legible and sympathetic to a generation that had mostly learned about Edison in school. The Oatmeal's version of Tesla emphasized traits that resonated with hacker culture: he was the brilliant outsider who was technically right but commercially screwed by a powerful, well-connected incumbent.

The comic led to a fundraising drive to save Wardenclyffe Tower (Tesla's unfinished wireless transmission facility on Long Island, slated for demolition) — The Oatmeal raised over $1.3 million via crowdfunding, which eventually became the Tesla Science Center at Wardenclyffe. It also landed in the middle of controversy about Tesla Motors' naming rights — The Oatmeal had previously defended Tesla Motors against a historian arguing the name was being used inappropriately. The cultural weight of "Tesla" as brand has only increased since.

## Key points

- Nikola Tesla invented AC induction motor, won the War of Currents, held ~300 patents including foundational radio work.
- Edison vs. Tesla framing: DC (Edison) vs. AC (Tesla) — AC won because it transmits efficiently over distance.
- The Oatmeal fundraiser → $1.37M raised → Tesla Science Center at Wardenclyffe preserved from demolition.
- Tesla died in 1943 in a New York hotel room, broke; Westinghouse had bought out his royalties to save the company.
- The wronged genius narrative was partly constructed — Tesla had real business failures and personal eccentricities that contributed to his fate.

## Summary

This Slate Moneybox piece from April 2013 made the supply-side argument for San Francisco housing — that the city's popularity is precisely why it should build more, not less. The core economics: San Francisco is geographically constrained (peninsula, hills, bay) and politically resistant to density. Combined with strong demand from tech employment growth, this supply constraint is the mechanism translating wage increases for tech workers into housing cost increases for everyone.

The argument was contrarian in 2013 because the dominant local politics were NIMBYist — neighborhood groups opposed new development as incompatible with existing character, shadow impacts, parking shortfalls, or gentrification (which the piece implicitly distinguishes from supply-constrained price increases). The piece articulated what would become the YIMBY (Yes In My Backyard) position: upzoning, height limit increases, and streamlined permitting are necessary conditions for housing affordability at city scale.

The implicit comparison is to cities that did allow density — Manhattan, Chicago's Loop — where tall buildings concentrated demand in a small geographic area and left the rest of the city more affordable by relative standards. San Francisco's mid-rise, low-density zoning instead spread the housing shortage across the entire city. The make more of it line in the bookmark's content captures the argument precisely: San Francisco is worth living in, so the supply of it should expand, not be preserved as a finite artifact.

## Key points

- Zoning restrictions as root cause: height limits, minimum setbacks, single-family zoning, and slow permitting are supply constraints that explain SF's affordability crisis.
- Housing supply argument: adding density absorbs demand without necessarily raising prices — the opposite of the folk theory that "luxury housing drives up rents."
- YIMBY movement precursor: this 2013 framing anticipated the Yes In My Backyard movement that organized in SF around 2015–2016.
- Geographic constraint: SF's peninsula location means horizontal expansion is impossible — the only direction is up, or sprawl to the exurbs.
- Tech sector employment as demand shock: the localization of tech jobs in SF and the Peninsula created income-driven demand that outpaced housing supply at any price point.

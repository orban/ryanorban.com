## Summary

Alberto Savoia (formerly of Google) introduces pretotyping as a method for validating product-market fit at a fraction of the cost and time of traditional prototyping. The central insight is that most new products fail not because they're built badly but because they're the wrong thing entirely — there aren't enough people who want them. Traditional prototypes answer Can we build it? and Will it work?, but the more important questions — Should we build it? and Will people actually use it? — require different validation methods.

A pretotype is the minimal, often fake, simulation of a user experience that lets you collect real behavioral data about demand before committing to full development. Examples Savoia gives include "The Mechanical Turk" (a human performing automation to simulate an AI service), "The Fake Door" (a landing page for a product that doesn't yet exist, measuring signups), and "The Concierge" (manually delivering a service that will eventually be automated). These methods produce actual usage data rather than opinions or projections.

The book itself was written as a pretotype: Savoia spent days rather than months on this pretotype edition to test whether there was enough demand for a full book on the subject before investing in writing it properly. The lean startup philosophy influences the work heavily — fail fast, MVP, and the primacy of market validation over engineering quality. The book argues that the biggest obstacle to entrepreneurship is not execution risk but the compulsion for premature perfectionism and the reluctance to expose rough ideas to real-world judgment.

## Key Points

- Pretotyping validates market demand before prototyping — tests the right it before worrying about building it right
- Most products fail due to lack of demand, not poor execution — pretotypes gather behavioral data to address this
- Key pretotype techniques: Mechanical Turk (fake automation), Fake Door (measure demand before building), Concierge (manual service simulation)
- Produces real usage data vs. opinions; even a failed pretotype is far cheaper than a failed prototype
- The book itself is a meta-pretotype: published rough and early to test whether a full version is warranted before investing months in writing it

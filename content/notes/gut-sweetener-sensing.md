## Summary

Research in neuroscience and nutrition has revealed that the gut-brain axis distinguishes between real sugar and artificial sweeteners through separate sensory pathways — a finding with significant implications for understanding why calorie-free sweeteners don't fully replicate the satisfaction of sugar, and for the broader debate over their metabolic effects.

The key distinction: sugar (glucose, fructose, sucrose) activates both taste receptors in the gut and a separate nociceptive/caloric-sensing pathway that signals energy content to the vagus nerve and brain. This second pathway — independent of sweetness taste — is what communicates calories have arrived. Artificial sweeteners like saccharin, sucralose, and aspartame bind taste receptors and produce the sweet sensation, but don't activate the caloric-sensing pathway because they don't deliver energy.

This two-pathway model helps explain several puzzling observations: why artificial sweeteners don't fully suppress appetite the same way sugar does (the caloric signal is missing), why high-dose saccharin in rodent studies sometimes increases caloric intake (the uncoupling of sweet taste from energy intake may dysregulate appetite signaling), and why gut microbiome effects differ between sugar and zero-calorie alternatives. The research points toward the gut as an active sensory organ, not just a passive absorption system.

## Key points

- Sugar activates two gut pathways: taste receptors (sweetness) + caloric-sensing via the vagus nerve (energy signal).
- Artificial sweeteners only activate taste receptors — the caloric signal is absent, even though the sweet sensation is present.
- This uncoupling may explain incomplete appetite satisfaction and potential compensation effects in sweetener users.
- Gut microbiome responds differently to sugar vs. artificial sweeteners — metabolic effects extend beyond calories.
- The gut as active sensory organ: these findings are part of a broader research direction on the gut-brain axis in appetite regulation.

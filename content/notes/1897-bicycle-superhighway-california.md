## Summary

This Motherboard piece covers the 1897 California Cycleway — a privately financed, elevated wooden cycling highway proposed to run from Pasadena to Los Angeles. The project was developed by Horace Dobbins during the 1890s bicycle boom, when cycling was a mainstream form of urban transportation rather than a recreation or sport. The 9-mile elevated structure was designed to let cyclists avoid the muddy, unpaved roads between cities and commute in reasonable time.

The article uses this history to make a pointed argument about path dependence in urban infrastructure: the car's dominance of American cities was not inevitable. In the 1890s, cyclists had significant political clout — the League of American Wheelmen lobbied successfully for paved roads, and cycling-infrastructure investment was considered serious urban planning. The internal combustion engine was a new entrant competing against an established, well-organized cycling culture. The car won for a combination of reasons (economic power of oil industry, Ford's assembly-line pricing, post-WWII suburban development policy) — not because it was obviously superior for all urban use cases.

The California Cycleway only completed about 1.5 miles before the project ran out of funding, and the broader cycling culture collapsed as cars arrived. This is now an important piece of cycling history invoked by bike infrastructure advocates arguing that dedicated cycling infrastructure at scale (elevated, grade-separated) has been technically feasible for over a century.

## Key points

- The 1890s were a golden era of cycling in the US — bikes were transportation, not recreation; ridership was mainstream across classes
- Horace Dobbins privately financed the Cycleway as a toll road — the business model predated public bike infrastructure by over a century
- The collapse of 1890s cycling culture prefigures the induced demand argument: when car infrastructure is built, cycling infrastructure is neglected, which reduces cycling, which justifies more car infrastructure
- League of American Wheelmen (founded 1880) was the first major cycling advocacy group — their lobbying for good roads ironically helped pave the way for automobiles
- Connected to contemporary debates about protected bike lanes, elevated cycling infrastructure (like the Hovenring in the Netherlands), and bike-first urban planning

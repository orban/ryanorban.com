## Summary

Anders Hoff (online as @inconvergent) creates generative art using mathematical algorithms that simulate natural processes — erosion, plant growth, diffusion-limited aggregation, sand dune formation. The work sits at the intersection of computational art and applied mathematics: the visual outputs are beautiful, but they're also legible as simulations of physical systems.

His approach is heavily influenced by algorithmic botany (L-systems, branching structures) and stochastic processes. Many pieces use Brownian motion or noise functions to introduce controlled randomness that prevents the output from looking mechanical. The tension between the deterministic structure of the algorithm and the unpredictable detail of the output is central to what makes the work compelling — you can see the rule system but can't predict the specific result.

Technically, Hoff's tools are minimal — primarily Python with numpy and custom plotting code, outputting SVG or directly driving plotters. This is a contrast to much of the creative coding scene that relies on Processing or p5.js — his work runs closer to scientific computing than interactive art.

## Key points

- Simulates natural processes (erosion, growth, diffusion) with minimal code and no rendering engine.
- Heavy use of stochastic processes — controlled randomness that produces organic-looking outputs.
- Diffusion-limited aggregation, L-systems, and Brownian motion are recurring techniques.
- Outputs via SVG and pen plotters — physical artifacts from digital processes.
- Contrast with Processing/p5.js scene: closer to scientific computing than interactive art.
- The inconvergent name signals the aesthetic philosophy: systems that approach but never reach convergence.

## Summary

Nick Szabo wrote this 2005 piece (bookmarked in 2012 after resurfacing on social media) about one of the more arcane tactics in pharmaceutical patenting: polymorph patents. The same chemical molecule can crystallize in different structural arrangements (polymorphs), and pharmaceutical companies systematically patent each distinct polymorph to extend their exclusivity window beyond the original compound patent. This is why drug patents last effectively longer in practice than the nominal 20-year term.

The Paxil (paroxetine) case Szabo describes is particularly strange: one of Paxil's crystal forms turned out to be self-replicating in the sense that once a manufacturing facility had made it, trace amounts would contaminate future batches and convert them to the same form. This is crystallographic seeding — a real phenomenon in solid-state chemistry. The analogy to Kurt Vonnegut's ice-nine (a fictional ice polymorph that would spread and freeze all water on contact) gives the post its memorable frame.

The broader point is about patent evergreening — a strategy where companies file continuous streams of patents on minor variations (new polymorphs, new formulations, new dosing schedules) to prevent generic drugs from entering the market even after the core compound patent expires. This is standard practice in Big Pharma and has been the subject of significant litigation and regulatory reform efforts.

## Key points

- Polymorph patents cover different crystal structures of the same molecule — each one is patentable as a distinct compound.
- Patent evergreening uses polymorphs, formulations, and dosing patents to extend effective exclusivity well beyond the base patent.
- Paxil's crystallographic contamination problem made one polymorph effectively dominant — once it appeared, it was impossible to manufacture other forms cleanly.
- The ice-nine metaphor (Vonnegut's Cat's Cradle) is apt: a self-propagating crystal form that eliminates alternative states.
- Nick Szabo was writing this in the context of thinking about property rights, contracts, and smart contracts — the pharmaceutical example illustrates how property rights can be gamed through definitional manipulation.

## Summary

In Spring 2012, Peter Thiel taught CS183 at Stanford University — a course on startups that covered technology, business strategy, and his contrarian framework for thinking about competition and monopoly. Blake Masters, a law student who attended, took exhaustive notes and published polished essays from each class on Tumblr. The notes became widely circulated in the startup community; they were later revised and expanded into the book Zero to One (2014).

Class 5 focused on the mechanics of last mover advantage — Thiel's argument that being first to a market matters less than being the last company standing in a monopoly position. He used examples from the history of technology to argue that great companies aren't just building products; they're building durable monopolies through some combination of network effects, economies of scale, brand, or proprietary technology. The framework challenged the common startup advice to move fast — Thiel argued that speed without durability is just churn.

The broader CS183 curriculum was notable for being openly contrarian: Thiel argued that most business advice is wrong, that competition is destructive rather than healthy, and that the right question for a startup is not how do we compete? but "how do we build something that others can't replicate?" This framing — monopoly as the goal — ran counter to standard economics education but resonated with founders building in winner-take-all markets like social networks and operating systems.

## Key points

- Peter Thiel's core thesis: competition is for losers — the goal is to build a durable monopoly in a market you define.
- Last mover advantage matters more than first mover: being early is less important than being the final dominant player.
- Four sources of monopoly: network effects, economies of scale, brand, proprietary technology — durable companies usually have multiple.
- The CS183 notes by Blake Masters were widely circulated in 2012 and later became Zero to One.
- Thiel taught this at Stanford in spring 2012 — at the peak of startup mania following Facebook's IPO preparation.
- Connects to Thiel's PayPal Mafia background and his pattern of backing companies in winner-take-most markets.

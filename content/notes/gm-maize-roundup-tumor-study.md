## Summary

The Séralini study (led by Gilles-Éric Séralini at the University of Caen) was published in *Food and Chemical Toxicology* in September 2012 and caused an immediate media storm. The paper claimed that rats fed NK603 Roundup-resistant GM maize for their full lifetime developed tumors at significantly higher rates than controls, with dramatic photographs of tumor-bearing rats accompanying the press release.

The study received instant mainstream coverage as evidence that GMOs were carcinogenic. The headline claim was explosive and the images were alarming. What happened next is a textbook case in how science communication and peer review interact badly under media pressure: within days, scientific critics identified serious methodological problems — the Sprague-Dawley rat strain used is known to develop spontaneous tumors at high rates regardless of diet, the sample sizes were too small (10 rats per group) for statistical significance, and the control group data was not clearly presented.

In November 2013, the journal retracted the paper. EFSA, Food Standards Agency, and other regulatory bodies reviewed it and found it provided no credible evidence of harm. Séralini had staged the press release under embargo conditions that prevented pre-publication scientific critique — journalists were required to sign NDAs before receiving the paper, and couldn't consult outside experts until after publication. This manufactured urgency and imagery outpaced the peer review process.

## Key points

- Séralini study (2012): claimed GM maize + Roundup caused tumors in rats — widely covered, later retracted in 2013
- Methodological problems: wrong rat strain (high spontaneous tumor rate), insufficient sample size, selective data reporting
- NDA embargo strategy prevented pre-publication scientific critique — the media cycle ran before experts could respond
- Retraction didn't undo the public impression — availability bias means the initial alarming headlines are more memorable than the correction
- Illustrates the replication crisis dynamic in biomedical research: dramatic results get coverage, methodological failures get corrections nobody reads

## Summary

A Stack Overflow Blog post from February 2022 on the real tradeoffs of moving from software engineering into engineering management. The piece is notable for being honest about what you lose rather than focusing only on what you gain — a counterweight to the career progression narrative that management is simply the next level.

The core losses are: **technical depth** (you stop building the mental models that come from reading code and writing it daily; your skills atrophy faster than you realize), **the maker's schedule** (Paul Graham's concept — managers live in meeting schedules; engineers need long uninterrupted blocks), **direct contribution** (your work is now indirect and harder to attribute — you enabled the outcome but didn't produce it), and **autonomy over your time** (your calendar is owned by your team's needs).

The gains are real too: broader impact, developing people, influencing direction, seeing across the organization. But these are diffuse and long-horizon in ways that the clear feedback loop of shipping code is not. The post likely quotes or references the common observation that many engineers who try management come back to IC roles — not because they failed, but because they discovered the trade wasn't worth it for them personally.

The staff engineer track (individual contributor growth beyond senior engineer) emerged partly as a structural answer to this: a path to broader impact without management's tradeoffs. The post is useful reading for engineers considering the transition and should be read alongside An Elegant Puzzle (Will Larson) and The Manager's Path (Camille Fournier).

## Key points

- Technical skills atrophy in management — even a few months away from coding measurably affects depth.
- Maker's schedule (long uninterrupted blocks) is incompatible with the meeting-heavy manager's schedule.
- Direct contribution vs. indirect leverage: management is higher-leverage but lower-attribution work.
- The staff engineer track is the structural answer for engineers who want broader impact without management's tradeoffs.
- Many engineers who try management return to IC — self-knowledge matters more than the "right" career path.

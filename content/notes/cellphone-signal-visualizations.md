## Summary

Nickolay Lamm produced a series of illustrations showing what cell phone signal fields would look like if they were visible to the human eye — rendered as colorful, psychedelic clouds around cell towers and in urban environments. The project was widely shared in 2013 because it made invisible infrastructure suddenly vivid.

The visualizations map radio frequency (RF) signal strength and propagation patterns around cell towers as color fields: stronger signal in warmer colors, weaker in cooler. The patterns show how signals bounce off buildings, diffract around obstacles, and interfere with each other — the actual physics of RF propagation rendered as an art piece.

The work sits in the "invisible infrastructure made visible" genre that was gaining cultural traction in the early 2010s: mapping Wi-Fi signals, electromagnetic fields, and data flows as visual landscapes. Projects like Timo Arnall's Immaterials (Wi-Fi visualization) were in the same vein. The appeal is partly aesthetic, partly the revelation that we're constantly immersed in dense electromagnetic fields that our perceptual system simply doesn't register.

## Key points

- Nickolay Lamm visualized RF signal propagation from cell towers as colored fields — warm colors for strong signal, cool for weak.
- Shows RF multipath propagation: signals reflect off buildings, create interference patterns, vary in strength over short distances.
- Genre: "invisible infrastructure visible" — related to Wi-Fi mapping, electromagnetic field visualization projects of the early 2010s.
- Artistically compelling because it renders the invisible electromagnetic environment most city-dwellers are immersed in daily.
- Relevant to understanding why dead zones occur even in apparently well-covered areas — multipath and building reflections.

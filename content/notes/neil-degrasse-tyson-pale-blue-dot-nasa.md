## Summary

This video captures Neil deGrasse Tyson's recurring theme: that the Apollo program's photographs of Earth from space — particularly Earthrise (1968, taken by Bill Anders on Apollo 8) and Carl Sagan's "Pale Blue Dot" image (Voyager 1, 1990) — represent a genuine shift in human self-perception. The insight, as Tyson frames it: "We went to the Moon and discovered Earth."

The perspective shift was real and documented. The environmental movement gained critical mass in the years after Earthrise. Earth Day was founded in 1970, two years after Apollo 8. The Blue Marble photograph from Apollo 17 (1972) became one of the most reproduced photographs in history. Seeing the Earth as a small, fragile object in space — the view from elsewhere — changed politics and culture in ways that rational argument hadn't.

The bookmark included the #Penny4NASA hashtag, a 2012 campaign advocating for restoring NASA's budget to 1 cent per tax dollar (roughly double its then-current share). Tyson was the public face of this effort, arguing that space exploration's downstream benefits — technologies, economic growth, inspiration of STEM talent — far exceeded the investment. The campaign connected to broader debates about science funding during an era of budget austerity.

## Key points

- Earthrise (1968, Apollo 8) and Pale Blue Dot (1990, Voyager 1) as genuine paradigm shifts in human self-perception.
- "We went to the Moon and discovered Earth" — Tyson's formulation of the overview effect.
- Environmental movement linkage: Earth Day (1970) followed directly from the perspective shift.
- #Penny4NASA: 2012 campaign advocating NASA budget = 1¢ per tax dollar (~0.5% of federal budget); Neil deGrasse Tyson as advocate.
- The overview effect — astronauts' reported cognitive shift from seeing Earth from space — has been studied and documented independently.

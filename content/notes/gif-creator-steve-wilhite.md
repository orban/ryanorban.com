## Summary

Steve Wilhite, the CompuServe engineer who invented the GIF (Graphics Interchange Format) format in 1987, received a Webby Award Lifetime Achievement honor in 2013. He used his five-word acceptance speech — a Webby tradition — to declare definitively: It's pronounced JIF, not GIF. This triggered a predictable internet argument.

The GIF format was designed for CompuServe's online service as a compact, lossless image format suitable for the dial-up bandwidth of 1987. The LZW compression algorithm it used was efficient for graphics with limited color palettes. The support for multiple frames was a later addition that enabled the animated GIF — a capability that sat mostly dormant until the early web era, then exploded in the mid-2010s.

The GIF's longevity is somewhat accidental. Better formats (PNG for static images, WebM/MP4 for animations) are objectively superior in every technical dimension. GIF persists because of social inertia, ecosystem support, and the particular aesthetic quality of its lossy-compression artifacts and limited palette. It's also famously inefficient — a 1-second animation encoded as GIF is typically 5-10x larger than the same clip as MP4.

## Key points

- GIF invented by Steve Wilhite at CompuServe in 1987 as a compact color image format for dial-up transmission
- Uses LZW compression — efficient for limited-color graphics, poorly suited for photographs
- Multiple-frame support was the accidental root of the animated GIF phenomenon
- Wilhite's pronunciation ruling: JIF — ignored by the majority of internet users
- PNG replaced static GIF for lossless graphics in the late 1990s; MP4/WebM are technically superior for animations
- GIF persists through social momentum and ecosystem ubiquity, not technical merit

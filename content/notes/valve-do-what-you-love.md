## Summary

Michael Abrash — the graphics programming legend who wrote Zen of Code Optimization and Graphics Programming Black Book, coauthored Quake with John Carmack, and spent time at Microsoft and Intel — wrote this post shortly after joining Valve. It's a meditation on what do what you love actually means in practice.

Abrash's argument is nuanced: the naive version of "do what you love" — find your passion and follow it — is often bad advice. Most people don't have a strong passion that maps cleanly to a job. What actually produces satisfaction and mastery is the *reverse* process: getting very good at something tends to produce the motivation and love for it. Cal Newport would later develop this argument at length in *So Good They Can't Ignore You* (2012, the same year). The question isn't what do I love? but "what am I willing to do the work to get good at?"

Abrash's own career illustrated this: he didn't start as a passionate graphics programmer — he became one by doing the work, solving hard problems, and getting deep enough that the problems themselves became intrinsically interesting. His move to Valve (and later to Oculus) followed the same logic: the work was interesting and the people were excellent.

The post also touched on Valve's unusual flat organizational structure (detailed in the Valve Employee Handbook), which was getting significant attention in 2012 as an unusual experiment in non-hierarchical software company management.

## Key points

- Michael Abrash (graphics programming legend, co-created Quake's engine) joined Valve from Intel in 2011.
- Thesis: love follows mastery, not the reverse — "do what you love" should be "get so good they can't ignore you."
- Pre-dates but aligns with Cal Newport's *So Good They Can't Ignore You* (2012) on career capital theory.
- Valve's flat organization: no managers, employees choose projects, move desks freely — radical for a software company.
- Abrash later became chief scientist at Oculus VR — followed the interesting work into virtual reality.

## Summary

Burrito Justice — a San Francisco neighborhood history blog — investigated the mysterious brick rings embedded in asphalt at various city intersections. The content field notes this was retweeted by Philip Bump with the summary: "Why some intersections in San Francisco have brick rings embedded in them."

The circles are remnants of San Francisco's extensive streetcar network. When streetcar lines were pulled up and paved over, the circular brickwork around the former streetcar poles or junction points was left in place — preserved beneath the asphalt until repaving exposed them. San Francisco had one of the most extensive streetcar networks in the US before the great mid-century conversion to buses and automobiles.

This is a characteristic Burrito Justice piece: urban archaeology focused on San Francisco's built environment, recovering the hidden history visible in street-level details most residents walk past without noticing. The same intersection might have streetcar brick circles, earthquake rubble fill, and mid-century highway-era drainage infrastructure all layered beneath it.

## Key points

- San Francisco brick rings are artifacts of the city's former streetcar infrastructure — evidence of the pre-automobile transit network
- The city had one of the densest streetcar networks in the US before the 1940s-50s conversion to buses
- Urban archaeology of SF streets reveals multiple historical layers: streetcar era, 1906 earthquake rebuilding, and highway-era infrastructure
- Burrito Justice is a notable example of neighborhood-scale hyperlocal urban history blogging in San Francisco
- The broader context: US cities systematically dismantled streetcar networks (partially through General Motors Streetcar Conspiracy-era decisions) — these brick remnants are physical evidence of that transformation

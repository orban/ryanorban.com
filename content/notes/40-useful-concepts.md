## Summary

Gurwinder's Substack essay collects 40 named mental models and cognitive concepts with brief explanations of each, curated for practical usefulness in everyday thinking and decision-making. The selection spans psychology, philosophy, economics, and epistemology — concepts like Chesterton's Fence (don't remove a fence until you understand why it was built), Goodhart's Law (when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure), Poe's Law (extremist parody becomes indistinguishable from sincere belief), and Fundamental Attribution Error (attributing others' behavior to character, our own to circumstance).

The value of named mental models is compression: giving a concept a memorable name makes it faster to retrieve and apply. When you see a system that optimizes for a proxy metric at the expense of the actual goal, having "Goodhart's Law" available means you can think and communicate about it faster than explaining the pattern from scratch. Gurwinder's list skews toward concepts useful for understanding human behavior, institutional dynamics, and epistemological failures — which makes it particularly relevant for product thinking, organizational design, and media criticism.

The mental models genre has been popularized by Farnam Street (Shane Parrish's blog), Charlie Munger's advocacy for a latticework of mental models, and various rationalist community resources. Gurwinder's list is less exhaustive than some but more curated — weighted toward concepts that are genuinely surprising or counter-intuitive rather than just categorizing obvious things. A good companion to Farnam Street's mental model library for a different editorial angle.

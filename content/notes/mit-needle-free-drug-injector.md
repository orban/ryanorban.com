## Summary

Researchers in Ian Hunter's lab at MIT developed a needle-free injection device using a Lorentz-force actuator — the same electromagnetic principle that makes speakers move. A precisely controlled electromagnetic coil propels a tiny piston at high velocity, forcing a microscopic jet of liquid through the skin at ~100 m/s. The jet diameter is about the width of a mosquito's proboscis; recipients report feeling no pain.

The device is programmable in a meaningful sense: the same actuator can adjust the jet velocity and volume profile during the injection. This allows it to first deliver a fast burst to penetrate the skin's stratum corneum, then slow to a gentler flow for drug deposition — mimicking the two-phase behavior a skilled injector produces with a needle. You can also vary delivery depth.

The implications are practical. Roughly 10% of people have needle phobia severe enough to affect their healthcare decisions — avoiding flu shots, blood draws, or insulin injections. Medical facilities generate billions of used needles annually, creating sharps disposal costs and infection risks. A needle-free device addresses both. The NPR coverage connected this to the "#StarTrekFuture" framing — medical tricorders and non-invasive diagnostics had been a cultural touchstone for what advanced medicine should look like.

## Key points

- Lorentz-force actuator: electromagnetic coil drives a piston that fires liquid as a ~100 m/s jet through skin.
- No pain: jet diameter ~mosquito proboscis width; velocity calibrated to penetrate skin without nerve stimulation.
- Programmable pressure profile: fast burst to penetrate stratum corneum, gentle flow for deposition.
- Addresses needle phobia (~10% population), sharps disposal infrastructure, and cross-contamination risks.
- Ian Hunter lab (MIT): same group works on high-precision linear actuators for surgical robotics.

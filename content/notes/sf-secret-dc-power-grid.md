## Summary

IEEE Spectrum profiled San Francisco's largely unknown underground DC power distribution network — a legacy system from the early electrical era that survived into the modern age. While almost all of the United States runs on AC power (as Tesla and Westinghouse established in the War of Currents against Edison's DC systems), San Francisco retained a small DC distribution network serving certain commercial and industrial loads.

The existence of this DC grid is a technical artifact: early urban electrical systems often used direct current because it was simpler to control motor speeds without the need for variable-frequency drives. Elevators, printing presses, and certain industrial equipment were designed for DC. Converting these customers to AC would have required replacing all their equipment, so the DC infrastructure was maintained even as everything else converted.

This is also relevant to modern energy infrastructure discussions: data centers and some renewable energy systems prefer DC internally (servers run on DC, solar panels produce DC), which has sparked interest in DC microgrids as a more efficient alternative to the AC-DC-AC conversion chain.

## Key points

- San Francisco has a functioning underground DC power distribution network — a legacy of pre-AC electrification.
- AC vs DC War of Currents: Tesla/Westinghouse AC won the 1890s battle, but DC survived in pockets where converting equipment would have been impractical.
- DC persistence: elevators, industrial motors, printing equipment designed for DC — cheaper to maintain the grid than replace all customers' equipment.
- Modern relevance: data centers run internally on DC; solar/battery storage produce DC; the case for DC microgrids is being revived.
- City infrastructure often carries 100+ year-old technical decisions that are expensive to change even when the technology has moved on.

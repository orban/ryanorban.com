## Summary

The USGS (United States Geological Survey) Water Science School created an oft-shared visualization showing what Earth's total water supply would look like if condensed into a single sphere sitting next to Earth. The sphere is about 860 miles (1,385 km) in diameter — strikingly small against the full 7,918-mile diameter of Earth. The image makes viscerally clear that water, despite covering 71% of Earth's surface, is a thin veneer rather than a deep supply.

The breakdown is even more striking when you separate by type. Total water (oceans + glaciers + groundwater + lakes + rivers + everything else): the large sphere. All freshwater: a much smaller sphere. All surface freshwater in lakes and rivers — the water humans most directly use: a tiny sphere, barely visible. The visual format communicates something that percentage tables can't: the difference in orders of magnitude between ocean water and directly accessible freshwater.

This became a widely circulated visualization because it corrects an intuition most people have about Earth's water abundance. Water scarcity discussions often feel abstract because of how ubiquitous water appears in daily life. Seeing Earth covered in ocean and thinking we have plenty of water misunderstands the distribution — the oceans are salt water, most freshwater is locked in glaciers and deep groundwater, and what's left that humanity can readily access is a thin, finite resource.

## Key points

- Total water sphere: ~860 miles diameter. Earth diameter: ~7,918 miles. Water is a surface skin, not a deep reserve.
- Freshwater breakdown: ~2.5% of total water is fresh; of that, ~69% is locked in ice caps and glaciers.
- All surface freshwater (lakes + rivers + swamps): ~0.3% of freshwater, or ~0.0075% of total water.
- USGS Water Science School produces educational materials on the global water cycle and groundwater systems.
- Visual format (spheres at scale) communicates magnitude differences that percentage tables obscure.

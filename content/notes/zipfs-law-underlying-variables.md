## Summary

Zipf's Law is one of the most striking empirical regularities in science: across natural language, city populations, firm sizes, website traffic, and dozens of other domains, the frequency of an item is approximately inversely proportional to its rank. The second most common word appears half as often as the first; the third city by population is about a third as large as the largest. The law's universality has puzzled researchers for decades — why does the same pattern appear in such disparate systems?

This PLOS Computational Biology paper by Henry Lin and Max Tegmark proposes a general explanation: Zipf's Law emerges naturally whenever you observe a quantity that is an exponential function of underlying unobserved variables with any well-behaved distribution. The mechanism is elegant — if the observed variable is multiplicative in latent factors (as many real quantities are), the resulting marginal distribution will have a heavy tail with the Zipf signature, even if the latent variables follow a simple Gaussian or exponential distribution. No special generative process is required.

This explanation has significant implications for how we interpret power laws in data. The Zipf signature isn't necessarily evidence of a specific self-organizing mechanism or optimization process — it can be an artifact of aggregating over hidden heterogeneity. This connects to similar debates in network science (are scale-free networks really generated by preferential attachment, or are they measurement artifacts?) and in linguistics (does Zipf's Law in language reflect anything about communication efficiency, or is it just what you'd expect from aggregating word usage across diverse speakers and contexts?).

## Key points

- Zipf's Law (rank-frequency power law) appears across language, cities, income, and many other domains — the paper explains why without invoking special mechanisms.
- Mechanism: if observed variable = exp(sum of latent variables), any well-behaved latent distribution produces a Zipf-like marginal.
- This is a null hypothesis for power laws — before concluding a power law reflects a special generative process, check whether it's just latent variable aggregation.
- Authors: Henry Lin and Max Tegmark (Tegmark is the MIT physicist known for Our Mathematical Universe).
- Published in PLOS Computational Biology (2016) — open access.

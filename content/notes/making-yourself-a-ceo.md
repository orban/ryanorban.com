## Summary

Ben Horowitz wrote this post on his blog (later moved to a16z) to push back on the common framing that great CEOs are born rather than made. His argument: being CEO is learnable, but the learning process is counterintuitively difficult because the normal feedback mechanisms that let people improve their skills break down at the top of an organization. Nobody in the company has strong incentives to tell the CEO when they're wrong — the asymmetric power relationship makes honest feedback rare and unwelcome feedback even rarer.

The core SKILL Horowitz identifies is designing and maintaining channels for getting accurate information. CEOs who don't work at this actively end up making decisions in an information vacuum — hearing what people think they want to hear, missing the real problems until they're critical. Mechanisms include deliberately creating forums where people feel safe delivering bad news, cultivating external advisors who have no stake in flattering you, and forcing yourself to evaluate your own decisions post-hoc against actual outcomes rather than the stated reasoning at the time.

The second theme is the specific adjustment from engineering or functional leadership to CEO. Technical founders and functional leaders are used to being the person with the most context in the room on their domain. The CEO role requires making decisions under radical uncertainty across domains where you're not the expert — and accepting that you'll frequently be wrong in ways you won't understand immediately. The adjustment is learning to be decisive despite incomplete information rather than waiting for certainty.

## Key points

- The CEO feedback vacuum: power asymmetry makes honest feedback structurally rare — the CEO must actively design mechanisms to receive it.
- Learnable skill, not innate trait: the specific muscle to develop is decision quality under uncertainty, not charisma or vision.
- Post-hoc evaluation is critical: comparing actual outcomes to your stated reasoning at decision time is how you detect flawed mental models.
- The expert-to-generalist transition: technical founders must shift from being the most-informed person in the room to deciding across domains where they lack full context.
- Ben Horowitz drew from his experience as CEO of Opsware — a company that nearly failed multiple times before selling to HP for $1.6B.

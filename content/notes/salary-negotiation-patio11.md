## Summary

Patrick McKenzie (patio11) wrote this essay in 2012 and it remains the canonical reference on salary negotiation in tech. The central argument: most employees don't negotiate because they fear it's inappropriate or risky, but these fears are largely unfounded. Employers almost never rescind offers over negotiation, and the expected value of asking is strongly positive — even a 5-10% increase compounds significantly over a career.

The framework McKenzie builds is rooted in information asymmetry. Employers negotiate salaries constantly; most employees do it a handful of times in their lives. Professionals who negotiate habitually — real estate agents, lawyers, car dealers — accept this as normal. The framing matters: position negotiation as ensuring both sides feel the deal is fair, not as adversarial conflict. The magic phrase he recommends is "I'm very interested in working here, but I need to resolve the compensation before accepting." This keeps you from refusing or accepting — just deferring the decision until the number moves.

He also covers the specific mechanics: never give a number first (whoever names first loses anchoring advantage), counter every number, let silence work for you, and get everything in writing. The essay distinguishes between base salary (hardest to change post-hire) and total compensation (where there's more flexibility in signing bonus, equity, vacation, title).

## Key points

- Never give the first number — let the employer anchor, then counter regardless.
- Negotiation is almost never unsafe: offers are rarely rescinded over a counter.
- The information asymmetry is severe — employers negotiate constantly, candidates rarely.
- Compound impact: a $5k increase in starting salary, raised at the same percentage each year, is worth much more over a career.
- Get everything in writing before giving notice to current employer.
- By Patrick McKenzie (patio11), also author of Don't End The Week With Nothing.

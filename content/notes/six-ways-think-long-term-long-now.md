## Summary

The Long Now Foundation published this essay synthesizing six distinct cognitive tools for extending temporal perspective in decision-making. The Long Now Foundation (stewarded by Stewart Brand, Brian Eno, and others) is explicitly focused on 10,000-year thinking — their 10,000 Year Clock is the physical embodiment of this goal. This post is a more practical distillation of what long-term thinking actually means in practice.

The six tools: (1) **Deep Time Humility** — developing visceral intuition for geological and evolutionary timescales, which makes our current moment feel less uniquely pivotal and decisions feel more reversible than we assume. (2) **Cathedral Thinking** — medieval cathedral builders accepted they wouldn't see the finished work; some investments are right to make across generations. (3) **Seven Generation Thinking** (from Haudenosaunee governance tradition) — explicitly considering the impact of decisions seven generations out (~175 years). (4) **Legacy Thinking** — considering what you want to leave behind, which reframes short-term tradeoffs. (5) **Presilience** — thinking about resilience before crises rather than after. (6) **Backcasting** — starting from a desired future and working backward to understand what must be true today.

These tools are particularly relevant for product and organizational decisions where short-term incentives systematically underweight long-term consequences — an endemic problem in technology companies optimizing for quarterly metrics.

## Key points

- Cathedral Thinking: accept that some investments pay off across generations, not quarters.
- Seven Generation Thinking: Indigenous governance principle of 7-generation impact assessment (~175 years).
- Backcasting: define the desired future, then work backward to identify present actions.
- Deep Time Humility: geological/evolutionary timescales make current decisions feel less permanently consequential.
- From the Long Now Foundation — associated with Stewart Brand, Brian Eno, and 10,000-year thinking.
- Complementary cognitive tools, not a hierarchy — different tools suit different decision contexts.

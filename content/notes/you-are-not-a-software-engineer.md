## Summary

Chris Aitchison's 2011 essay argues that the engineering metaphor misleads how we think about software development. Real engineering — bridges, circuits — starts with complete specifications and builds to plan. Software is fundamentally different: requirements change mid-build, systems grow in unpredictable directions, and the code itself is more like a living thing than a constructed artifact.

His proposed alternative is the **gardener** metaphor. Gardens grow organically, require ongoing tending, need pruning and weeding (refactoring), and can't be fully specified upfront. A good gardener works with the natural tendencies of the plants rather than forcing a rigid plan. Similarly, good programmers work with the grain of their codebase — finding the shapes that want to emerge rather than imposing an architecture designed before understanding the problem.

This connects to debates about agile software development and technical debt — both of which make more sense under the gardening framing than the engineering one. Technical debt isn't a construction defect; it's an overgrown garden that needs attention. Refactoring isn't fixing mistakes; it's seasonal maintenance.

## Key points

- The engineer metaphor implies upfront specification, predictable construction, and static artifacts — none of which describe how software actually works.
- The gardener metaphor captures organic growth, ongoing maintenance, unpredictable evolution, and the need for continuous care.
- Refactoring maps naturally to pruning: necessary, ongoing, and not a sign of original failure.
- Technical debt reframes as neglected garden — something that accumulates naturally and requires periodic attention rather than being a failure mode.
- The metaphor you use shapes your management practices: engineering thinking leads to Gantt charts and "done" milestones; gardening thinking leads to ongoing investment and living systems.

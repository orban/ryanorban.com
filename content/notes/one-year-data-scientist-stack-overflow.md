## Summary

David Robinson — statistician, author of the broom and tidytext R packages, and later Chief Data Scientist at DataCamp — wrote this retrospective after his first year as a data scientist at Stack Overflow. The post is notable for its honesty about the gap between the academic statistics training most data scientists have and what the job actually requires.

The core observation: communication is more important than algorithms. Robinson found that the hardest part of the role wasn't building models — it was convincing stakeholders that the findings were real and mattered, and presenting analyses in ways that non-statisticians could act on. This required developing new skills in visualization, in writing clear summaries, and in knowing which analysis to do rather than how to do it. The idea that data science is mostly linear regression and communication — not fancy models — recurs throughout.

He also reflects on the value of public work: his contributions to open source R packages and his Variance Explained blog generated the reputation that got him the job. This was an early articulation of what became a common career advice trope in data science — build in public, create examples, contribute to open source. Stack Overflow's unique position as a company whose product is a dataset made it a particularly interesting place to work: the company's questions, answers, and user behavior were themselves the primary data source for internal analyses.

## Key points

- Communication > algorithms: the hardest part of data science at a company is getting findings believed and acted on, not building the model.
- R fluency — specifically ggplot2 and dplyr — was more useful than deep ML knowledge for this role.
- Building in public (Variance Explained blog, open source R packages) was the primary hiring signal.
- Stack Overflow's data is its product — the internal data science role had access to uniquely rich behavioral data from millions of developers.
- Written by David Robinson in 2016; he later became a prominent voice for Tidyverse methods and written communication in data science.

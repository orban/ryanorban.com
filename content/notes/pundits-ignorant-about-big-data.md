## Summary

Andrew McAfee (co-author of The Second Machine Age and MIT Sloan researcher) wrote this sharp Harvard Business Review critique of the shallow statistical reasoning appearing in mainstream commentary about big data in 2013. The piece catalogued specific types of errors: treating correlation as causation, using anecdotes to disprove statistical findings, and conflating we found a pattern with we understand the mechanism.

McAfee's core target was the pattern where a pundit would hear a big data claim, find one counter-example, and conclude the claim was wrong. This is the "Texas Sharpshooter Fallacy" applied in reverse — drawing a target around the one hole that disproves the claim rather than evaluating the preponderance of evidence. Statistical literacy demands understanding that aggregate patterns hold even when individual exceptions exist; one person who smoked and lived to 95 doesn't refute the link between smoking and lung cancer.

The piece also called out the it's just correlation dismissal — treating correlation as meaningless without causation. McAfee's rejoinder: predictive analytics doesn't need causal understanding to be useful. If certain patterns in customer behavior reliably predict churn, acting on that pattern has value even without a causal story. Causation is needed to intervene (change the cause); correlation is sufficient to predict (anticipate the outcome).

## Key points

- **Correlation vs. causation**: correlation is sufficient for prediction, required for intervention — conflating them causes both false dismissals and overconfident interventions
- **Anecdote as counterevidence**: individual exceptions don't disprove population-level statistical findings — sample size and statistical significance exist precisely to handle individual variation
- **Base rate neglect** (related): media coverage of big data results often omitted confidence intervals, sample sizes, and effect sizes — making claims sound stronger or weaker than they were
- Andrew McAfee and Erik Brynjolfsson were among the most credible academic voices on data economics in this period — MIT backing gave these critiques more weight than practitioner complaints
- The piece was influential in the data journalism community — cited in style guides for how to report on statistical findings
- Data literacy as a professional SKILL: the failures McAfee catalogued weren't just media failures but reflected a gap between the pace of data adoption and the statistical education of business decision-makers

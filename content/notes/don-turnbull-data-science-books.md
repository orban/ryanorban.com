## Summary

Don Turnbull (then at the University of Texas iSchool) shared book recommendations for people entering or deepening their data science practice via DataScholars. In 2013, "data science" as a unified field was new enough that there was genuine ambiguity about what canonical reading list the field had — unlike software engineering (which had *The Pragmatic Programmer*, *SICP*, *Clean Code*) or statistics (which had Gelman, Hastie, or Rice depending on orientation), data science didn't yet have a settled canon.

The 2013 consensus reading list for data scientists tended to cluster around a few books: The Elements of Statistical Learning (Hastie, Tibshirani, Friedman) for ML theory; Python for Data Analysis (Wes McKinney) for pandas-based practice; Data Science for Business (Provost & Fawcett) for business context; and classics like Tufte's *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* for visualization principles. Applied statistics texts like Think Stats (Allen Downey) were popular for programmers approaching statistics.

Turnbull's iSchool context likely shaped toward the information science and communication ends of data science — how to ask good questions, how to design data collection, how to communicate findings — rather than pure ML engineering. The post represents the genre of "what should I read to become a data scientist?" that was ubiquitous in 2012-2014, when the field's practitioners were defining it in real time.

## Key points

- The 2013 data science canon was forming in real time — no settled you must read X the way mature fields have.
- The Elements of Statistical Learning (ESL): the rigorous ML reference — freely available PDF from Stanford.
- Think Stats (Allen Downey): statistics for programmers — Python-based, intuition-first approach that matched the self-teaching data scientist demographic.
- Edward Tufte: visualization principles — *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* remained essential despite predating the web by decades.
- iSchool perspective: data science from the information management angle emphasizes question design, data quality, and communication over pure algorithmic sophistication.

## Summary

At Stanford GSB's 2012 entrepreneur conference, a panel of executives and investors laid out the emerging big data opportunity. The framing: data was growing faster than Moore's Law, creating a structural gap between what could be generated and what could be analyzed. This gap was both the problem and the business opportunity.

The analogy drawn was to the oil industry's development: raw data is like crude oil — you need extraction, refinement, and distribution infrastructure before it's valuable. The panel predicted entirely new business categories would emerge around data management, just as financial services emerged around money. The first order effect would be enormous in advertising, where Google's click targeting had demonstrated that moving from 2% to 3% conversion rates was worth billions — and there was an order of magnitude more headroom.

Privacy regulation was flagged as a significant future constraint, already reshaping European markets and expected to arrive in the US. The value of personal data in contexts like insurance pricing was estimated at thousands of dollars per individual — which made privacy policy a financial issue, not just an ethical one. This tension between data value and data rights became one of the defining issues of the 2010s.

## Key points

- Data growth is outpacing Moore's Law, creating persistent infrastructure demand.
- The oil-economy analogy: raw data needs extraction, refinement, distribution layers — each a business opportunity.
- Advertising is the most immediately impacted sector: marginal improvements in targeting are worth billions.
- Personal data carries concrete monetary value (thousands of dollars per person in insurance contexts).
- Privacy regulation was already reshaping European markets in 2012 — US expected to follow.
- Unique data assets (competitors can't replicate them) are more valuable than analytical sophistication alone.

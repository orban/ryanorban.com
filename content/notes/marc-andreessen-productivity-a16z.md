## Summary

This Andreessen Horowitz post covers Marc Andreessen's personal productivity system, which is notable for being deliberately anti-conventional. The most striking element: Andreessen largely operates without a structured schedule, in what he's called a no schedule approach — no standing meetings, no time-blocked calendar. Instead, he stays in reactive mode, responding to what comes up and following interests in real time. This is the opposite of the deep work / maker's schedule advice that dominates productivity writing.

The system makes more sense given his specific job. A partner at a large venture firm isn't primarily a deep, focused creator — he's a connector, a pattern-recognizer across domains, and an opportunity-identifier. This role benefits from high information diversity and serendipitous connections more than from uninterrupted focus blocks. The productivity advice that applies to programmers, writers, and researchers may not apply to investors.

His reading approach is similarly voracious and undisciplined by most standards: he reads widely across history, technology, economics, and culture rather than tracking to a reading goal. The breadth is the point — lateral thinking and pattern recognition across domains requires cross-domain input.

## Key points

- The "no schedule" approach is a deliberate choice suited to a connector/investor role — not a general recommendation for knowledge workers who need deep focus blocks.
- Reading breadth over depth: wide coverage across domains builds the mental models and analogies that make cross-domain insight possible.
- Andreessen is a strong advocate of reading history as a predictive tool — past technological transitions inform how current ones will unfold.
- The contrast with Cal Newport's Deep Work framework is instructive: different roles have different optimal productivity structures. Newport's advice targets knowledge workers with clear deliverables; Andreessen's role is defined by serendipity and insight.
- Related: Paul Graham's essays on keeping one's schedule open; Tyler Cowen's voracious-reading approach as an economist.

## Summary

Tindie launched in 2012 as a marketplace for independent hardware makers to sell their custom electronics projects — Ryan described it as Etsy for electronics. The platform targeted the growing maker community that had emerged around Arduino, Raspberry Pi, and open-source hardware, providing a place to sell small-batch runs of custom boards, sensors, modules, and accessories that didn't fit into the categories served by electronics distributors like Mouser or Digikey.

The timing was good: the maker movement was accelerating in 2012, with Maker Faire growing, Kickstarter funding hardware projects, and 3D printing making small-scale manufacturing more accessible. The missing piece was a distribution channel specifically for the long tail of small-batch hardware products — the custom I2C sensor breakout boards, specialized development boards, and niche accessories that a single engineer might make 100 units of. Tindie provided that channel.

The analogy to Etsy was apt but also revealing of the challenge: Etsy works because handmade physical goods are low-risk purchases (worst case, a $20 scarf is disappointing). Electronics carry more risk — a poorly designed board can damage other components, brick a microcontroller, or simply not work as described. Tindie addressed this through ratings and seller reputation. Tindie was acquired by Hackaday parent company Supplyframe in 2016, giving it more stable backing within the hardware/maker ecosystem.

## Key points

- Tindie is a marketplace for indie hardware makers to sell small-batch electronics projects.
- Targeted the gap between maker/hobbyist production and traditional electronics distribution channels.
- Built on the momentum of Arduino, Raspberry Pi, Kickstarter, and the broader maker movement of 2011–2013.
- Analogy to Etsy for handmade goods — same long-tail marketplace model, applied to electronics.
- Risk profile differs from craft goods: electronics quality/correctness matters more, driving seller reputation importance.
- Acquired by Supplyframe (parent of Hackaday) in 2016 — embedded in the maker ecosystem's media/commerce side.

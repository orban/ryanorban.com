## Summary

In 2012, a widely-reported study claimed that DNA had limited power to predict common diseases — contradicting much of the promise behind the Human Genome Project and the subsequent wave of investment in genomics and personalized medicine. The study found that identical twins (who share virtually all their DNA) diverge significantly in health outcomes, suggesting that genetic factors explain less of disease risk than proponents claimed.

UCSF researchers (likely in the lab of Atul Butte or affiliated groups) pushed back strongly. Their argument: the study underestimated genomics' value by focusing only on common disease prediction and ignoring the many conditions where genetic factors are highly deterministic — rare diseases, pharmacogenomics (how genes affect drug response), and specific variants with large effect sizes. The bookmarker's comment captured the tension well: "Study Says DNA's Power to Predict Illness Is Limited. Who'd have thought? Experts in human genetics, for starters." — a dry note that the critics of the negative study were the domain experts.

The debate was important because 2012 was when companies like 23andMe (consumer genomics), and early precision medicine initiatives were trying to translate genomic research into products. If DNA couldn't reliably predict common diseases, the consumer genomics business model was in question. UCSF's response argued for a more nuanced framing: genomics is not uniformly predictive, but the cases where it is are clinically important, and the field was early enough that dismissing it based on current limitations was premature.

## Key points

- The debate: genomic risk scores for common diseases (type 2 diabetes, heart disease) had weak predictive power by 2012, leading some to question the entire project.
- UCSF counter-argument: strong genetic signals exist for rare diseases and pharmacogenomics — these were being ignored in the critique.
- 23andMe had launched consumer genomics in 2007 — the predictive power debate directly affected its value proposition.
- Twin studies showed that identical twins diverge in health outcomes despite sharing DNA, pointing to epigenetics and environment.
- The resolution: genomics is powerful for specific applications (rare disease, drug response) but not for general common disease prediction with the technology of 2012.
- Precision medicine has since become more specifically defined — targeting subgroups where genomic factors are decisive, not everyone.

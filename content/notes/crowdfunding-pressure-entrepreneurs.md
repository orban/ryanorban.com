## Summary

By mid-2012, Kickstarter had produced several massively successful campaigns — Pebble raised $10M, Double Fine Adventure raised $3M — and the platform was being celebrated as a way for creators to bypass traditional gatekeepers. The NYT piece examined what happened after: many creators were struggling to actually fulfill their promises, having raised money on the basis of prototypes, optimistic timelines, and manufacturing cost estimates that turned out to be wildly wrong.

Hardware crowdfunding was the worst case. A working prototype and a compelling video could raise hundreds of thousands of dollars from backers who were effectively pre-ordering a product that didn't exist. The gap between prototype and manufacturable product is brutal: tooling costs, component sourcing, quality control, customs, logistics — each of these can multiply the per-unit cost several times. Creators who raised $500K assuming $50/unit cost might discover actual landed cost was $120/unit. They'd taken on the obligations of a product company without the infrastructure to fulfill them.

Kickstarter projects raised the expectation of accountability without the protections: backers were owed nothing legally, there were no refund mechanisms, and the platform took no responsibility for delivery. This created a new kind of public obligation — the creator owed their backers morally even when they couldn't deliver legally. The social pressure of having hundreds or thousands of public supporters waiting was a new psychological burden that traditional VC-funded startups didn't face.

## Key points

- Hardware crowdfunding projects routinely underestimated manufacturing costs by 2x–5x due to tooling, sourcing, and logistics realities
- Kickstarter creates moral obligation to backers without legal mechanism — creators are exposed to reputational harm, not just financial loss
- Successful campaigns created premature public commitment before product viability was proven — opposite of lean startup's validate before building
- Pebble and Double Fine Adventure were the 2012 poster cases; Pebble later struggled to deliver on promised features
- The platform evolved to add updates, stretch goals, and backer communication norms — all attempts to manage the accountability gap

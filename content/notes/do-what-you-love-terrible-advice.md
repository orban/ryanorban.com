## Summary

Jeff Haden's Inc.com piece argues that do what you love is bad career advice because it gets the causal relationship backwards. Most people don't have pre-existing passions that clearly translate to careers; passion for work typically develops after becoming competent at it, not before. The article draws on Cal Newport's research (particularly So Good They Can't Ignore You) showing that people who have compelling, satisfying careers usually describe developing passion for their work after years of deliberate practice — the passion came from the mastery, not the other way around.

The practical problem with "do what you love" as advice: it encourages people to search for a pre-existing passion, quit jobs when that passion doesn't materialize, and resist developing deep expertise in fields that don't feel immediately exciting. It treats career choice as self-discovery rather than SKILL development.

The alternative framing: focus on getting very good at something that has genuine value, that you have some aptitude for, and that allows you to accumulate career capital (skills and reputation that give you options). Passion follows from autonomy and mastery, which follow from competence — competence that takes years to build and doesn't feel like "love" in the early stages.

## Key points

- Passion follows mastery, not the reverse — competence creates the conditions for engaging, satisfying work
- "Do what you love" is survivorship bias: successful people who love their work arrived at that love through years of skill development, then retroactively reframe it as having followed their passion
- Cal Newport's career capital model: invest in rare, valuable skills; use that capital to get autonomy and alignment with what matters — passion is a byproduct
- The advice is particularly harmful for early-career people who abandon skill-building in search of pre-existing passion
- Deliberate practice and tolerance for early incompetence are prerequisites for the mastery that enables career satisfaction

## Summary

This tweet observes that while traditional Moore's Law (transistor density doubling roughly every 2 years) has slowed, the number of machine learning papers submitted to arXiv has continued to grow exponentially. The implication is striking: staying genuinely current with ML research requires reading 100+ papers per day — making comprehensive coverage impossible for any individual researcher.

The observation is empirically well-supported. arXiv cs.LG submissions grew from a few hundred per month in 2012 to tens of thousands per month by 2022. The field expanded from a small research community to one including major corporate labs (Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, DeepMind), hundreds of university groups globally, and an expanding practitioner community contributing methods papers. The growth reflected both genuine scientific progress and the career incentives of academia, where paper count remains a primary measure of productivity.

The consequences of this volume problem are real: important work gets lost, incremental papers consume as much attention as fundamental ones, replication crises go undetected because reviewers lack bandwidth, and practitioners fall farther behind the frontier. The response has been specialization (researchers track only their subfields), tools (semantic search over arXiv via Semantic Scholar, Connected Papers), and community curation (Twitter / X must-read paper culture, newsletters like The Batch and Import AI). None of these fully solve the problem — the knowledge graph grows faster than navigation tools can adapt.

## Key points

- arXiv ML paper submissions grew exponentially 2012-2022; estimated 100+ papers/day to stay current as of 2022.
- Moore's Law analogy: computational scaling slowed while research output scaling accelerated.
- Consequence: genuine comprehensiveness is impossible — researchers specialize further or fall behind.
- Tools for navigation: Semantic Scholar, Connected Papers, Papers With Code, curated newsletters.
- The knowledge fragmentation problem: important work goes unnoticed, replication failures persist, practitioner gap widens.

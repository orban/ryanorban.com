## Summary

Written by Fred Culick (Caltech) and Henry Jex (Systems Technology, Inc.) for the Smithsonian's 80th anniversary symposium, this AIAA Wright Flyer Project report applies modern wind-tunnel testing and analytical methods to the 1903 Wright Flyer. The LA section of AIAA built two replicas — one for wind tunnel tests, one modified to fly — and used vortex-lattice computation and small-scale tunnel data to characterize the aircraft's aerodynamic properties.

The central finding is counterintuitive: the 1903 Flyer was intrinsically unstable in both pitch and roll. The Wrights were sophisticated about flight control (their wing-warp-to-rudder linkage is validated as genuinely clever) but were "largely ignorant of dynamic stability considerations." The aircraft could barely be stabilized by a skilled pilot under controlled conditions — meaning Wilbur and Orville were extraordinary aviators as much as extraordinary engineers.

Using quasilinear pilot-vehicle analysis and simulation, the report shows that closed-loop control by a skilled pilot was feasible but demanding. The biplane canard configuration is analyzed as a multi-surface system. The Wrights had "practically all the fundamental understanding they needed to show the world how to fly" by December 17, 1903 — but the margin was thinner than the mythology suggests.

## Key points

- Modern analysis confirms the 1903 Flyer was inherently unstable in pitch and roll — controllable but barely
- The wing-warp-to-rudder linkage is validated as effective: the Wrights understood control coupling even without stability theory
- Vortex-lattice computation and wind-tunnel data agree closely, validating the aerodynamic model
- The Wrights lacked formal dynamic stability theory but compensated with exceptional pilot SKILL and incremental testing
- The canard configuration (horizontal stabilizer forward of the wings) was novel and contributed to the pitch instability

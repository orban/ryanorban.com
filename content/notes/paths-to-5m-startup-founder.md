## Summary

Gabriel Weinberg, founder of DuckDuckGo, wrote this as a practical financial reality check for founders who imagine startup success as a uniform path to wealth. The post maps out the different ways a founder can actually end up with $5 million, and the math behind each path — which turns out to require very different outcomes depending on your equity stake and dilution.

The core insight: a $5M personal outcome from a startup requires vastly different company outcomes depending on how much equity you own. A founder with 50% equity needs a $10M acquisition. A founder who's raised a Series A and B and is down to 10% equity needs a $50M exit. A founder at 2% post-Series C needs a $250M exit just to clear $5M. The dilution math makes early outcomes and secondary sales much more attractive than they appear from the outside.

Weinberg also distinguishes the lifestyle business path: a profitable company generating $1M/year in profit, paying the founder a salary and dividends, gets you to $5M in five years without any exit. This path is largely invisible in startup discourse because it doesn't make headlines, but it's available to many more founders than the unicorn track.

## Key points

- Dilution changes the exit math dramatically — a 10% equity stake requires a $50M acquisition to net $5M.
- The lifestyle business path (profitable SMB, no exit needed) is underrated and more achievable.
- Secondary sales (selling founder shares before a company exit) are an option that lets founders take chips off the table early.
- Venture capital funding optimizes for large exits, not founder wealth — misaligned incentives are real.
- The $5M target is useful as a concrete anchor; abstract "success" discussions obscure the actual math.
- Gabriel Weinberg went on to build DuckDuckGo as a profitable, independent company — practicing what he preached.

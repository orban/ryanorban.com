## Summary

This Nature paper from the Allen Institute for Brain Science published a comprehensive gene expression atlas of the adult human brain — mapping the activity of roughly 20,000 genes across nearly 1,000 anatomically distinct regions in two donor brains. The Allen Human Brain Atlas was the result of several years of systematic sampling: each brain was sectioned, regions identified by expert neuroanatomists, and RNA extracted and assayed on microarray chips to measure which genes were active and at what levels.

The core finding: roughly 84% of all human genes are expressed somewhere in the brain, with gene expression patterns highly consistent between the two individuals — suggesting the transcriptome of brain regions is more stereotyped than previously understood. The atlas revealed that different brain regions have strikingly distinct gene expression signatures, with cortical regions more similar to each other and subcortical regions more distinct. This molecular fingerprinting of brain anatomy had immediate implications for understanding neurological and psychiatric disorders.

The dataset was made publicly available at brain.alleninstitute.org, which was significant in itself — the Allen Institute (funded by Paul Allen) had a track record of releasing large neuroscience datasets as public goods rather than publishing and paywalling. This open-data philosophy made the atlas a widely used research resource, and it underpins significant subsequent work on the molecular bases of schizophrenia, autism spectrum disorder, and Alzheimer's disease.

## Key points

- Allen Human Brain Atlas: ~20,000 genes × ~1,000 brain regions × 2 donors — the first comprehensive spatial transcriptomics map of the human brain
- 84% of human genes expressed somewhere in the brain; highly consistent patterns between individuals
- Cortical regions share more gene expression profiles with each other than with subcortical regions — molecular evidence for functional divisions
- Released as open data at brain.alleninstitute.org — Allen Institute's explicit strategy of funding large public neuroscience datasets
- Foundational for GWAS follow-up: knowing where a disease-linked gene is expressed helps prioritize brain regions for study

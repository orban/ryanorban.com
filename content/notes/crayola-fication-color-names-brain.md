## Summary

This piece from Empirical Zeal explores how the act of naming colors changes how humans perceive them — a case study in linguistic relativity applied to visual perception. The series uses the Crayola crayon company's historical color naming practice as a lens: as Crayola added more named colors (splitting red into scarlet, brick red, maroon), they were participating in a broader cultural process of carving perceptual space into discrete, named categories. The question is whether those names then feed back to shape perception itself.

The neuroscience evidence is striking. The left hemisphere of the brain is dominant for language processing. Studies using visual half-field paradigms — presenting colors to the left or right visual field, which routes them to the right or left hemisphere respectively — find that people are faster and more accurate at discriminating colors that cross a category boundary when the stimulus is in the right visual field (left hemisphere, language-dominant). The same physical color difference is harder to detect when it falls within a named category. In other words, language doesn't just describe the perceptual world — it participates in constructing it at the neural level.

The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis (or linguistic relativity) comes in two forms: the strong version (language determines thought — now largely discredited) and the weak version (language influences thought, particularly perception). This color research supports the weak version: having a name for a color category makes differences within that category harder to detect and differences across categories easier. The practical implication connects to how labeling systems in general — whether for colors, emotions, or social categories — create cognitive grooves that shape what we notice and how easily we discriminate. The Crayola-fication metaphor is apt: naming proliferates, and each new name reshapes the perceptual landscape.

## Key points

- Linguistic relativity applied to color perception: category names (red, scarlet, maroon) change how quickly and accurately we discriminate colors.
- Left hemisphere advantage: named color categories show perceptual facilitation in the right visual field (routes to left/language hemisphere) — direct neural evidence for language-perception interaction.
- Within-category differences are harder to detect than cross-category differences of the same physical magnitude — language creates perceptual grooves.
- Sapir-Whorf hypothesis weak version supported: language influences perception rather than determining it.
- Crayola's color naming history as a cultural experiment: proliferating names partition perceptual space in ways that then feed back into how children and adults experience color.
- Connected to broader questions about categorization, labeling, and framing in cognition — where labels are applied, attention and discrimination follow.

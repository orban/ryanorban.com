## Summary

Anthony Atala's 2011 TED talk became one of the most-viewed science talks in TED history. Atala, director of the Wake Forest Institute for Regenerative Medicine, walked onstage carrying a lab-grown kidney and proceeded to demonstrate a bioprinter printing a kidney scaffold live. The visual was striking enough that the audience gave a standing ovation before the talk ended.

The substantive content was more nuanced. Atala's lab had successfully implanted lab-engineered bladder tissue in patients — not printed, but grown on biodegradable scaffolds seeded with the patient's own cells. The printed kidney in the talk was a scaffold, not a functional organ; full organ bioprinting with vascularization remained an unsolved problem. But the direction was clear: tissue engineering was moving toward using patients' own cells to build replacement organs, eliminating the rejection problem.

The connection to BioCurious (this was bookmarked in a thread with @bobirakova and @PatrikD) shows the moment when DIY biology and community lab communities were looking at professional bioprinting as the direction their amateur work was pointing toward. The gap between what a community lab could do with a modified inkjet printer and what Atala could do was large, but the underlying technique was recognizably the same: deposit cells in a scaffold, let them self-organize.

## Key points

- Anthony Atala (Wake Forest) — demonstrated live bioprinting of a kidney scaffold at TED 2011.
- Lab had already successfully implanted lab-grown bladder constructs in patients by 2012.
- Key challenge not yet solved: vascularization — growing blood vessels through the tissue to keep it alive.
- Bioprinting vs. scaffold seeding: Atala's clinical successes used scaffolds + patient cells, not direct bioprinting.
- Same bookmark thread as BioCurious bioprinter work — shows DIY-bio community tracking professional tissue engineering.

## Summary

Ryan Waggoner makes a straightforward but underappreciated argument: most side projects, startups, and creative endeavors fail not from lack of talent or bad ideas but from inconsistency — the failure to show up and do the work day after day. Motivation spikes at the start of any project, then decays as reality sets in. The gap between initial enthusiasm and sustained habit is where most projects die.

The failure mode is rarely dramatic. It's not a pivotal decision that kills a side project — it's the gradual accumulation of skipped sessions, deferred writing days, delayed code commits. Each individual skip feels harmless; collectively they amount to project abandonment. Waggoner's point is that treating consistency as the primary SKILL to develop is more honest and more useful than focusing on strategy or technique when the real bottleneck is showing up at all.

This connects to a broader literature on habits and creative practice: Steven Pressfield's Resistance in *The War of Art*, Jerry Seinfeld's don't break the chain method, and behavioral research showing that habit formation depends more on regularity than on intensity. For technical work in particular — writing, coding, building — daily practice compounds in ways that sporadic bursts don't, both in skill development and in the momentum needed to ship anything complete.

## Key points

- The most common startup and side project killer isn't a bad idea or wrong market — it's the slow accumulation of missed work sessions.
- Motivation is unreliable as a driver of consistency; systems and habits are more durable.
- Showing up as a learnable skill distinct from the work itself — habit formation precedes skill development.
- Resistance (in Steven Pressfield's framing) is the force that makes not-working feel easier than working — showing up is the primary act of overcoming it.
- In technical work, daily compounding applies: consistent small efforts produce more complete output than sporadic intense sprints.

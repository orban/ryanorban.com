## Summary

This Wall Street Journal feature applies chronobiology research to everyday decisions about when to do different kinds of work. The core finding is that the body's circadian rhythm — the 24-hour biological clock driven by light, temperature, and hormonal cycles — creates predictable peaks and troughs in cognitive performance, physical ability, mood, and alertness. Most people operate on a fixed schedule regardless of these rhythms, leaving performance on the table.

The peak timing varies by activity type. Analytical and logical tasks — the kind that require focused attention and working memory — peak in the late morning for most people, when core body temperature is rising and cortisol is at an effective level. Creative and associative thinking peaks in the early afternoon or late morning, depending on chronotype (whether you're a morning person or evening person). Physical performance — strength, coordination, reaction time — peaks in the mid-to-late afternoon, when body temperature is highest. These are averages; individual chronotypes shift the windows by a few hours in either direction.

The article's practical framing is scheduling optimization: protect your peak analytical window for the work that demands it most, don't schedule important decisions during post-lunch alertness dips, time exercise when physical performance peaks rather than when it's convenient. The research draws on studies of surgery outcomes (morning surgery has lower complication rates), chess tournament performance, and athletic records. The underlying mechanism is the hormone cycle: melatonin dropping in the morning enables alertness, cortisol peaking in late morning drives focus, body temperature peaking in afternoon drives physical performance.

## Key points

- Circadian rhythm creates predictable cognitive and physical performance windows — not random variation.
- Analytical/logical peak: late morning for most chronotypes, when cortisol and body temperature are on the rise.
- Physical performance peak: mid-to-late afternoon, when core body temperature peaks — optimal for strength, speed, coordination.
- Post-lunch dip is real and biologically driven — not a discipline problem. Mediterranean cultures' siesta timing is consistent with this.
- Individual chronotype (morning vs. evening person) shifts these windows by ~2 hours; the relative ordering of peaks stays consistent.

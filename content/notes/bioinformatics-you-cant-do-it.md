## Summary

Mick Watson (opiniomics) makes a blunt argument: bioinformatics is a computational discipline, and if you can't write code, you shouldn't get to use the label. The post draws a sharp line between biologists who use bioinformatics tools (who are users, not bioinformaticians) and people who can actually build, debug, and extend those tools. His position: the field has a credentialing problem driven by journals and hiring processes that don't distinguish the two.

The argument resonated widely in 2014 because the same debate was playing out across data-related fields: data science, bioinformatics, econometrics, and computational social science all had practitioners who used statistical software as black boxes and others who built the methods. Watson's framing — you're not doing the field if you can't program — was intentionally provocative, drawing pushback from biologists who felt excluded from a field that analyzes their data.

The underlying practical point is real: people who can't code can't reproduce or audit others' analyses, can't adapt pipelines to new data formats, and can't catch bugs introduced by tools they don't understand. This is a genuine epistemological problem in computational biology. The post predates the reproducibility crisis discussions in computational biology by a couple of years but anticipates the same concerns.

## Key points

- Bioinformatics as a discipline requires programming — users of bioinformatics tools are not doing bioinformatics, they're consuming outputs.
- The credentialing problem: journals and hiring processes in 2014 didn't distinguish capable computational biologists from biological data consumers, which diluted standards.
- Connects to the broader data science debate: data scientist was being applied to everyone from database admins to ML researchers, similarly eroding the label's signal.
- The ability to code is an epistemological requirement, not just a convenience — you can't audit or reproduce analyses you can't read.
- Reproducibility in computational science requires code, data, and environment to be available — which requires the analyst to have produced code in the first place.

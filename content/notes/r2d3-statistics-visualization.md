## Summary

R2D3 is a data visualization project by Stephanie Yee and Tony Chu that produces scrollytelling essays explaining machine learning and statistics concepts through animated D3.js visualizations. The most well-known piece, "A Visual Introduction to Machine Learning," walks through how a decision tree algorithm works — the feature splits, the depth decisions, the overfitting tradeoff — using an interactive visualization of actual housing data from San Francisco and New York.

The key insight of the project is that the algorithmic mechanics of ML are often much easier to understand visually than mathematically. Watching a decision tree partition a space step-by-step, seeing how a deeper tree memorizes training data while a shallower one generalizes better, or observing the bias-variance tradeoff play out as you adjust model complexity — these build intuitions that equations alone struggle to convey.

R2D3 is notable as a high-craft example of data journalism applied to technical education. The scrollytelling format (scrolling triggers animation progression) was an emerging medium in 2015-2016 when the project launched, and R2D3 became a reference example of how it could be applied to technical content. Each essay is a substantial production — the animated decision tree essay alone represents months of design and engineering work.

The site has a small number of pieces (not a high-volume publication), each done with exceptional care. This makes it a catalog to read carefully rather than a feed to follow.

## Key points

- Scrollytelling format: scroll through the essay and animated D3.js visualizations respond in sync.
- Decision tree essay is the flagship piece — partition space visualization makes the algorithm's logic visible.
- Addresses the bias-variance tradeoff visually — overfitting and underfitting as geometric properties, not just terms.
- High-craft, low-volume publication — each essay is a significant production artifact.
- Part of the visual explanations genre alongside Distill.pub, [The Pudding](/notes/the-pudding/), and Seeing Theory (Brown University probability visualization).

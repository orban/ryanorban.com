## Summary

Sheila Heti's short essay for Brick Magazine addresses the question any writer or solitary creative faces: why go out at all? The pull of staying in — working, reading, having control over one's environment and attention — is real, and Heti doesn't dismiss it. But the essay articulates why going out remains necessary: other people carry experiences, information, and ways of being that cannot be accessed from inside a room. The accidents of social life — the conversation that takes an unexpected turn, the person you wouldn't have sought out but encounter by chance — produce material and perspective that deliberate solitary work cannot generate.

The essay sits in a tradition of writing about the creative value of flanerie and social engagement: Walter Benjamin on the Paris arcades, Jane Jacobs on sidewalk life, the general idea that ambient exposure to human variety produces ideas that pure contemplation cannot. Heti's version is more intimate and ambivalent than these grand theories — she's writing about the specific texture of going to a party you weren't sure you wanted to attend and finding something there anyway.

The underlying tension is between attention economy arguments for radical focus (eliminate distraction, protect deep work) and the equally valid observation that isolation produces a narrowing — the world gets smaller, the range of inputs contracts, the work begins to feed on itself. Neither side resolves the question cleanly. Heti's essay is valuable precisely because it doesn't resolve it: it holds the tension and describes it accurately, which is often what good essays do. At the time it was widely circulated in literary and creative communities as a concise articulation of something people felt but hadn't quite put into words.

## Key points

- Sheila Heti's core observation: other people provide accidents, friction, and exposure to alternative ways of being that deliberate solitary work cannot replicate.
- The case for going out isn't enjoyment — it's the unpredictable inputs that come only from physical presence among others.
- Tension with deep work arguments: isolation protects focus but narrows inputs; social engagement interrupts focus but expands material.
- Sits in a tradition of essays about creative contact with the world: flanerie, situationist drift, Jane Jacobs on sidewalk observation.
- The essay's form — short, precise, not resolving the tension — is itself an argument: some questions don't have answers, only accurate descriptions.
- Brick Magazine literary context: written for an audience of writers and readers who would recognize the specific ambivalence being described.

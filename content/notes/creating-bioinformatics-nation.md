## Summary

This Nature commentary (2002) argued that the explosive growth of genomic data was outpacing the computational expertise available to analyze it, and that building a bioinformatics workforce required treating it as a national infrastructure challenge rather than a niche academic specialty. The Human Genome Project was completing, and the coming decades would require analyzing not one genome but millions.

The parallel to the broader data science shortage is direct: biology became a data-intensive discipline faster than educational institutions could produce computationally skilled biologists. The result was a generation of biologists who could design experiments but couldn't process their own data, and computer scientists who could write algorithms but didn't understand the biology.

Bioinformatics as a discipline emerged at the intersection: people who could read FASTQ files, understand sequence alignment algorithms, and also interpret results in biological context. The talent shortage the article described in 2002 for biology foreshadowed the identical shortage in data science broadly a decade later — the same structural mismatch between data production rates and analytical capacity.

## Key points

- Human Genome Project completion created a data production / analysis capacity mismatch
- Bioinformatics talent requires depth in both computer science (algorithms, databases) and biology (domain context)
- The article called for dedicated bioinformatics training programs, not just ad-hoc cross-training
- The pattern generalizes: every field that becomes data-intensive faces the same interdisciplinary training challenge
- Sequence alignment (BLAST, Smith-Waterman), phylogenetics, and genome assembly as early core bioinformatics problems
- 2002 presaged the 2012 data science talent shortage — the structural problem is the same across domains

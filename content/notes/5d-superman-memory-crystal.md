## Summary

Researchers at the University of Southampton demonstrated a technique for writing data into quartz glass (vitreous silica) using femtosecond laser pulses — a method they dubbed 5D optical data storage. The five dimensions refer to three spatial coordinates (x, y, z) plus the orientation and wavelength of the light used to encode each voxel. By encoding information in all five parameters rather than just presence/absence, they dramatically increased the information density per unit volume.

The numbers were striking: 360 terabytes per disc, with theoretical stability of 13.8 billion years at room temperature. Conventional optical media (CD, DVD, Blu-ray) degrades over decades due to oxidation and delamination; glass is chemically inert and mechanically stable over geological timescales. The read mechanism uses a polarized light microscope combined with a birefringence analyzer to decode the orientation-encoded bits.

The popular press coverage gave it the Superman memory crystal label — a reference to the crystalline data storage depicted in the Superman films. This was legitimate research from the Optoelectronics Research Centre, and Microsoft later pursued a similar approach through Project Silica, storing Warner Bros. films in glass for archival purposes. The fundamental challenge remains throughput: femtosecond laser writing is slow, making it suitable for archival rather than active storage.

## Key points

- Five dimensions of encoding: x, y, z position plus light orientation and wavelength — multiplying the information density over standard 3D optical storage.
- Quartz glass as medium: chemically inert, no organic components to degrade — theoretically stable over billions of years compared to decades for conventional optical media.
- 360 TB/disc capacity: orders of magnitude above Blu-ray at roughly 25-100 GB.
- Microsoft Project Silica later commercialized a similar approach for archival cold storage, validating the underlying physics.
- Write speed bottleneck: femtosecond laser inscription is slow — practical for archives, not transactional storage.

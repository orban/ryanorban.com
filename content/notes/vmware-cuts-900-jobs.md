## Summary

VMware announced layoffs of approximately 900 employees in January 2013 — about 5% of its workforce — citing a "planned exit of certain business lines." Shares fell 15% on the announcement. The timing placed this in the middle of VMware's broader strategic struggle with OpenStack and the accelerating shift of enterprise workloads to AWS and other public clouds.

The exit of certain business lines language pointed to products that hadn't succeeded: VMware's attempts to build products beyond server virtualization — cloud management tooling, collaboration software, and some specialized infrastructure products — were being wound down as the company focused on its core vSphere platform and expanded into software-defined networking (NSX) and hyper-converged infrastructure.

In retrospect, 2013 was a critical inflection point for VMware. The company was highly profitable (its vSphere hypervisor had near-monopoly share in enterprise virtualization) but facing a structural question: as compute moved to public cloud, the VMware stack moved with it only partially. VMware responded by building vCloud Hybrid Service (later VMware Cloud on AWS), but the long-term resolution was Broadcom's 2023 acquisition and the subsequent strategic restructuring that eliminated many of VMware's standalone product lines — echoing what the 2013 layoffs previewed.

## Key points

- 900 jobs, ~5% of workforce; shares fell 15% on announcement
- "Exit of certain business lines" indicated failed product diversification attempts outside core virtualization
- Strategic context: OpenStack commoditizing private virtualization, AWS capturing new workloads
- VMware was responding by focusing on NSX (software-defined networking) and vSAN (hyper-converged storage)
- Historical arc: 2013 restructuring foreshadowed the much larger changes after Broadcom's 2023 acquisition

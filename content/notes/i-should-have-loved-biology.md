## Summary

James Somers writes about why he never fell in love with biology in school — and why he should have. The essay argues that biology education presents life's machinery as a catalog of facts to memorize rather than as one of the deepest mysteries in the universe. The textbooks extracted the quest and left only the answers.

The turning point in the essay: Somers encounters The Cell: A Molecular Approach and discovers that the inside of a cell is more intricate and strange than anything in science fiction. Molecular machines — ATP synthase spinning at 9,000 RPM, ribosomes translating mRNA into proteins at 20 amino acids per second, DNA polymerase proofreading its own work — are genuinely astonishing when presented as what they are: evolved nanomachines operating in a physical world that we're only now beginning to image directly.

The critique applies broadly: most science education teaches conclusions rather than the live questions. Students learn that DNA encodes proteins, but not how mind-bending the question how does matter become alive? actually is. The essay is partly a lament and partly a manifesto for teaching science by leading with the questions that have no satisfying answer — the same questions that make researchers get out of bed. It's one of the better essays on the philosophy of science education, and connects to broader ideas about curiosity and how institutions systematically suppress it.

## Key points

- Biology curricula replace wonder with memorization — students learn what cells do without encountering how strange it is that they do it at all
- Molecular machines like ATP synthase, ribosomes, and kinesin motor proteins are legitimately astonishing objects that most biologists never fully appreciated themselves
- Good science education should lead with open questions rather than established answers
- The essay is by James Somers, also known for You Should Be Playing Bocce and other essays on learning and craft
- Connects to the Richard Feynman tradition of treating science as appreciation rather than expertise

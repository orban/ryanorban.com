## Summary

Jonah Lehrer's New Yorker "Frontal Cortex" column examined a puzzle: why do many people reject well-established scientific findings — evolution, climate change, vaccine safety — even when presented with clear evidence? The answer isn't ignorance. Research by Dan Kahan at Yale Law School and others showed that more scientifically literate individuals are often *better* at selectively interpreting evidence to confirm their prior beliefs — a phenomenon Kahan called "motivated numeracy."

The cognitive mechanism is motivated reasoning: when evidence threatens a belief tied to group identity or worldview, the brain's emotional systems activate first. Rather than updating on the evidence, people unconsciously seek reasons to dismiss it — identifying methodological flaws, citing outlier studies, or simply refusing to engage. This is not a failure of intelligence. It's a feature of how human social cognition evolved: maintaining group cohesion often mattered more than tracking truth.

This creates a peculiar situation for science communication: simply providing more facts doesn't work, and can backfire (the backfire effect, though later replications have been mixed). What does work is more disputed. Dan Kahan's research suggests framing matters more than content — who delivers the information and whether it signals in-group or out-group membership affects acceptance more than the information itself.

## Key points

- Motivated reasoning: the brain filters evidence through prior commitments, especially for identity-linked beliefs.
- Dan Kahan's "motivated numeracy": numerically sophisticated people are *better* at selectively misreading data to confirm prior beliefs.
- More information often doesn't help — and can trigger the backfire effect (though this effect has been hard to replicate consistently).
- Social framing matters: who delivers scientific information and whether they signal shared identity affects acceptance.
- Lehrer's piece was part of a wave of cognitive science popularizations in the early 2010s (Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* was published the same year).

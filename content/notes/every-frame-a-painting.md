## Summary

Every Frame a Painting is a video essay series by Tony Zhou and Taylor Ramos that ran from 2014-2016, analyzing the formal elements of filmmaking: cinematography, editing rhythms, use of negative space, sound design, and how directors develop visual language. Each essay focuses on a single filmmaker or technique, running 5-12 minutes.

The series became the reference example for the video essay genre on the internet — precisely crafted, analytically rigorous, and accessible without dumbing down. Essays on Edgar Wright's visual comedy, David Fincher's insert shots, Stanley Kubrick's one-point perspective, and Buster Keaton's physical comedy are widely cited as the best explanations of those filmmakers' techniques available in any medium.

For people who think visually or communicate with images, Every Frame a Painting is training material — not for filmmaking specifically, but for understanding how visual decisions carry meaning. The same principles apply to data visualization, UI design, and presentation: framing, emphasis, rhythm, and the relationship between what you show and what you leave out.

## Key points

- Focus on film form: the *how* of filmmaking (composition, editing, sound) rather than plot or theme — trains the eye for intentional visual decisions.
- Hosted on Vimeo (higher quality than YouTube in 2014) and later archived widely; the series ended in 2016 with a reflective post on why they stopped.
- Tony Zhou's essay on Jackie Chan's action choreography is one of the most-shared short-form film analyses — demonstrates how visual grammar enables storytelling without dialogue.
- The series predates the 2016-2020 video essay boom on YouTube; Every Frame a Painting set the template that Nerdwriter1, Like Stories of Old, and others followed.
- Adjacent SKILL: understanding visual storytelling applies directly to data visualization — both involve choosing what to show, at what scale, and in what sequence.

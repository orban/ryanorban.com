## Summary

Eric Fischer's Geotaggers' World Atlas Flickr set contains visualizations of tweet density correlations — overlaying geotagged tweet data against other geographic variables to find patterns. Fischer was a cartographer at Mapbox who became known for making large-scale social media geotag datasets visible as maps.

Fischer's core methodology: collect geotagged tweets via the Twitter streaming API, bin them by location at city-block or neighborhood granularity, and render them as density maps. The correlations work investigated what predicts tweet density beyond just population density — revealing that daytime commercial activity, nightlife, tourist areas, and income levels all correlate differently with where people tweet.

The Geotaggers' World Atlas series (which also covered Flickr and Instagram photos) showed that user-generated geotagged data is a rich proxy for urban activity patterns. The methodology influenced social media analytics platforms and urban planning researchers who wanted cheap proxies for foot traffic and activity intensity without deploying physical sensors.

## Key points

- Geotagged tweet density as a proxy for urban activity intensity — crowd-sourced sensor data
- Correlates with income, nightlife activity, tourist areas, and commercial density — not just raw population
- Eric Fischer was among the first to systematically mine Twitter and Flickr geotagged data at city scale
- Methodology: collect via streaming API → bin to grid → visualize as heatmap
- Reveals digital divide within cities: low-income neighborhoods appear darker even with high population density
- Inspired urban planning and public health applications — geotagged social media as a free behavioral dataset

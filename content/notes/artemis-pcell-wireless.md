## Summary

Artemis Networks (founded by Steve Perlman, previously of OnLive and Mova) claimed in 2014 to have developed pCell — a wireless technology that inverts the fundamental constraint of cellular networking. Traditional cellular networks divide spectrum into cells; users within a cell share bandwidth, and interference between signals is the enemy. pCell claimed to use interference *constructively* — treating overlapping radio waves as the signal rather than noise.

The technical claim: by precisely controlling the phase and timing of signals from multiple transmitters, pCell creates a personal cell (a pCell) around each device, delivering the full theoretical bandwidth of the spectrum directly to that device rather than sharing it across users. The physics draws on distributed MIMO and beamforming principles.

The reception in 2014 was skeptical from the wireless engineering community. The demo showed impressive data rates, but critics questioned whether the technology would scale outside controlled lab conditions, and whether the computational overhead of per-device beam synthesis was feasible at network scale. Artemis has remained in limited deployment, suggesting the commercial scaling challenge was not trivial.

## Key points

- pCell: Artemis Networks' claimed wireless breakthrough — uses interference constructively via precise phase control.
- Inversion of cellular model: instead of sharing spectrum per cell, delivers full bandwidth per device.
- Technical basis: distributed MIMO + beamforming — requires coordinated transmitters with precise timing.
- Founder: Steve Perlman — serial entrepreneur (OnLive cloud gaming, Mova facial performance capture).
- Significant 2014 press skepticism from wireless engineers about real-world scaling and computational feasibility.
- Never achieved mainstream deployment — illustrates the gap between lab performance claims and commercial wireless engineering.

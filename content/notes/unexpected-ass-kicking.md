## Summary

Joel Runyon's widely-shared essay about a chance conversation with an older, successful man that completely reframed what Runyon thought was possible in his own life. The piece resonated strongly in 2012 because it articulated something many ambitious people recognize but rarely hear stated directly: that inaction and half-measures are choices with real costs, and that most people settle for far less than they're capable of achieving.

The narrative: Runyon met someone unexpectedly who, rather than offering empty encouragement, cut through to a harder truth — that Runyon's results were exactly proportional to his commitment, and that real change requires an uncomfortable level of full commitment rather than the tentative efforts that feel safe. The essay framed this as an ass kicking because it was delivered bluntly, without the softening that most self-help content uses to protect the reader's comfort.

The piece is part of a genre of deliberate practice and growth mindset content that was popular in 2011–2013 (alongside Tim Ferriss's 4-Hour Workweek, Seth Godin's work on shipping and resistance, and Steven Pressfield's The War of Art). What distinguished Runyon's piece was the specificity of the encounter and his willingness to be honest about the gap between his own aspirations and his actual actions. The lesson about commitment — that the difference between success and failure is often simply whether you've fully committed — connects to Derek Sivers's concept of hell yeah or no and Cal Newport's writing on deep work.

## Key points

- Results are proportional to commitment — tentative efforts produce tentative results.
- The comfort of I'm working on it can be a substitute for actually shipping or committing.
- Full commitment is uncomfortable because it eliminates excuses — you either succeed or you fully failed.
- Connects to The War of Art (Steven Pressfield) on Resistance as the force that prevents real commitment.
- Deliberate practice and deep work require the kind of full commitment the essay describes.
- The "ass kicking" framing is memorable because it's honest about the gap between aspiration and action.

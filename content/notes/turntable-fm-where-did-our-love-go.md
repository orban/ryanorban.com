## Summary

Turntable.fm launched in May 2011 and within weeks had become the hottest product in Silicon Valley. The concept was simple and novel: virtual rooms where people took turns DJing for each other using shared music libraries, with cartoon avatars nodding along or booing. It was social listening as a game, and the tech community adopted it with unusual enthusiasm — teams held listening parties, people stayed on for hours, and DJ culture for a moment looked like it might translate perfectly to the web.

The founders — Seth Goldstein and Billy Chasen — had previously built stickybits, a QR code social network that didn't find its audience. Turntable.fm felt like vindication: product-market fit so strong it created its own vocabulary (dropping bops and womp womps). By the time SXSW 2012 came around, though, the initial fever had broken. Early adopters had cycled through the novelty. Casual users hadn't stuck. The piece examined what happens after a product's viral moment.

The structural challenge: Turntable.fm required multiple people to be in the same room at the same time. Synchronous social products are more engaging when they work, but they have an inherent cold start problem — a room with no DJs is useless, so density matters enormously. The platform that felt effortlessly alive when the tech early adopter crowd was all using it felt empty as that cohort's attention moved on.

## Key points

- Turntable.fm was a synchronous social music platform — DJs and listeners had to be present at the same time.
- The cold start problem hit hard: the product only worked when rooms had active DJs and listeners.
- Early adopters drove the initial viral moment; when they moved on, the product felt empty to new users.
- The founders had to negotiate complex music licensing with the major labels — a recurring obstacle for music startups.
- Turntable.fm eventually shut down in December 2013, unable to crack mainstream retention after the tech-crowd novelty wore off.
- Lesson: products that require coordination among users are harder to sustain than solo-use products.

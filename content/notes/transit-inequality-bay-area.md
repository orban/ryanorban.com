## Summary

Dan Grover's interactive visualization examined the relationship between income, neighborhood, and public transit access across the San Francisco Bay Area. The central finding: transit investment and quality are highly correlated with neighborhood income — wealthier neighborhoods near downtown have excellent BART and Muni access, while lower-income areas in the East Bay, South Bay, and outer neighborhoods of San Francisco have sparse, slow service that requires long walks and multiple transfers.

This was part of a wave of civic data visualization projects in 2012-2014 that used newly available government datasets (GTFS transit feeds, Census income data) combined with D3.js to make abstract policy problems visually concrete. The transit equity argument — that inadequate public transit is both a cause and symptom of economic inequality — was gaining traction as tech industry growth was reshaping the Bay Area's demographics and housing costs.

The project is also a case study in the power of geographic visualization: overlaying transit access scores on income maps reveals patterns that tables of statistics obscure. You can see the literal spatial boundary between areas served by fast-frequent service and areas served by irregular buses. The visual makes the policy argument more viscerally than any written analysis.

## Key points

- BART and premium Muni routes primarily serve corridors that are already economically advantaged — reinforcing spatial inequality
- Transit access measured as a composite: service frequency, walking distance to stops, coverage of job centers during commute hours
- GTFS (General Transit Feed Specification) made this analysis possible — machine-readable transit schedules opened up transit data to civic technologists
- The tech bus phenomenon was contemporaneous: private shuttle buses serving tech workers were beginning to use Muni stops, triggering the political conflict that defined Bay Area transit equity discourse through the 2010s
- A 2013-era example of civic data journalism — using open government data and D3.js for public-interest investigation rather than commercial analytics
- Related to subsequent work on 15-minute city planning and transit-oriented development advocacy

## Summary

Data Science Central maintained a curated book list for practitioners entering or deepening their data science knowledge. The page aggregated community recommendations spanning statistics, machine learning, Python programming, R, and big data infrastructure — the full stack of skills that data scientist increasingly implied in 2013.

The context: 2013 was the year data science formalized as a job category. Harvard Business Review's "Sexiest Job of the 21st Century" article had run the previous year, and the question of how to become a data scientist was live. Book lists like this served self-directed learners without formal CS or statistics backgrounds — the pathway into the field for many practitioners.

A typical list from this era would include: The Elements of Statistical Learning (Hastie, Tibshirani, Friedman — free online from Stanford), Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning (Bishop), Programming Collective Intelligence (Segaran), Data Analysis with Open Source Tools (Janert), and the then-emerging Python data stack books covering NumPy, SciPy, and pandas. The combination of rigorous statistics texts and practical programming books reflects the dual requirement of the role.

## Key points

- Data Science Central (Vincent Granville): one of the first dedicated data science community platforms, aggregating articles, job listings, and educational resources.
- The 2013 data science book canon was split: rigorous ML theory (ESL, Bishop) vs practical programming guides (Python, R, Hadoop).
- Self-directed learning was the dominant pathway into data science in 2013 — formal data science degrees didn't yet exist at most universities.
- Free Stanford resources (ESL, [Mining of Massive Datasets](/notes/mining-of-massive-datasets/)) alongside O'Reilly practical books defined the curriculum.
- The reading list has evolved significantly since: [fast.ai](/notes/fastai/), deeplearning.ai, and Andrej Karpathy's materials now dominate the practical learning path.

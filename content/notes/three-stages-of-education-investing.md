## Summary

This essay distinguishes three stages of education by when in life they occur and argues that investment attention is grotesquely concentrated on the first stage (K-12 and university) while the second and third stages are both larger in economic impact and almost entirely ignored.

The three stages roughly map to: (1) **foundational** — K-12 and higher education, heavily funded by EdTech investors; (2) **professional development** — early-career upskilling, partially addressed by platforms like Coursera and Udacity; (3) **reskilling/retraining** — mid-career workers displaced by automation, essentially unaddressed in 2015. The essay's argument is that stage 3 is both the hardest problem and the one with the greatest social and economic urgency — but it requires very different product design and business models than stage 1.

The context for this bookmark is Galvanize and Zipfian Academy: coding bootcamps were stage 2 at best, targeting young people making career transitions rather than 45-year-old factory workers needing retraining. The essay implicitly challenges whether the bootcamp model scales to the reskilling problem, or whether something fundamentally different is needed. It's also a commentary on how automation risk concentrates in mid-career workers who don't fit the university model but also don't fit the learn to code pipeline.

## Key points

- Stage 1 (K-12, university) gets most EdTech investment but isn't where the biggest displacement is happening.
- Stage 3 (reskilling displaced mid-career workers) is largest in scale and nearly unaddressed by investors.
- Bootcamps address stage 2 but don't serve the automation-displaced mid-career worker.
- Business model challenge: mid-career reskilling requires different incentive structures than consumer EdTech.
- Written in 2015 — the reskilling problem has only grown since, amplified by AI automation.

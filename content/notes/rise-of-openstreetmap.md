## Summary

By 2014, OpenStreetMap had crossed the threshold from hobbyist project to credible infrastructure. Major tech companies — including Foursquare, Apple, and Mapbox — were either using OSM data or had switched to it from Google Maps. The article documents how this happened: a decade of volunteer contributions, a 2010 license change to the Open Database License (ODbL) that made commercial use unambiguous, and the growth of companies like Mapbox that offered professional tooling and APIs on top of the OSM dataset.

The comparison to Wikipedia is apt and often made: both are crowd-sourced datasets where the community's collective edits produce something more comprehensive than any single organization could maintain. OpenStreetMap's data model (nodes, ways, relations with arbitrary key-value tags) is flexible enough to capture everything from road geometry to individual shop hours to hiking trail conditions. Local knowledge is hard to buy — volunteer mappers who live in an area know it better than any satellite imagery analysis could reveal.

Mapbox was the key commercial accelerator: a company that took OSM data and built professional-grade map tiles, styling tools, and APIs on top. Their business model demonstrated that OSM's open license didn't preclude commercial value-add — it just moved the monetization from data to services. This became a template for other open data ecosystems.

## Key points

- OpenStreetMap hit production quality for major commercial use by 2014 — Foursquare and Apple switching validated data reliability.
- Mapbox built the commercial API layer on top of OSM's open dataset — showed that data open-sourcing plus service monetization works.
- ODbL license (2010): replaced the Creative Commons license to clarify that derived databases must also be open — enabling commercial use while preserving openness.
- Volunteer contribution model: local knowledge advantage over automated methods — street name changes, new developments, business hours.
- Geospatial data as public infrastructure: the argument that basic map data shouldn't be proprietary given its civic importance.

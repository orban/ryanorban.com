## Summary

This Harvard Business Review piece by Kristian Hammond argues against the fetishization of data accumulation that dominated big data discourse in 2013. The prevailing narrative — collect everything, answers will emerge — was treating data as inherently valuable rather than as a means to an end. Hammond's counter: data is only valuable insofar as it enables better decisions, predictions, or actions. Without the right questions and the capacity to act on answers, more data is just more cost.

The piece was early in articulating what became a more common critique: that data science hype was inflating the importance of data acquisition and storage (where vendors had products to sell) while underemphasizing the harder work of analysis, modeling, and organizational change required to actually use data. A company with petabytes of logs and no analysts asking good questions has less value from data than a company with gigabytes and a clear analytical agenda.

Hammond was also gesturing at the distinction between descriptive analytics (what happened), predictive analytics (what will happen), and prescriptive analytics (what should we do) — and arguing that most big data projects were stuck at the descriptive level while claiming prescriptive value. This framework became foundational in data strategy conversations.

## Key points

- Data without questions and actions has no value — the data science leverage point is decision quality, not data volume
- Treating data accumulation as a strategy conflates the tool with the outcome
- The real investment needed: analysts who ask good questions, engineers who build pipelines that enable action, processes that close the loop between insight and decision
- Machine learning models trained on more data do improve — but only when the model architecture and features are right; raw volume alone rarely saves a bad problem formulation
- This argument prefigures later critiques of data moats as a competitive strategy — data advantages erode when the data becomes commoditized or when competitors build better models on less data
- Historically important as pushback against the data as the new oil framing that dominated 2012-2014

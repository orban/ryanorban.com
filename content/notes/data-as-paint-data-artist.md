## Summary

This piece proposed a metaphor that circulated widely in 2013 data circles: data is to the data scientist what paint is to the painter — a raw material that can be transformed into something expressive, not just something informative. The data artist framing extended beyond pure data visualization (making charts and graphs) to suggest that working with data could be genuinely creative: finding unexpected patterns, telling stories through structure, making aesthetic choices about what to emphasize and what to hide.

The argument sat at the tension between the data science framing (which emphasized rigor, statistics, and prediction) and the data journalism / infographics world (which emphasized communication and storytelling). Neither fully captured the practitioners who were doing something more like exploration and expression — using datasets to discover things that had no clear analytic question attached, making art from data structures, or treating the process of working with data as intrinsically interesting rather than instrumentally useful.

By 2013, tools like D3.js, Processing, and R's ggplot2 had lowered the barrier to expressive data visualization enough that artists who weren't statisticians could work meaningfully with data. The data artist concept recognized this cross-disciplinary space and named it — similar to how the term information design gave language to the discipline that sat between graphic design and statistical communication.

## Key points

- Data as creative medium: the data as paint metaphor positions datasets as raw material for expression, not just evidence for claims.
- Data artist as a distinct role from data scientist: the former prioritizes expression and exploration, the latter prioritizes rigor and prediction.
- Tooling democratization: D3.js, Processing, R/ggplot2 made expressive data work accessible to people without statistics training.
- Data journalism connection: outlets like The New York Times Graphics and The Guardian Data Blog were blurring the line between analysis and art.
- The metaphor has limits — data without truth isn't paint, it's propaganda — but names something real about exploratory, non-hypothesis-driven data work.

## Summary

Blake Masters' notes from Peter Thiel's Stanford startup course (CS183) cover one of Thiel's central strategic ideas: the goal isn't to be a first mover, it's to be a last mover — the company that makes the decisive, hard-to-copy breakthrough and then maintains that position indefinitely. The first mover advantage is usually temporary; competitors copy the product and compete on price. The last mover advantage is the durable form: you enter the market, achieve something no one else can replicate, and the category is yours.

Thiel's framework distinguishes between perfect competition and monopoly as the two poles of market structure. Perfect competition is the econ-textbook ideal — many competitors, zero economic profit, constant innovation to stay afloat. Monopoly, by contrast, is what great companies actually aim for: a position where there are no close substitutes and profit can compound indefinitely. His controversial claim is that all great businesses are, to varying degrees, monopolies, and that the social stigma around monopoly is a mistake.

The practical question the class addresses is how you build a monopoly legitimately: through proprietary technology (10x better than alternatives is the threshold he uses), network effects (each additional user makes the product more valuable — rare but powerful when it works), economies of scale, and brand (harder to copy than product features). The last mover framing is about time horizon: you want to still be winning in 10-20 years, not just next quarter.

## Key points

- First mover advantage is usually temporary; last mover advantage is about building a position competitors can't replicate.
- Peter Thiel draws a sharp line between perfect competition (economic death by a thousand cuts) and monopoly (the only viable destination for a great company).
- The four sources of durable competitive advantage: proprietary technology, network effects, economies of scale, brand.
- 10x better than the best alternative is Thiel's rule of thumb for proprietary technology — incremental improvements get copied; 10x improvements create new categories.
- CS183 was spring 2012 at Stanford; Blake Masters' notes were eventually published as Zero to One (2014).

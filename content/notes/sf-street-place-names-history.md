## Summary

Noah Veltman's interactive San Francisco street names project mapped the historical origins of SF streets, neighborhoods, and landmarks — connecting each name to its historical source through an annotated, clickable map. The project drew on local history research to explain names that had become opaque with time: why is there a Dolores Street, a Brannan Street, a Rincon Hill, a Potrero Avenue. The answers trace San Francisco's history from Ohlone place names through Spanish land grants, Gold Rush merchant and developer names, and the political decisions of the early city government.

Noah Veltman was a data journalist at WNYC who built civic data tools as a side project in this period. The SF streets project is an example of local history made navigable through data visualization — the kind of project that requires combining historical research with geographic data and web development, and that no commercial entity would fund but that a motivated individual could complete.

The project revealed patterns invisible from street-level experience: the grid of early Mission District streets was named by a Spanish-land-grant system that's visible in the clustering of Spanish names in that neighborhood. The Financial District concentration of surnames reflects the Gold Rush merchants who developed those blocks. Golden Gate Park and its internal roads have a different naming logic than the street grid — a designed space with intentional naming vs. the organic accumulation of the city's streets.

## Key points

- Spanish land grant era names cluster in the Mission and around old rancho boundaries — Guerrero, Valencia, Dolores trace the Misión San Francisco de Asís land claims
- Gold Rush merchant names dominate the Financial District: Brannan, Folsom, Howard, Tehama were all developers who bought and subdivided land in the 1850s
- Ohlone place names survive mostly in reused Spanish transliterations — the original names were obscured through the Spanish and then American periods
- The project is built on open data: the SF Open Data Portal provided the street centerlines, historical research came from books and the California Historical Society
- Civic data visualization pattern: combining open government data with domain research — the data layer is the substrate, not the story
- Related projects: Atlas of San Francisco by Stamen Design, OpenStreetMap historical annotation efforts

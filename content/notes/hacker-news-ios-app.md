## Summary

The Next Web reviewed a Hacker News iOS app that took strong design inspiration from Instapaper — the read-later service by Marco Arment that had set the standard for clean, typography-focused reading apps. The Instapaper influence meant: minimal chrome, comfortable text rendering, offline reading capability, and focus on the reading experience rather than on community features or gamification.

The Hacker News audience and iOS app design were a natural combination: the same tech-focused community that read HN also paid for quality software, had strong opinions about typography and UX, and used iPhones at high rates. Several HN apps competed in 2012 — Hacker News by Reeder, early versions of what became Apollo-style readers, and others — all trying to make the notoriously bare-bones HN interface into something pleasant to read on mobile.

The Instapaper design language — white background, serif text, minimal UI — was particularly influential on iOS reading apps from 2010-2014. Marco Arment had found a design vocabulary that made long-form reading comfortable on a small screen. Apps that adopted it (Pocket, Reeder, various HN clients) got credit for being thoughtful about the medium. The deeper insight was that mobile news reading was a distinct context — not just "the web on a smaller screen" — and required its own design conventions.

## Key points

- Instapaper by Marco Arment set the reading-app design standard: minimal chrome, clean typography, offline support.
- Hacker News has always had a bare-bones HTML interface by design (Lisp hacker aesthetic).
- Third-party HN apps filled the gap — multiple competing clients, differentiating on design and feature set.
- iOS app quality became a cultural signal in 2012 — polished iOS apps got disproportionate press coverage.
- Marco Arment sold Instapaper to Betaworks in 2013; Pocket (formerly Read It Later) was the main competitor.

## Summary

Here Is Today is a simple interactive data visualization that uses a single design technique — progressive zooming out through timescales — to make geological and cosmic time comprehensible. You start with today highlighted on a bar. The bar zooms out to show the current month. Then the year. Then your lifetime. Then human history. Then civilizations. Then the age of humans. Then mammals. Then life on Earth. Then the age of the Earth. Then the age of the universe. Each step dwarfs the previous.

The design insight is that comparing directly across scales (placing "today" next to age of the universe) produces incomprehension rather than understanding — the numbers are too large to be meaningful. Progressive reveal through intermediate scales gives each comparison a cognitive anchor. You understand each ratio before the next one is introduced, building an intuition for the full scale chain.

This is a case study in what Edward Tufte called small multiples taken to its logical extreme: the same format (highlighted bar segment) repeated at every scale, ensuring the encoding is learned once and applied consistently. The simplicity — no labels until you hover, no numbers, just proportional bars — means the design is the message. The tool was created by Luke Twyman and became widely shared as a perspective design example.

## Key points

- **Progressive scale reveal** as a visualization technique: each zoom-out anchors the next ratio, preventing the cognitive overload of direct cross-scale comparison
- Small multiples principle: same encoding repeated — the viewer learns to read it once, then sees the pattern across all instances
- The scale visualization genre: Powers of Ten (Eames, 1977) pioneered the continuous zoom approach; Here Is Today applies it as a web-native interactive
- Minimalism as design choice: no numbers, no labels, just proportional bars — removes any ambiguity about what the visualization is saying
- Applicable insight for data presentation: when showing quantities that span many orders of magnitude, progressive contextualization outperforms direct comparison
- Related works: The Scale of the Universe (interactive), Kurzgesagt's time videos — the same pedagogical problem of making large numbers visceral

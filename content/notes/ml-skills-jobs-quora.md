## Summary

This Quora thread asked what skills were required for machine learning jobs in 2013 — a period when data scientist was newly crowned "sexiest job of the 21st century" and companies were scrambling to hire people who could do statistical modeling at scale. The answers reflected a mix of academic ML background (probability, linear algebra, calculus) and practical engineering skills (Python, R, SQL, Hadoop).

The 2013 ML job landscape was notably different from today. Most companies didn't have dedicated ML engineer roles — the same person was expected to wrangle data, build features, train models, and deploy them. scikit-learn was still maturing, deep learning was just beginning its rise (AlexNet won ImageNet in 2012), and Hadoop was the dominant way to handle large datasets. The SKILL set that got you hired was a mix of statistics, Python or R proficiency, and MapReduce fundamentals.

Strong Quora answers in this era typically emphasized that machine learning jobs required being comfortable with ambiguity — understanding which algorithm to apply, how to frame a business problem as an ML task, and how to evaluate whether a model was actually useful. Domain knowledge (knowing what questions are worth asking) was considered as important as technical facility.

## Key points

- Core ML skills in 2013: linear regression, logistic regression, decision trees, SVMs, k-means, naive Bayes — the scikit-learn standard library.
- Math requirements: probability theory, statistics, linear algebra, optimization (gradient descent) — the foundations that don't change.
- Engineering requirements: Python or R, SQL, Hadoop familiarity — the T-shaped practitioner who could work across the stack.
- Feature engineering was considered the real differentiator: building good features from raw data mattered more than model choice.
- 2013 was pre-deep learning dominance — neural networks appeared on lists but weren't the first tool most practitioners reached for.
- Jeremy Howard, Andrew Ng, and the Stanford CS229 course were frequently cited as starting points.

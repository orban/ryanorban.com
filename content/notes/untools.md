## Summary

[Untools](/notes/untools/) is a clean, curated reference site collecting thinking frameworks and mental models for problem solving, decision making, and understanding systems. Each tool gets a concise explanation, a diagram where useful, and practical guidance on when to reach for it. The emphasis is on breadth (covering many frameworks) while keeping each entry short enough to actually read — unlike the 200 mental models lists that become unreadable.

The frameworks span several categories: systems thinking tools (causal loop diagrams, stocks and flows, feedback loops), decision-making frameworks ([second-order thinking](/notes/second-order-thinking/), inversion, opportunity cost, regret minimization), problem-solving approaches (first principles thinking, issue trees, MECE principle), and communication/argument structure (pyramid principle, Minto pyramid). Many are drawn from consulting (McKinsey, BCG) frameworks but stripped of their corporate presentation veneer.

The curation is the value. Individual frameworks like [second-order thinking](/notes/second-order-thinking/) (what are the consequences of the consequences?) or inversion (what would make this project fail? now avoid those things) are well-documented elsewhere. Having them in one browsable collection with practical examples makes it easier to reach for the right tool at the right moment rather than having to recall which book the framework was in. [Untools](/notes/untools/) positions itself as an intellectual toolbox you visit when stuck rather than a course you work through linearly.

## Key points

- [Untools](/notes/untools/): browsable collection of thinking frameworks — each with a concise explanation and practical when to use it
- Categories: systems thinking, decision-making, problem solving, communication/argument structure
- [Second-order thinking](/notes/second-order-thinking/): ask what the consequences of the consequences are — avoids solutions that create bigger downstream problems
- Inversion: reframe as what would cause failure? then avoid those — often easier than reasoning forward to success
- MECE principle (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive): from consulting; useful for ensuring problem decompositions don't overlap or miss cases
- Related: Farnam Street's mental models library, The Great Mental Models book series

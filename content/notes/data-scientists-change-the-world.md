## Summary

This GigaOm piece from 2013 profiled data scientists who had honed their skills on ad targeting and recommendation systems and were turning those techniques toward social impact: predicting disease outbreaks, optimizing food bank logistics, identifying at-risk students, and improving government services. The framing captures a real moment of idealism at the peak of the data science hype cycle.

The techniques involved were largely the same as commercial applications: logistic regression, random forests, clustering, and A/B testing — applied to different domains with different cost structures. The key difference is what counts as a successful outcome. Ad targeting optimizes for click rate; predicting at-risk students requires very high precision (false positives have real human costs). The domain shift forces rethinking which metrics matter.

Organizations like DataKind (founded 2011) and DrivenData formalized this as "data science for social good" — volunteer data scientists working on nonprofit and government datasets on a pro-bono basis. The movement has grown since 2013 but the fundamental challenges remain: messy public datasets, privacy constraints, and the difficulty of defining outcomes in domains where success is harder to measure than more clicks.

## Key points

- Ad targeting → social good: same techniques (random forests, collaborative filtering), different objectives
- DataKind and Data Science for Social Good as organizational forms for applying data science to nonprofits
- Healthcare: predicting hospital readmissions, disease outbreak detection using search query trends
- Education: predicting student dropout risk from behavioral signals in learning management systems
- Precision/recall trade-offs change radically when false positives have human costs
- The challenge: public sector data is messier, more privacy-constrained, and harder to label than commercial data

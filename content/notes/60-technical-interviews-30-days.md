## Summary

Emmanuel Okafor (meekg33k) ran a deliberate experiment: he completed more than 60 technical interviews in a single month, treating the process as a practice regimen rather than a job search. The resulting essay synthesizes 13 lessons across the three phases of an interview: preparation, execution, and post-interview reflection.

The key findings from volume: **preparation phase** — most candidates under-prepare on behavioral interview questions relative to data structures and algorithms. The STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioral questions is well-known but rarely practiced to the same depth as algorithmic questions. **During the interview** — communication matters more than most candidates assume. Interviewers are evaluating can I work with this person? as much as can they solve this problem? Thinking aloud, asking clarifying questions, and checking in on your approach before coding are all evaluated positively. **Post-interview** — consistent debrief immediately after each interview (what question, how you approached it, what you should have done differently) is the fastest way to improve — but almost no one does it systematically.

The volume data is unusual and valuable: most interview advice comes from a handful of experiences. 60+ interviews across 30 days creates a statistically meaningful sample from which real patterns emerge — which questions appear repeatedly (linked lists, trees, dynamic programming), which soft-SKILL behaviors differentiate candidates, and what rejection patterns look like vs. success patterns.

## Key points

- Behavioral interview preparation is systematically under-invested relative to algorithms.
- Communication during coding: think aloud, clarify the problem, confirm approach before coding — evaluated as a signal.
- Systematic post-interview debrief (immediate, written) accelerates improvement dramatically.
- Volume of interviews creates pattern recognition that 5-10 interviews can't — repeat question types emerge.
- Data structures and algorithms: trees and graphs appear disproportionately; dynamic programming is the hardest but common at top companies.

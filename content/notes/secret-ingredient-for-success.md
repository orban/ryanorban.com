## Summary

This New York Times Sunday Review piece (by Kate Feiler Kaufman and Richard Kaufman) argues that the distinguishing factor in high achievers isn't raw talent, IQ, or early advantage, but a specific psychological trait: the willingness to confront uncomfortable feedback about oneself and update accordingly. The secret ingredient is ruthless self-awareness paired with the emotional resilience to use critical information rather than deflect it.

The piece draws on research from Carol Dweck's growth mindset work and adjacent research on psychological flexibility — the capacity to engage with difficult information about yourself without becoming defensive. People with this trait seek out criticism, take it seriously, and update their behavior; people without it rationalize failure and repeat it. This creates a compounding advantage over time: the feedback-seekers improve faster, and improvement creates confidence that makes further feedback-seeking easier.

The specific claim about high achievers is that they've learned to decouple their self-worth from their performance — good enough at their own internal scorecard that external criticism doesn't feel existential. This is similar to Aaron Swartz's "Raw Nerve" essays on intellectual honesty: the capacity to hear you're wrong without it meaning you're bad.

## Key points

- Self-awareness and feedback integration compound over time — small initial advantages in processing failure create large long-term SKILL differences
- Growth mindset (Dweck): belief that abilities can be developed through dedication changes how people respond to difficulty
- Decoupling self-worth from performance is a prerequisite for learning from failure — hard to do, learnable
- High achievers often describe a specific moment when they learned to sit with difficult feedback rather than explaining it away
- The secret framing is populist, but the underlying research (Dweck, Seligman on learned optimism) is solid

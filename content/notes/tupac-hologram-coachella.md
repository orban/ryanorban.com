## Summary

The April 2012 Coachella performance featuring a projected "Tupac hologram" was a cultural watershed moment for discussions about digital resurrection and the posthumous use of deceased performers' likenesses. Dr. Dre and Snoop Dogg were performing when a convincing projection of Tupac Shakur appeared on stage, performed two songs, and then dissolved into vapor — generating enormous online buzz and mainstream media coverage.

The technology involved was not actually a hologram in any physical sense. It used Pepper's ghost, a Victorian stage illusion technique from 1862, where a transparent screen reflects an image from below stage. What was genuinely impressive was the CGI quality and the motion capture work done by AV Concepts and Digital Domain to create a convincing moving image. The illusion fooled most viewers who hadn't studied stage magic.

The cultural conversation it kicked off was more significant than the tech. Questions about whether deceased artists could be brought back for tours, whether estates should be able to commercialize a performer's likeness indefinitely, and whether audiences would accept or resist resurrections — all of these accelerated after Coachella 2012. It presaged later debates about deepfakes and AI-generated likenesses of real people by almost a decade.

## Key points

- The hologram was actually Pepper's ghost — a 19th-century illusion updated with high-quality CGI projection.
- Created by AV Concepts and Digital Domain at the direction of Dr. Dre — cost reportedly around $400,000.
- First major mainstream moment for digital resurrection discourse: could estates tour hologram versions of deceased artists?
- The mislabeling as "hologram" stuck — the same incorrect term is still used for similar stage effects today.
- Preceded the deepfake era by years, but raised the same fundamental questions about consent and identity.

## Summary

Matt Swanson makes a tight argument about language and self-honesty: I don't have time is almost never literally true. You have the same 24 hours as everyone else. What you mean is "I don't prioritize this enough to do it" — but that framing is uncomfortable, so the time excuse substitutes. Swanson's point is that accepting the honest version is actually more useful: it forces you to acknowledge that time allocation is a choice, not a constraint.

The practical implication is about clarity in decision-making. When you tell yourself "I don't have time to work on that side project, the honest version is I'm choosing to spend evenings on other things instead." That reframe surfaces the actual trade-off. Maybe the other things are genuinely more important — in which case you've made a clear decision. Maybe they're not — in which case the false framing was letting you avoid making a decision at all.

This connects to broader writing on prioritization: the most useful things you can do are often the ones you're procrastinating on with time excuses. The excuse is a symptom of avoidance, and "I don't have time" is an especially durable form because it attributes the problem to external constraint rather than internal choice. Time blocking and commitment devices can help, but only after you've accepted that the real issue is priorities.

## Key points

- "I don't have time" almost always means I don't prioritize this — the honest version is more useful.
- Time is a fixed resource but its allocation is a choice; the framing matters for making clear decisions.
- The false time excuse lets you avoid acknowledging trade-offs — accepting the honest version forces the decision into the open.
- Useful diagnostic: wherever you regularly say I don't have time for X, ask whether X is actually lower priority or whether you're avoiding commitment.

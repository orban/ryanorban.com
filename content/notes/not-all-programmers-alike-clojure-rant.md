## Summary

Scott Locklin published this essay in 2012 making the uncomfortable argument that programmer aptitude is not uniformly distributed and that language communities self-select. His specific target: the Clojure and Lisp communities' tendency to believe that their language preference reflects superior intellect and that everyone should be writing functional, parenthesized code. His counter: most software gets written by programmers who think concretely, iteratively, and imperatively — and that's fine, because most software problems don't require the abstraction that Lisp enables.

The underlying claim is that programming languages and their communities tend to attract and retain different cognitive types, and that Lisp/Clojure communities over-index on abstraction-lovers and theorists while underrepresenting the population of programmers who build reliable systems through careful, concrete, incremental work. This isn't an anti-Lisp argument per se — Locklin was a competent Lisp user — it's an argument against the elitism that sometimes accompanies language preference.

The Hacker News discussion (saved alongside the original) amplified and contested the argument. The piece touched something real: the culture of programming language tribalism, the way type systems wars, functional programming vs. OOP debates, and Vim vs. Emacs arguments often masked aesthetic preferences as technical claims. Paul Graham's essays on Lisp were the cultural background — influential but responsible for some of the attitude Locklin was criticizing.

## Key points

- Locklin's argument: programmers have different cognitive styles, and Lisp/Clojure communities mistake a style preference for a competence hierarchy
- Language tribalism recasts aesthetic or cognitive preferences as technical superiority — a form of in-group signaling
- Clojure: JVM-hosted Lisp by Rich Hickey — genuinely well-designed, but its community in 2012 had a reputation for elitism
- Paul Graham's Lisp essays created a cultural frame where Lisp = intelligence — Locklin was explicitly pushing back on that frame
- The meta-point: diversity of programmer type and approach is the actual strength of the field, not a deficiency to be corrected by moving everyone to functional languages

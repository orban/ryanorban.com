## Summary

This guide by Douglas Van Bossuyt covers practical radio communications for off-road and overlanding contexts — the problem of staying in contact when you're in remote areas without cellular coverage. It walks through the major radio categories: FRS/GMRS (short-range handheld radios that don't require a license, or GMRS requiring a simple license), VHF/UHF handheld radios for longer range, CB radio (still common in trucking), and ham radio which offers the most capability but requires an FCC license.

The guide is notable for being pragmatic rather than hobbyist — it's oriented toward people who want reliable communication, not radio enthusiasts who enjoy the hobby for its own sake. It covers satellite communicators like Garmin inReach and SPOT for truly remote areas where even ham radio won't reliably reach others. Each option is assessed for cost, range, license requirements, and ease of use.

The practical framing is useful for understanding the tradeoffs: GMRS is the right call for a group of vehicles traveling together within a few miles; ham radio is necessary for longer-range emergency communication; satellite communicators are the fallback when no terrestrial option works. The guide also covers antennas — mobile antennas mounted on vehicles dramatically improve range over handheld units.

## Key points

- FRS/GMRS: short-range (1-5 miles), no license (FRS) or simple license (GMRS), good for convoy comms.
- Ham radio (amateur radio): most capable, longest range, requires FCC license (Technician level).
- Satellite communicators (Garmin inReach, SPOT): work anywhere with sky view, subscription-based, for true backcountry emergencies.
- Mobile antennas dramatically extend range over handheld units.
- CB radio is still used on highways and for communication with trucks.

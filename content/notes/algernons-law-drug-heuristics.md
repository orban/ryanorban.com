## Summary

Algernon's Law is a heuristic formalized by Gwern Branwen on his website: if a drug that safely and reliably improved cognitive function existed, evolution would have already selected for the biological state that drug induces. The name references the mouse in the novel *Flowers for Algernon* — whose engineered intelligence boost eventually reverses, reflecting the idea that dramatic cognitive enhancement has costs that balanced selection over millions of years would have already optimized away.

The underlying reasoning is a form of evolutionary debunking argument. Human cognition has been under strong selection pressure for hundreds of thousands of years. If a simple chemical change could significantly and safely improve general intelligence or cognitive performance, that change would likely already have been favored by selection — either by dietary means, developmental timing, or metabolic pathway adjustment. The fact that we haven't naturally converged on this state suggests either: (a) the benefit is real but the cost is significant (the tradeoff is unfavorable), (b) the benefit is smaller than claimed, or (c) the drug is exploiting an environment-specific mechanism that evolution hadn't yet encountered.

This provides a useful prior for evaluating nootropics and cognitive enhancement claims. Most proposed cognitive enhancers either work primarily in deficit states (restoring impaired function rather than enhancing normal function), have significant side effects or dependency risks that show up on longer timescales, or show effects in self-reported studies that don't replicate under controlled conditions. The law doesn't rule out all cognitive enhancement — it suggests skepticism is warranted and that any genuine enhancement likely has a corresponding cost. Gwern's broader pharmacology writing applies this reasoning rigorously to specific compounds like modafinil, piracetam, and caffeine.

## Key points

- Algernon's Law: safe, effective cognitive enhancement would have been selected by evolution already — strong prior against dramatic nootropic claims.
- The argument: evolution has had hundreds of thousands of years to optimize human cognition; easily-accessible improvements would already be built in.
- Exceptions: compounds that work primarily in deficit states (restoring impaired function), or that exploit novel environmental inputs evolution hadn't encountered.
- Applies to: most nootropics, cognitive enhancers, and smart drugs — a skeptical prior grounded in evolutionary biology rather than empirical dismissal.
- Gwern Branwen's broader work applies this heuristic systematically to specific compounds: modafinil (works in sleep-deprived states), caffeine (genuine but modest effect, clear dependency mechanism).
- Named after the mouse in *Flowers for Algernon* — symbolic of artificial enhancement with hidden costs.

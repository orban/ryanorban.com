## Summary

Ben Kuhn makes a case that one-on-ones are far more valuable than their reputation suggests, and that their value comes from something non-obvious: not specialized knowledge, but genuine care and the forcing function of regular accountability. He discovered this when running weekly check-ins with his partner during her PhD — helping her complete the dissertation roughly a year faster than she would have otherwise.

The mechanism isn't complicated. A regular one-on-one forces you to surface what you're actually working on and whether it connects to longer-term goals. The facilitator asks questions a specialist might not: why are you procrastinating on this? what feedback are you not seeking? what difficult conversation are you avoiding? These aren't domain questions — they're meta-level questions about how you're working, and most people rarely get to answer them out loud.

What separates effective one-on-ones from accountability partnerships is that "Eve's progress was the top idea in my mind" — the facilitator is genuinely invested. That care produces sharper questions, more personalized guidance, and willingness to push on uncomfortable topics. The same structure could benefit PhD programs, early-career engineers, and anyone doing independent work where direction-setting is hard.

## Key points

- One-on-ones work primarily as a forcing function to clarify goals and surface what's blocking progress — not as a source of domain expertise.
- Genuine care from the facilitator matters more than specialized knowledge: the best 1:1 partner has your progress as their top idea.
- Topics that regularly pay off: work prioritization, procrastination patterns, feedback they're not seeking, stress processing, avoiding hard conversations.
- The technique applies well beyond management: PhD advising, peer mentorship, any independent knowledge work.
- Accountability partnerships often fail because they lack this genuine investment — it's the difference between checking in and actually caring.

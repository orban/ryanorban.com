## Summary

This 2015 Ars Technica piece covers the first published demonstration of a CRISPR-based gene drive — a genetic construct that, once introduced into a population, spreads itself to nearly all offspring rather than following normal 50% Mendelian inheritance. The researchers called it a mutagenic chain reaction: one organism carrying the construct produces offspring that almost all inherit it, spreading exponentially through the population.

Gene drives work by using CRISPR to copy themselves onto the homologous chromosome during reproduction. Normally, if you have one copy of a gene and one wild-type copy, half your offspring get each. A gene drive cuts the wild-type copy and uses itself as the repair template — converting heterozygotes to homozygotes and ensuring near-100% transmission. Over generations, this can theoretically replace a trait in an entire wild population.

The applications are dramatic in both directions. For disease control: malaria-resistant mosquitoes with a gene drive could eliminate malaria transmission by replacing wild mosquito populations. For species conservation: gene drives could eliminate invasive species that are destroying ecosystems. For ecological risk: a gene drive released into the wild, even accidentally, could irreversibly alter or eliminate entire species. The 2015 moment was the first proof of concept that this worked in a multicellular organism, making it no longer purely theoretical.

## Key points

- Gene drive = CRISPR construct that spreads through a population by converting heterozygotes to homozygotes during reproduction.
- Near-100% inheritance rate vs. normal 50% — exponential population penetration over generations.
- Applications: malaria control (mosquitoes), invasive species elimination, disease vectors.
- Risk: irreversible ecological change, no undo once a drive escapes into the wild.
- 2015 was the first multicellular organism proof of concept — made this a real bioethics emergency rather than theory.
- Connects to broader synthetic biology safety discussion: when does beneficial technology require international governance?

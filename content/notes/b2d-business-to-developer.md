## Summary

Semil Shah wrote this TechCrunch piece in late 2012 arguing that a third market category — selling tools to software developers specifically — was being overlooked by investors and founders fixated on the B2B vs. B2C dichotomy. The B2D label captured companies like GitHub, Twilio, Stripe, and Heroku that weren't selling to enterprise decision-makers and weren't selling to consumers, but were selling to individual developers who had both purchasing authority and strong opinions about their tools.

The core insight is that developers are a different kind of buyer. They have high technical evaluation standards and low tolerance for poor tooling, but they also have real purchasing power — and the tools they adopt can spread bottom-up into their organizations without a formal procurement process. A developer who loves a tool becomes an internal advocate; the sales cycle becomes evangelism rather than enterprise negotiation. GitHub's growth from developer side project to company-wide platform is the canonical case.

Shah also speculated about the longer-term implication: B2D tools would automate redundant developer work, making some roles obsolete while democratizing app creation by abstracting infrastructure complexity. From 2024, this reads as an understatement — the developer tools category has expanded enormously, and the thesis that good tools spread virally from individual developers upward into organizational adoption is now standard thinking in PLG (product-led growth) strategy.

## Key points

- B2D firms sell to individual developers, not IT buyers — spreading through bottom-up adoption rather than top-down procurement.
- GitHub as the 2012 marquee case: started as a developer tool, expanded into organizational platform through usage rather than sales.
- Developer buyers differ from enterprise buyers: high technical standards, low patience for polish theater, but genuine purchasing power.
- Twilio, Stripe, Heroku fit the pattern: API-first tools designed for developers to evaluate and adopt independently.
- Precursor to PLG (product-led growth) as a formal strategy — Shah articulated the mechanism before the terminology hardened.

## Summary

James Clear's summary of Marshall Goldsmith's book about the interpersonal behavioral habits that hold back otherwise successful people. The core insight is counterintuitive: the behaviors that got you to senior levels — assertiveness, confidence, winning at all costs, adding your opinion to every discussion — often actively block you at the next level, where collaboration, listening, and giving credit matter more than individual performance.

Goldsmith identifies 20 habits that manifest in successful executives. Some examples: **adding too much value** (the compulsion to always add something to an already-good idea, which subtly signals that your input is more important than others'), **making destructive comments** (sarcasm and cutting remarks framed as just being honest), **starting with No, But, or "However"** (negating everything before engaging with it), **failing to give recognition** (taking credit without passing it on), and **not listening** (being physically present in conversations but visibly disengaged).

The mechanism: these habits become invisible over time because they're surrounded by success. When you're individually brilliant and driving results, people work around your interpersonal sharp edges. At the executive level, you're evaluated on what you enable in others — and the same sharp edges now have outsized negative effects.

Goldsmith's prescription is behavioral rather than attitudinal: don't try to change who you are, just change specific behaviors. The interventions are small and observable: ask for feedback, apologize immediately when you slip, say thank you more, write stop doing lists.

## Key points

- Success can mask bad interpersonal habits — they didn't prevent early career advancement so they never got corrected.
- The habits are subtle: excessive value-adding, negating before engaging, failing to give recognition, not listening actively.
- Leadership effectiveness at senior levels depends more on enabling others than on individual performance.
- Goldsmith's approach is behavioral — change specific observable behaviors, not personality.
- Practical interventions: solicit feedback, apologize in real time, write "stop doing" lists alongside to do lists.

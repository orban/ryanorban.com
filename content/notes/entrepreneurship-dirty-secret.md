## Summary

Alan Gleeson makes an uncomfortable argument at the height of the 2012 startup culture boom: entrepreneurship is not a universal good, and encouraging everyone to start a business is actively harmful. The piece appeared when entrepreneur had become a near-religious identity in tech culture, and YC-style startup accelerator programs were multiplying like franchises. Gleeson's position was that the romanticization of founding was producing legions of underprepared, unsuited founders who burned years and resources on doomed ventures.

The core claim is practical: not everyone has the temperament, skills, or circumstances to run a company. The survivorship bias in startup culture — where we celebrate the Jobses and Zuckerbergs while the vast majority of founders quietly fail — creates a distorted picture of the base rates. Gleeson argues that well-intentioned advisors who encourage people to just build it do real damage when the person in question doesn't actually have what it takes.

This is a minority view in 2012 tech media, which is partly why it registered as a bookmark. The counterargument — that we should lower the cost of trying and let the market sort out who succeeds — was the dominant frame at the time. But Gleeson's point survives: the social costs of failed entrepreneurship (financial ruin, psychological damage, opportunity cost) are real and underreported.

## Key points

- Survivorship bias in startup narratives makes the odds look better than they are — we hear the wins, not the silent failures.
- Not everyone is temperamentally suited for founding — risk tolerance, execution mindset, and domain knowledge all vary enormously.
- The boom in startup accelerator programs and entrepreneurship education in 2012 was partly supply-side: institutions needed curriculum, not evidence that it worked.
- The social glorification of entrepreneurship may be doing harm by misallocating human capital away from careers where people would actually excel.
- Counterpoint: lowering barriers reduces the cost of trying, which can be net-positive even with high failure rates.

## Summary

A Brooklyn Hacker blog post reflecting on what a software engineer learned after spending a year working in a marketing role. The core insight was about audience mismatch: engineers tend to design and build products for people who think like engineers — who appreciate technical elegance, want control, and read documentation. Marketing forces you to confront that most customers don't think this way, don't care about implementation details, and make decisions based on framing and trust rather than technical merit.

The perspective shift the author described: in engineering, success is measurable and objective (does it work? is it fast?). In marketing, success is about changing perceptions and behavior — a fundamentally messier, more ambiguous challenge. This forced the engineer to develop comfort with uncertainty and subjective feedback that their technical background hadn't prepared them for.

The piece also touched on how marketing exposure improves engineering product decisions. Engineers who understand how their products are actually positioned and sold tend to build different things than engineers who never interact with customers. The product thinking that comes from understanding why customers buy — not just what they buy — makes for better feature prioritization and UX decisions. In 2012, the full-stack engineer concept was emerging partly from this recognition.

## Key points

- Engineers build for other engineers; marketing forces you to internalize the actual customer's frame of reference.
- Technical success is measurable; marketing success is about changing perceptions — a harder epistemic environment.
- Exposure to marketing improves product thinking in engineers: better feature prioritization, less gold-plating.
- The discomfort of subjective, ambiguous feedback is a SKILL that technical backgrounds don't build.
- Understanding how something is sold changes what you build — go-to-market thinking should inform engineering decisions.

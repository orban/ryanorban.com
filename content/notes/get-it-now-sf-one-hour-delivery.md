## Summary

Get It Now was a 2012 San Francisco startup offering on-demand delivery of any product within one hour — predating the on-demand delivery wave that would produce Postmates, Instacart, DoorDash, and Gopuff over the following years. The PandoDaily coverage captures the moment when this was genuinely novel rather than expected.

The concept was: a courier picks up any item from any store and brings it to you within an hour. No inventory, no warehouse — just logistics and hustle. This model was unit-economically fragile from the start (a fact that would become apparent industry-wide by 2020), but the user value proposition was real, especially in a dense urban environment like San Francisco where car-free residents and time-poor tech workers were natural customers.

The framing in 2012 was "this is amazing and obviously the future." Looking back from a decade later, it was both: instant delivery became a $50B+ industry, and most of the companies pursuing it either failed or survived only with enormous sustained subsidies. Get It Now itself didn't survive. But the 2012 version of this idea was the proof-of-concept that showed consumer demand existed, which is why a wave of better-capitalized companies entered the market.

## Key points

- Get It Now operated a pure logistics model (no inventory), which was capital-light but operationally difficult to scale.
- The 2012 San Francisco market (dense, tech-wealthy, car-light) was the natural petri dish for this kind of service.
- On-demand delivery became a category because of early experiments like this, but most early players didn't survive the scaling phase.
- Unit economics were the killer: a $5-10 delivery fee doesn't cover courier costs unless order density is very high.
- The pattern: consumer appetite proved → venture capital floods in → category winners survive → unit economics require enormous scale to close.

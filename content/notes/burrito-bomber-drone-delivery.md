## Summary

Darwin Aerospace's "Burrito Bomber" was a fixed-wing UAV (unmanned aerial vehicle) modified to deliver burritos to GPS coordinates. The system used a Mission Planner-controlled autopilot, a custom payload bay, and a parachute drop mechanism to deposit a cylindrical burrito container at a specified location. The Hackaday coverage treated it as an amusing hack; the creators documented it seriously, with schematics and flight logs.

The tweet that saved this — "TacoCopter, meet the Burrito Bomber. The future is here" — referenced TacoCopter, an earlier 2012 media moment when a startup (later revealed as a joke) proposed taco delivery by helicopter, which briefly went viral. The Burrito Bomber was explicitly inspired by and named in relation to TacoCopter.

In retrospect, both projects were early signal for what became a serious industry. Amazon Prime Air announced drone delivery development in December 2013 (just a year after this). Wing (Google/Alphabet), Zipline (medical supplies in Rwanda), and eventually Amazon's FAA-approved delivery drones were the serious implementation of what maker projects like the Burrito Bomber explored for fun. The 2012 maker community's drone work — including this — was part of the knowledge base that fed into commercial development.

## Key points

- Fixed-wing UAV: more efficient for long-distance delivery than multirotor (quadcopter) — trades hovering ability for range and battery life
- GPS waypoint delivery: the core capability; autonomous navigation to a coordinate, payload drop, return — still the basic architecture of commercial delivery drones
- TacoCopter precedent: 2012 viral startup pitch (later revealed as conceptual) for helicopter taco delivery; culturally positioned drone delivery as sci-fi comedy
- Amazon Prime Air announced December 2013: the moment that made drone delivery a serious business category; followed a wave of maker projects like this one
- Zipline: the most successful implementation of autonomous delivery — medical supplies in Rwanda, later US — demonstrates the technology's real-world viability

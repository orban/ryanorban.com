## Summary

The Dunning-Kruger effect, named for David Dunning and Justin Kruger's 1999 paper Unskilled and Unaware of It, describes a metacognitive failure: people with limited competence in a domain lack the very skills needed to recognize their own incompetence. Novices don't know what they don't know. Experts, meanwhile, tend to underestimate their relative competence because they assume tasks that are easy for them are easy for others.

This Ars Technica piece revisited the original finding and engaged with ongoing methodological debates. One significant critique: the pattern Dunning and Kruger observed may be partly an artifact of regression to the mean — a statistical phenomenon where extreme low scorers necessarily have large gaps between self-rating and actual performance, not because of a psychological mechanism but just because of where they are on the distribution. Ulrich Ecker and others raised this statistical confound.

The genuine psychological finding — that metacognition is domain-specific and impaired when domain knowledge is impaired — survives these critiques. You need to know some calculus before you can accurately judge your calculus ability. The effect also has practical implications: expert blind spot is the flip side, where deep expertise makes it hard to explain concepts at a novice level because you've forgotten what it's like not to know.

## Key points

- Dunning-Kruger effect (1999): low-competence individuals overestimate ability; experts underestimate their relative edge.
- Mechanism: metacognitive accuracy requires domain knowledge — you can't assess what you can't recognize.
- Methodological critique: part of the effect may be regression to the mean artifact, not purely a psychological mechanism.
- Expert blind spot: experts underestimate difficulty of their domain for novices, complicating teaching.
- The general finding (metacognition requires domain knowledge) is robust; specific magnitude claims are contested.

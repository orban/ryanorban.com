## Summary

Brian Harvey (UC Berkeley) wrote this essay as a defense of SICP as the foundation of introductory computer science education, in response to pressure to replace it with more immediately practical Python-based curricula. The essay appeared as Berkeley's CS61A course was debating whether to retire SICP — a decision that ultimately went the other way (Python-based CS61A launched in 2011).

Harvey's argument: SICP matters not because it teaches Scheme but because it teaches the right ideas in the right order. The book treats programming as a way of thinking about processes and procedures, introducing abstraction at multiple levels — procedural abstraction, data abstraction, metalinguistic abstraction (building interpreters). The choice of Scheme is deliberate: its minimal syntax eliminates the noise that obscures what's actually being learned.

The cultural weight of the bookmark is clear from the original tweet: "CS61A still remains my favorite and most informative class." CS61A at Berkeley was notorious for the depth of insight it delivered — students who worked through it understood things about computation that Python-first courses didn't reach. Harvey and SICP shaped a generation of Berkeley CS graduates, and the debate about whether to replace abstraction-first teaching with syntax-first teaching remains live.

## Key points

- Harvey's core argument: SICP teaches *ideas* — abstraction, recursion, higher-order functions — not a programming language
- Scheme's minimal syntax is a feature: it removes noise so the concepts are visible
- CS61A at Berkeley used SICP until 2011 then switched to Python-based curriculum
- The essay frames the SICP vs. Python debate as depth vs. accessibility — both real values in tension
- Brian Harvey also created Snap! (later), an educational visual programming language for Berkeley's intro CS course

## Summary

Berlin Brandenburg Airport (BER) is one of the most spectacular infrastructure project failures in modern history. Radio Spaetkauf and RadioEins produced this podcast series documenting how a straightforward airport expansion became a 14-year delay, €7B+ cost overrun, and a running punchline for German bureaucratic dysfunction.

The opening in 1999 was planned for 2011. BER finally opened in 2020. In that interval: five cancelled opening ceremonies (including one where catering had been ordered and VIP guests were en route when the opening was called off), a fire suppression system that nobody understood how to test, electrical wiring that failed inspections repeatedly, and a management structure that distributed accountability so thoroughly that nobody was ever clearly responsible for anything.

The podcast is entertaining as disaster narrative but also genuinely instructive about how large projects fail. The BER story is a case study in optimism bias, scope creep, coordination failure in multi-stakeholder public projects, and the compounding effects of small decisions made by people who assumed someone else was tracking the big picture. The fire suppression system story alone — where the system was so complex that triggering it for testing would destroy the airport, so it was never tested — is a perfect example of building something that technically exists but cannot function.

## Key points

- BER planned opening 2011, actual opening 2020 — 9-year delay on a 3-year planned construction
- Fire suppression system: so over-engineered that it couldn't be tested safely without damaging the building — a legendary systems design failure
- Management structure: joint ownership between Berlin, Brandenburg, and federal government created a principal-agent problem with no clear authority
- The cancellation of the 2012 opening ceremony (with guests invited, catering ordered) became a symbol of management breakdown
- Classic megaproject pathology: Bent Flyvbjerg's research on cost overruns and schedule delays applies directly

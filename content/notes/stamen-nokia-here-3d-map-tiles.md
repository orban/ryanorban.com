## Summary

Stamen Design (the San Francisco design studio known for distinctive map tile styles like Watercolor and Toner) collaborated with Nokia Here to render Nokia's 3D urban model data as a web visualization. Nokia Here had been building lidar-scanned 3D models of cities for navigation purposes — the data captured building footprints, heights, and geometries at high resolution. Stamen's contribution was treating this engineering data as raw material for design: rendering it as isometric 3D city views with distinctive visual styling.

Stamen Design had been doing this kind of work since 2003 — taking data sources (tile servers, GPS traces, geographic datasets) and transforming them into visually compelling artifacts that communicated something beyond the raw information. Their map styles (Watercolor, which rendered OSM tiles in watercolor-painting style; Toner, the high-contrast black and white version) had become widely used via the Stamen map tiles service. The Nokia Here collaboration extended this to 3D.

The technical approach for 3D tiles in 2013: web WebGL was immature, so the visualization was likely pre-rendered from the 3D data rather than rendered in real-time in the browser. The bookmark's exclamation — "Holy fuck yes. @Stamen does Nokia 3d tiles, and it's gorgeous" — captures the reaction to seeing urban 3D data rendered with Stamen's visual sensibility. This represented an early example of lidar data being used for artistic rather than purely navigational purposes.

## Key points

- Stamen Design: SF design studio known for distinctive map tile rendering styles (Watercolor, Toner, Terrain) — artful treatment of geographic data.
- Nokia Here: Nokia's maps division (later acquired by HERE Technologies) building lidar-scanned 3D city models for navigation.
- 3D urban models: lidar point clouds combined with building footprints — engineering data repurposed as design material.
- Isometric rendering: 3D city viewed from fixed oblique angle — captures depth and structure without requiring real-time 3D rendering.
- The broader pattern: geographic/urban datasets being treated as creative raw material — data art at the intersection of cartography and design.

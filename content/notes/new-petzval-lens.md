## Summary

Lomography launched the New Petzval Art Lens as a Kickstarter campaign in 2013 — a modern reimagining of Josef Petzval's 1840 portrait lens design, rebuilt for contemporary DSLR cameras with Canon EF and Nikon F mounts. The original Petzval lens was designed for daguerreotype portrait photography and was the dominant portrait lens formula for decades. Its optical characteristic — sharp central focus with a distinctive swirling bokeh in the background — was a side effect of the lens formula that photographers now deliberately seek.

Lomography occupies a niche in photography: a company that sells analog film cameras with deliberately imperfect optical properties, positioned as an antidote to digital photography's clinical precision. The New Petzval was an extension of that ethos into the DSLR world — a modern mechanical build with 19th-century optical math, letting photographers produce images with the aesthetic of Victorian portrait studios using contemporary camera bodies.

The lens achieved fully brass construction, a brass aperture wheel (using Waterhouse stops, the pre-iris mechanism of early photography), and manual focus only — a tactile object in a world of autofocus motors. Ryan's bookmark note tagged it as "for the discerning hipster photographer," which captures the market positioning accurately.

## Key points

- Josef Petzval (1840): designed the original lens formula for the Viennese court — a fast (f/3.6) portrait lens that dominated photography for 30+ years.
- Swirly bokeh: the optical signature of the Petzval formula — caused by astigmatic field curvature — creates characteristic vortex-like out-of-focus regions.
- Waterhouse stops: interchangeable aperture plates (physically inserting brass discs) rather than an iris diaphragm — authentic to the original era's mechanism.
- Lomography Kickstarter model: crowdfunding for limited-run specialty optics at premium prices (original campaign ~$500) targeting photography enthusiasts.
- Positioned explicitly against clinical digital optics: the appeal is character and imperfection, not sharpness or accuracy.

## Summary

This LessWrong community post (likely from the effective altruism adjacent community) catalogs practical pathways to acquiring second citizenships and residencies as a form of geographic diversification and risk mitigation. The framing is pragmatic: having options to relocate matters, and the author has done the research to make those options legible.

The four main pathways covered are: miscellaneous programs (Panama's friendly-nations visa for permanent residency with minimal maintenance requirements; COFA nations; Israel's Law of Return; Canada's Express Entry), ancestry-based options (European countries — Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Austria, Slovakia, Czechia, Ireland, Italy — allowing heritage-based citizenship without relocation), financial investment (most require $100k+, though St. Lucia is highlighted as affordable at ~$15,000–$35,000 net cost), and naturalization through extended residence.

Each pathway trades off differently along key dimensions: documentation burden, passport strength and visa-free access, cost and maintenance, likelihood of approval, and whether benefits extend to spouses and descendants. The Spain route is called out specifically for Latin American passport holders (2-year naturalization, vs. the standard 10). Canada offers citizenship after 3 years of residency.

## Key points

- Ancestry-based European citizenship is often the cheapest route and requires no relocation — worth checking family history even a generation or two back.
- Passport strength varies significantly: a second EU passport opens far more doors than a Caribbean investment citizenship.
- Panama's friendly-nations visa offers permanent residency relatively quickly with low ongoing requirements — a favorite for those wanting a backup without committing to citizenship.
- St. Lucia stands out among investment-based citizenships for relatively low net cost (~$15k–$35k), compared to $100k+ for most others.
- The effective altruism community and rationalists increasingly treat optionality — including geographic — as a form of long-horizon risk management.

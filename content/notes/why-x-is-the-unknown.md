## Summary

Terry Moore's TED talk traces the etymology of x-as-unknown back through history to medieval translation work. When Arabic algebraic texts were translated into Spanish in the 12th century, the Arabic word shei (شيء, meaning "thing" or something) — used by al-Khwarizmi and other mathematicians to denote an unknown quantity — became a problem. The Spanish language has no "sh" sound. Translators used the Greek letter chi (χ), which was written as "x" in Latin script. From there, x spread through European mathematics and became the standard symbol for an unknown.

Algebra itself is an Arabic word (from al-jabr, "the reunion of broken parts"), and the foundational algebraic text was al-Khwarizmi's 9th-century "Al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala." His name also gave us the word algorithm. The symbol x that every high school student writes on homework sheets is a phonological accident of medieval translation practice, not a deliberate choice.

This is a broader pattern in history of mathematics: seemingly arbitrary notational conventions have concrete historical origins. The equals sign (=) was invented by Robert Recorde in 1557. The integral sign (∫) was chosen by Leibniz as an elongated S for summa. The notation we use shapes how we think, and it usually has roots in contingency rather than design.

## Key points

- "x" for unknown derives from Arabic "shei" (thing) → Greek chi (χ) → Latin x, via 12th-century Spanish translation where "sh" sound didn't exist.
- al-Khwarizmi's algebra texts were the source; his name also gives us "algorithm."
- Mathematical notation is historically contingent — its apparent universality is partly an accident of transmission.
- Same talk by Terry Moore also notes that "ok and ampersand" have similarly contingent origins.
- The story connects Arabic mathematics to the European Renaissance through translation networks in Toledo and Andalusia.

## Summary

Emma Goldman's 1934 essay was written near the end of her life, after decades of anarchist organizing, two deportations, time in prison, and exile from the United States. The question she poses isn't rhetorical — she answers it directly and without self-pity: yes, her life was worth living, despite the suffering, because it was spent in pursuit of ideas she considered worth dying for.

Goldman's argument is essentially about the relationship between meaning and struggle. A comfortable life spent avoiding conflict has no claim to have been lived fully. The essay reflects the anarchist tradition's view that authentic human life requires resistance to domination — that a person who accommodates every unjust system to preserve comfort has traded their humanity for safety.

The essay was saved during Ryan's Zipfian Academy period — an unusual outlier among the data science and programming resources. The save likely reflects the early SF tech culture's complicated relationship with the Goldman legacy: San Francisco was Goldman's city, and her ghost haunted the labor and housing politics that surrounded the tech boom.

## Key points

- Written in 1934, after Goldman's exile from the US; a retrospective justification of a life spent outside safety and convention.
- Central argument: meaning comes from alignment between values and actions, not from outcomes or recognition.
- Emma Goldman was a foundational anarchist thinker and organizer — free speech, birth control rights, anti-militarism, labor organizing.
- The essay is archived by UC Berkeley Libraries as part of the Emma Goldman Papers project.
- A counterpoint to the instrumentalism of the tech world Ryan inhabited in 2013 — worth anything because it was useful? Or worth something intrinsically?

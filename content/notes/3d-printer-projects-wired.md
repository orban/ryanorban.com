## Summary

Wired's 2012 roundup of projects for MakerBot and similar desktop 3D printers — dinosaur skull replicas, DNA double helix models, space shuttle parts, replacement hardware, and jewelry — captured the optimism around consumer 3D printing at its early-2012 peak. The MakerBot Replicator had just launched, and the sub-$2,000 price point felt like a democratizing moment. 3D printing was moving from industrial rapid prototyping to a tool that motivated amateurs could use.

The list reflects what desktop FDM (Fused Deposition Modeling) printers were actually good for in 2012: rigid plastic objects with moderate detail, optimized for form over precision. Dinosaur bones are a perfect application — the shape matters, the tolerances don't. DNA models are similarly forgiving. Space shuttle replacement parts had real utility (NASA lost some tooling documentation and was using 3D scanning + printing to recreate parts). Jewelry was more aspirational — FDM surface quality in 2012 was rough.

MakerBot at this moment was the leading name in consumer 3D printing, having grown out of the open-source hardware community (RepRap project). The company would be acquired by Stratasys in 2013 for $403M, close its retail stores by 2017, and eventually enter financial distress — the gap between the 2012 excitement and the actual consumer market for desktop 3D printers turned out to be large. Most people didn't have enough uses to justify the cost and maintenance burden.

## Key points

- MakerBot Replicator (early 2012): sub-$2,000 desktop FDM printer — democratizing rapid prototyping.
- RepRap project (Adrian Bowyer, Bath) was the open-source precursor that made MakerBot possible.
- FDM printers good for: replacement parts, models, rigid structures where precision ≤ 0.5mm is fine.
- MakerBot acquired by Stratasys 2013 for $403M; consumer 3D printing market never reached predicted scale.
- Thingiverse (MakerBot's model repository) accumulated millions of printable designs and outlasted the MakerBot brand.

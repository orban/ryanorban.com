## Summary

James Somers distills what he considers the best general advice he's encountered, drawn from William James's writing on habit formation. The core insight from James: the purpose of forming habits is to free up attention for higher-level activity, and the critical factor in actually forming a habit is to act on every resolution *immediately*, without exception. Not to plan, not to wait for the right moment, not to decide you'll start Monday — to act now, while the intention is fresh.

William James argued that a resolution not acted upon immediately begins to dissipate — the emotional energy behind it decays, and we start negotiating exceptions. Each exception weakens the habit-forming mechanism. The practical implication is that the difference between someone who successfully forms a habit and someone who doesn't is usually not willpower or motivation in the abstract, but whether they acted on the intention at the moment it formed.

Somers connects this to the more general principle that the gap between intention and action is where most self-improvement breaks down. The value of the insight isn't novelty — it's how simple and actionable it is. Unlike most productivity advice, it doesn't prescribe a system; it prescribes a single behavioral heuristic: when you decide to do something, start now. This is more aligned with how habit formation actually works neurologically (immediate reinforcement strengthens the behavior pattern) than the elaborate systems in most productivity writing.

## Key points

- William James: act on every resolution immediately — each exception weakens the habit-forming mechanism.
- The gap between intention and action is where most self-improvement fails.
- A single heuristic: when you decide to do something, start now, before the intention decays.
- Neurologically correct: immediate action reinforces the behavior pattern more than delayed, planned action.
- James Somers's distillation — one of the more pointed and actionable pieces in the productivity canon.

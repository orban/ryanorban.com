## Summary

Foldit is a crowdsourced protein folding game developed by the University of Washington. Players manipulate 3D protein structures through an intuitive game interface, guided by a score that reflects how folded (low-energy, physically plausible) the structure is. In 2011, Foldit players solved the structure of a retroviral protease from Mason-Pfizer monkey virus — an HIV-related enzyme — in about 10 days, cracking a problem that had resisted automated methods and human biochemists for 15 years.

The solve was published in Nature Structural and Molecular Biology, with the player community credited as authors. This was remarkable not just for the result but for the method: human spatial reasoning, distributed across thousands of players who had no formal biochemistry training, outperformed purpose-built computational algorithms. The game tapped an aspect of human cognition — 3D spatial manipulation — that computers were still weak at in 2011.

This connects to broader themes in distributed cognition, human-computer interaction, and the limits of automation. Some problems respond to computational brute force; others need the kind of flexible, intuition-guided search that humans do well. Foldit found a way to harness that capability at scale.

## Key points

- Foldit players decoded an HIV-related retroviral protease structure in 10 days that automated methods hadn't cracked in 15 years.
- Human spatial reasoning > automated search for protein structure optimization — a genuine gap in computational capability circa 2011.
- Published in Nature Structural and Molecular Biology with player community as credited authors — unusual for academic science.
- Gamification as a mechanism for contributing to scientific research: players contributed 95% for the game experience, 5% aware they were solving real problems.
- Landmark case for crowdsourcing complex scientific problems that require human intuition rather than algorithmic exhaustion.

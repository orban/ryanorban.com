## Summary

Erik Torenberg's essay on career strategy uses the Warren Buffett concept of an economic moat — a durable competitive advantage — and applies it to individuals. A personal moat is a set of skills or capabilities that are hard for others to replicate, compound over time (building passive social or knowledge capital), and are specific to your particular combination of talents and interests.

The key diagnostic question: what comes naturally to you but challenges others? The things you find easy but that others find difficult are the most defensible moat candidates, because they're hard to reverse-engineer from the outside. If thousands of people have done it, or a Quora post explains it step-by-step, it's probably not a moat.

The SKILL combination approach (attributed to Scott Adams, creator of Dilbert) is particularly useful: you don't need to be the world's best at any single skill. You can build a moat by being very good (top 25%) at 2-3 complementary skills that rarely coexist. The resulting combination is rare and powerful even if none of the individual components is world-class.

## Key points

- Personal moats should be hard to learn and hard to replicate — if something is teachable via a standard curriculum, it doesn't differentiate.
- The 10,000-hour question reframed: not "what are you willing to grind at? but what would you master if given time, and what would that combination enable?"
- Skill combinations are often more defensible than single-skill depth — e.g., an ML engineer who can also talk to customers and communicate findings to executives.
- Moats build passive capital: social proof, a reputation, a network that grows without constant tending — not just a skill that requires continual active deployment.
- Related to Paul Graham's do things that don't scale early, Michael Porter's competitive positioning, and the career capital framework in So Good They Can't Ignore You.

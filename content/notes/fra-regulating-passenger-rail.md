## Summary

The Federal Railroad Administration (FRA) sets safety standards for all railroad equipment in the United States — originally designed for heavy freight locomotives and cars. The problem: those same standards have been applied to passenger rail, requiring trains to be built like tanks. FRA regulations mandate that passenger cars be able to withstand enormous collision forces through structural reinforcement (buff strength), resulting in vehicles that weigh roughly four to five times as much as equivalent European or Japanese trains.

This weight difference has cascading consequences. Heavy trains require stronger track, more powerful locomotives, more infrastructure investment, and higher operating costs. They can't share light rail corridors without special engineering. And because Amtrak operates predominantly on freight-owned track, US passenger trains are already at a disadvantage — the FRA's weight requirements make it worse. A Bombardier or Siemens passenger car certified for European use cannot legally operate on US tracks without expensive modification or waiver.

The perverse outcome: the regulations intended to make trains safe have made passenger rail economically unviable at scale. Europe and Japan run far lighter, faster, and cheaper trains on dedicated infrastructure. The US runs heavy, slow trains on shared freight track, then wonders why ridership is low. The EBBC article argued this was a structural policy failure, not a cultural one — Americans would take trains if trains worked.

## Key points

- FRA buff strength requirements mandate US passenger cars be 4-5x heavier than European equivalents.
- European and Japanese trains certified for their markets cannot operate on US tracks without expensive modification.
- Heavy trains require heavier track infrastructure, higher maintenance costs, and slower speeds on curves.
- Amtrak operates mostly on freight-owned right-of-way — FRA weight rules compound the disadvantage.
- Countries with successful passenger rail (France, Japan, Germany) use lighter vehicles on dedicated infrastructure.
- The article frames this as regulatory capture: freight railroad interests shaped rules that incidentally hamper passenger rail competition.

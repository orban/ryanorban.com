## Summary

This Defmacro (Slava Akhmechet) Substack post challenges the meritocratic model of corporate advancement. The blurb captures the premise directly: "Almost everyone who does great work toils in relative obscurity. Performance reviews are social fiction. How do people really advance through the corporate hierarchy?"

The core argument is that promotions in most organizations are not primarily a function of output quality, but of visibility, relationship capital, and strategic positioning. The people who advance fastest are typically those who work on high-visibility projects with influential stakeholders, communicate their work effectively upward, and manage political dynamics as deliberately as they manage technical work.

This creates a game within the work: doing excellent work on invisible problems may produce less career advancement than doing adequate work on visible problems. Performance reviews often ratify decisions already made through informal social processes — they're the documentation, not the decision. Understanding this distinction changes how a rational person allocates effort.

The post is skeptical of the idea that you can simply do great work and get noticed — the visibility of work is itself a variable to manage.

## Key points

- **Visibility is a variable**: working on internally visible projects with influential stakeholders has different career returns than equally important but less visible work.
- **Performance reviews are lagging indicators**: the actual promotion decision is often made before the formal review — the review reflects existing informal consensus.
- **Relationship capital compounds**: people promote others they trust and have social proof for — this trust is built through interaction, not evaluation forms.
- Related to Paul Graham's distinction between "impressive to people inside vs. outside tech" and Cal Newport's "career capital" model.
- The cynical implication: optimizing purely for external quality metrics (ship great code, solve hard problems) is necessary but not sufficient for advancement in large organizations.

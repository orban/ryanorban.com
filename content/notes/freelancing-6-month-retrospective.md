## Summary

Mike Rooney's 2012 retrospective on his first six months of freelancing as a software engineer is a candid account from the early phase of the independent contractor wave — before "remote work" and "freelancing" had the polished lifestyle branding they'd acquire by 2015. Rooney came from a traditional salaried position and documented the practical reality: how to find clients, how to price work, how to handle the isolation and self-direction of working independently, and the cashflow patterns that make freelancing financially different from a salary.

The core tension Rooney identifies is the feast-or-famine dynamic. Freelancing means inconsistent income by design — some months are flush with billable hours, others are spent on business development and unpaid prospecting. Managing this requires either a financial buffer (several months of runway) or maintaining a diversified client base so no single client loss is catastrophic. The psychological shift is equally significant: without external accountability structures (a manager, a team, daily standups), self-imposed discipline becomes the primary driver of output. Some people discover this suits them; others find they need external structure to stay productive.

On the software engineering side specifically, Rooney noted that freelance clients often have less mature codebases than employers — technical debt, unclear requirements, shifting priorities. The engineer who thrives freelancing tends to be comfortable scoping work conservatively, communicating proactively about blockers, and delivering something functional in shorter cycles rather than pursuing architectural elegance. The side projects tension is also present: freelancing creates the illusion of more time for personal projects but the billing pressure and irregular schedule often consume more mental energy than a 9-to-5.

## Key points

- Freelancing income is irregular by design — requires financial runway (3-6 months) or diversified clients to avoid anxiety-driven bad decisions.
- Business development is unpaid work that reduces effective hourly rate; experienced freelancers price this into their rates.
- Remote work isolation requires intentional structure: scheduled social contact, coworking, or community membership.
- Freelance clients often have higher technical debt and less clarity than employers — scope creep and shifting requirements are the norm.
- The psychological skills (self-direction, proactive communication, conservative estimation) are as important as the technical skills.
- 2012 context: pre-platform economy — clients found via personal networks, not Upwork or similar; hourly rates negotiated individually.

## Summary

A Stanford University research group published a 2013 paper demonstrating RNA-based transistors that could implement Boolean logic gates inside living E. coli cells. The components — called transcriptor transistors — controlled the flow of RNA polymerase along a DNA strand using engineered RNA sequences, analogous to how electronic transistors control current flow with voltage. By combining multiple transcriptors, the researchers built AND, OR, NOT, and NAND gates, and proved that the same logic that underlies electronic computing could operate inside a biological cell.

The significance was both theoretical and practical. Theoretically, it demonstrated that cells could perform arbitrary computation using only the molecular machinery they already contain — no external hardware required. Practically, it opened a path toward cells that can make decisions based on their internal state: a therapeutic cell that detects cancer markers and releases a drug only when specific conditions are met, or a bioreactor cell that adjusts its metabolism based on multiple input signals simultaneously.

Drew Endy's group at Stanford (with Jerome Bonnet) was central to this work. Endy is one of the founders of synthetic biology as a formal engineering discipline — the idea that biological systems can be designed from modular, reusable parts (BioBricks) with predictable behavior, just as electronic systems are designed from resistors, capacitors, and transistors. This genetic transistor work was a demonstration that the transistor-level abstraction could be realized in biology, not just the higher-level circuit abstraction.

## Key points

- Transcriptor: RNA-based biological transistor — controls RNA polymerase flow along DNA, analogous to voltage-controlled electron flow in silicon transistors.
- Boolean logic in cells: AND, OR, NOT, NAND gates built from transcriptors — proves arbitrary computation is possible inside living cells.
- Drew Endy / Stanford: leading synthetic biology group; Endy is a founder of the BioBrick standardization movement in synthetic biology.
- Therapeutic applications: cells that compute their internal state and respond conditionally — detect-and-destroy cancer cell concept.
- Synthetic biology as engineering: the ambition to design biological systems from composable, predictable parts — paralleling the engineering of electronic circuits.

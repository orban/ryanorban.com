## Summary

This Harvard Business Review piece by Jim Stikeleather argues that effective data storytelling is fundamentally about narrative craft, not visualization technology. The most common failure mode in data communication: analysts present what they found (a tour of the data) rather than what it means (a case for action). Audiences don't experience data — they experience stories. If you don't structure your findings as a story with a clear protagonist, conflict, and resolution, the data will not drive decisions.

The framework centers on three questions you must answer before choosing any visualization: Who is the audience? What do they already know and believe? What decision or action should they take after seeing this? The visualization type follows from the answer — a line chart for trend, a scatter for correlation, a bar for comparison — but the wrong choice of message renders even a perfect chart useless.

Stikeleather draws on Nancy Duarte's work on presentation structure and Edward Tufte's data-ink ratio principle: every visual element should earn its presence by adding information. The combination — a story arc from the communication side, visual parsimony from the design side — defines what good data presentation looks like. This was influential in how data scientists at the time were taught to communicate with business stakeholders.

## Key points

- Start with audience and message, not data and tools — what decision should they make after seeing this?
- Structure findings as narrative: context (what's the situation?), complication (what's the problem?), resolution (what should we do?)
- Visualization type follows from message, not data type — the same data can support many chart types depending on what you want to say
- Edward Tufte's data-ink ratio: remove every visual element that doesn't add information (gridlines, 3D effects, chart junk)
- Nancy Duarte's presentation arc: present the gap between what is and what could be — that tension is what motivates action
- The tour of the data anti-pattern: walking through every finding in order without a unifying argument — audiences leave entertained but not changed

## Summary

The Verge covered Twitter's visualization of geotagged tweets as luminous heat maps — rendering global and city-scale tweet density as art. The visualizations showed the geography of Twitter usage: bright clusters around dense urban areas, dark gaps over oceans and rural regions. Major events (concerts, sporting matches) produced bright spikes on the city maps, visible as anomalous hotspots in the tweet density.

The work built on the tradition of Eric Fischer's Geotaggers' World Atlas (Flickr photo density maps), which had shown that geotagged social media data could produce beautiful, information-rich maps of human activity and movement. Twitter's version used the streaming API to capture real-time geotag data and visualize it.

What made these visualizations significant in 2013: they made the quantified society visible in a way that felt poetic rather than surveillance-like. The density patterns revealed which cities were Twitter-active, which events generated geographic spikes, and which regions were effectively absent from the platform — a map of the digital divide as much as a map of activity.

## Key points

- Geotagged tweets as a signal for human activity and movement density
- Visualization technique: density/heat map rendering, similar to Eric Fischer's Flickr atlas
- Twitter enabled geotag on ~1-2% of tweets in 2013 — a biased but large sample of public posts
- Eric Fischer's Geotaggers' World Atlas was the aesthetic antecedent
- The maps reveal the digital divide geographically: tweet density maps are also internet access maps
- Real-time event spikes visible in geotagged data — stadiums, concerts, protests all show up

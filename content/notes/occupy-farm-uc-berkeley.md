## Summary

In April 2012, a coalition of activists occupied a 10-acre plot of land in Albany, California owned by UC Berkeley — the Gill Tract, a remnant of the university's former agricultural station. The occupiers, inspired by the broader Occupy movement, planted corn, tomatoes, and other crops, demanding the land be used for urban farming and community food production rather than developed into a retail center. Mother Jones covered the story through the lens of a reporter conflicted about the tactics while sympathetic to the goals.

The Albany Gill Tract had been used for agricultural research by UC Berkeley for decades. With declining university agricultural programs and budget pressures, UC had approved plans to develop part of the land into a Whole Foods and housing. Occupy the Farm argued that an urban food desert surrounded the tract, that the agricultural land was irreplaceable, and that public university land shouldn't be privatized for retail development. The occupation planted actual crops to make the land-use argument concrete rather than abstract.

The tactical tension the Mother Jones piece explored: direct action (planting crops, occupying land) is a more radical form of protest than permitted demonstrations, and it forced an uncomfortable question about whether the ends (preserving urban agricultural land) justified the means (trespass, civil disobedience). The broader food justice movement was active in the Bay Area — urban farming, community gardens, access to fresh produce in underserved neighborhoods — and the Gill Tract occupation landed at the intersection of that movement and Occupy's post-encampment energy.

## Key points

- Albany Gill Tract: 10-acre former UC Berkeley agricultural research plot, targeted for retail/housing development.
- Occupation planted actual food crops — making the "this should be farmland" argument literal and photogenic.
- Occupy movement tactics (occupation, direct action) applied to land use and food justice rather than financial inequality.
- Food desert framing: the surrounding Albany/El Cerrito area had poor access to fresh produce.
- UC Berkeley eventually maintained some agricultural use of part of the tract after sustained pressure.

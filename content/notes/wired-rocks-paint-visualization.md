## Summary

This WIRED Design article profiles a high-speed photography project by Fabian Oefner that captures rocks being dropped into thick paint at microsecond timescales. The impact creates mushroom-cloud and flower-shaped formations as paint is displaced — the physics of the Rayleigh-Taylor instability and fluid dynamics made visible.

The work is at the intersection of physics visualization, fine art photography, and the then-booming trend of high-speed photography. The images were widely shared on social media in late 2013 because they are genuinely striking: what looks like an abstract floral painting is actually the exact shape of a gravitational impact frozen in time.

High-speed photography was democratizing in 2013 with affordable cameras (Canon DSLRs with high-speed modes, later iPhone slow-motion video) enabling artists and scientists to work in this medium without specialized equipment.

## Key points

- Fabian Oefner's work: rocks dropped into paint photographed at microsecond exposures — reveals fluid dynamics invisible to normal vision.
- The flower shapes are fluid dynamics in action: Rayleigh-Taylor instability, splash crown formation.
- Part of the science as art genre of photography that circulated widely on social media in 2013.
- High-speed photography was becoming accessible to non-specialists in 2013 with affordable DSLR equipment.
- Shared originally as visualize gravity on Twitter — technically accurate, if dramatic.

## Summary

A clinical review by Grant Tinsley and Paul La Bounty examining the evidence base for intermittent fasting (IF) protocols and their effects on body composition and cardiometabolic health markers. The review covers three main IF protocols: **alternate-day fasting** (alternating ad libitum and fasting days, with fasting days capped at ~25% of maintenance calories), **whole-day fasting** (1–2 complete fast days per week with free eating otherwise — the 5:2 pattern), and **time-restricted feeding** (all eating compressed into a daily window, typically 4–8 hours).

The overall conclusion is that IF produces fat loss and improvements in blood lipids, glucose, and inflammatory markers comparable to continuous caloric restriction — but the mechanism is almost entirely explained by the resulting energy deficit rather than any fasting-specific metabolic effect. Where IF may have a genuine advantage is in lean mass preservation: some protocols, particularly those combined with resistance training, show better muscle retention than equivalent deficit achieved through daily restriction. The evidence is mixed and many studies have methodological limitations (short duration, no control for total calorie intake), making strong causal claims difficult.

Practically, IF protocols have high adherence in some populations because restricting eating windows or days is simpler to comply with than constant daily tracking. This behavioral advantage — not metabolic superiority — may explain their real-world effectiveness.

## Key points

- Three IF categories: alternate-day fasting, whole-day fasting (5:2), and time-restricted feeding — each has different compliance and muscle-sparing profiles
- Fat loss and metabolic improvements are comparable to caloric restriction when total energy intake is matched — fasting per se adds little beyond the deficit
- Lean mass preservation may be better with IF protocols, especially when combined with resistance training, but evidence is inconsistent
- Compliance advantage: restricting timing or days is behaviorally easier than continuous daily tracking for many people
- Short study durations (most under 24 weeks) limit conclusions about long-term effects and cardiovascular outcomes

## Summary

MC Stockman, D Thomas, J Burke, and CM Apovian published this review in *Current Obesity Reports* (Springer, 2018) to take stock of what the clinical and animal evidence actually shows about intermittent fasting — and to temper the hype. The headline finding is sobering: most human IF studies produce minimal weight loss and only marginal improvements in metabolic biomarkers. The animal data is more encouraging, but translating it to humans is complicated by adherence, dietary composition, and study duration.

The review distinguishes between caloric restriction and intermittent fasting and finds they produce comparable weight loss and improvements in insulin sensitivity when matched for calories. This undercuts the popular claim that IF works through mechanisms beyond simple calorie reduction — at least for the weight-loss outcomes. Where IF might have a distinct edge is in the animal literature, which shows reduced oxidative stress, enhanced cognition, and delayed aging markers, though robust human evidence for these effects is sparse.

The practical implication the authors draw is that IF can work as a dietary strategy, but it's not metabolically special. For patients who find time-restricted eating easier to maintain than continuous caloric restriction, it's a legitimate tool. For everyone else, the evidence doesn't support choosing IF over other approaches on mechanistic grounds. The paper is a useful corrective to the wave of enthusiastic IF coverage that preceded it.

## Key points

- Most human intermittent fasting trials show only minimal weight loss; effect sizes are comparable to standard caloric restriction when calories are matched
- Animal models show more distinct benefits: reduced oxidative stress, cognitive improvements, and delayed aging — but human translation is not established
- Insulin sensitivity improvements from IF and continuous caloric restriction are similar in magnitude, suggesting no metabolic advantage specific to IF
- CM Apovian (Boston Medical Center obesity specialist) led the review, lending clinical credibility to the skeptical framing
- Practical takeaway: IF is a viable dietary approach for adherence reasons, not because of unique metabolic mechanisms

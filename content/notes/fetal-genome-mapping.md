## Summary

A 2012 paper demonstrated that a fetal genome could be reconstructed almost entirely from a maternal blood sample and a paternal saliva sample — no amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling required. The technique exploits the fact that fetal cell-free DNA circulates in maternal blood during pregnancy. By sequencing this mixture and comparing it against paternal DNA, researchers could infer the fetal genome by subtraction and recombination inference.

The research, led by Jay Shendure at the University of Washington, was published in Science Translational Medicine. It represented a significant advance over earlier non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), which could detect common chromosomal aneuploidies (like Down syndrome / trisomy 21) but couldn't reconstruct a full genome. A complete fetal genome, in principle, reveals everything from single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) linked to disease risk to traits like eye color — though predicting most complex traits from genotype alone remains an unsolved problem.

The immediate clinical application was detecting single-gene disorders like cystic fibrosis and Huntington's disease prenatally without the ~1% miscarriage risk of amniocentesis. The broader ethical questions — what should parents know, what should they act on, what happens to the data — were raised immediately and remain live debates in bioethics.

## Key points

- Fetal genome reconstruction from maternal blood + paternal saliva — no invasive procedure required.
- Led by Jay Shendure (University of Washington), published in Science Translational Medicine.
- Technique: sequence cell-free fetal DNA in maternal blood, subtract maternal genome, infer recombination from paternal.
- Enables non-invasive detection of single-gene disorders like cystic fibrosis alongside chromosomal aneuploidies.
- Raises prenatal genomics ethics: what parents should be told, genetic counseling requirements, data storage.

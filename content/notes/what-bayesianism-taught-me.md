## Summary

This LessWrong post isn't about the mechanics of Bayes' theorem — it's about the epistemic habits that come from taking Bayesian reasoning seriously as a framework for belief. The core shift: instead of treating beliefs as binary (true or false), Bayesianism treats them as probability distributions over possible states of the world. You hold your beliefs with a degree of confidence proportional to evidence, and you update that confidence when new evidence arrives.

The practical lessons follow from this. You stop saying I believe X and start saying I think X with ~80% confidence — which forces you to actually examine how confident you are. You notice when you're updating your beliefs asymmetrically (quick to raise confidence when evidence confirms, slow to lower it when evidence contradicts). You become sensitive to the difference between no evidence for X and evidence against X.

The LessWrong / rationalist community in 2014 treated Bayesian epistemology as essentially the correct framework for reasoning under uncertainty, influenced by Eliezer Yudkowsky's sequences. This post captures the aha moment that community described: the change in how you hold beliefs, not just the ability to compute posterior probabilities.

## Key points

- Bayesian reasoning reframes beliefs as probability assignments, forcing explicit acknowledgment of uncertainty rather than false confidence.
- Updating on evidence (shifting the probability up or down) is the core mental operation — the question is always "how much does this evidence change my estimate?"
- The base rate fallacy becomes obvious once you're thinking in priors: you can't evaluate evidence without knowing the prior probability of what you're testing.
- Calibration — having your stated confidence levels match actual frequencies — is a learnable SKILL; prediction markets and superforecasting rely on it.
- Connects to frequentist vs. Bayesian debate in statistics, but the epistemic habits are broader than any particular statistical methodology.

## Summary

Neatorama covered three necktie knots that were circulating on social media in early 2013: the Eldredge knot, the Trinity knot, and the Cape knot. These were viral in the sense that instructional images and YouTube tutorials were being widely shared — a genre of "did you know you could do this?" content.

The Eldredge knot (created by Jeffrey Eldredge around 2007) produces a dramatic fishtail pattern — the small end of the tie is used as the active end rather than the wide end, which is unusual. The Trinity knot produces a three-way symmetrical pattern. The Cape knot creates a layered, architectural shape.

All three were much more complex than the standard Four-in-Hand or even the Windsor knot, requiring 15+ steps vs the Four-in-Hand's 4-5. They were conversation pieces rather than practical daily wear — requiring a very long tie and a specific collar spread to work.

## Key points

- The Eldredge knot inverts the standard active-end convention: the narrow end does the wrapping, leaving the wide end as a core
- These knots required rehearsal to execute reliably — impractical for daily wear but suitable as occasional statements
- The viral spread of exotic tie knots in 2013 reflected Pinterest and Reddit content dynamics: visual, shareable, surprising
- The standard Four-in-Hand remains dominant for its simplicity and versatility; Windsor knot for formality — exotic knots occupy a niche

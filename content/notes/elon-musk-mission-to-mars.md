## Summary

Wired's 2012 Q&A with Elon Musk on SpaceX's ambitions for Mars colonization — published when SpaceX was still a scrappy challenger to Boeing and Lockheed Martin, and the Mars mission was broadly viewed as a distant aspiration rather than a near-term plan. The Falcon 9 had its first successful flight in 2010, and Dragon had just completed its first cargo mission to the ISS (May 2012).

Musk's core argument: humanity becoming a multi-planetary species is insurance against extinction-level events. Earth is a single point of failure. Mars is the nearest viable candidate for establishing a self-sustaining civilization. The economic path there requires radical reusability — rocket stages that land and fly again — because launch costs at existing prices make Mars colonization economically impossible.

Reading this in 2012 required taking several things on faith: that SpaceX could develop fully reusable rockets (the Falcon 9 wasn't yet), that the regulatory environment would permit commercial crewed flight, and that public interest and revenue from launch services could subsidize the Mars program. The Falcon 9 Full Thrust reusable first stage landing happened in December 2015; Starship development was still years off. The interview is a document of conviction preceding execution.

## Key points

- Elon Musk in 2012: SpaceX had proven orbital launch; reusable rockets were the stated next goal
- The multi-planetary argument: redundancy against extinction — Mars as civilization backup
- Economic thesis: radical reusability is necessary to reduce cost per ton to Mars by orders of magnitude
- Dragon had completed its first ISS cargo mission in May 2012 — SpaceX was 2 years old as an orbital company
- The Starlink revenue model that would fund Starship didn't exist yet — the business case for Mars was thinner in 2012

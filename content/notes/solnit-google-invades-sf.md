## Summary

Rebecca Solnit's diary piece in the London Review of Books (February 2013) was one of the essays that crystallized the emerging debate about tech industry wealth and gentrification in San Francisco. Solnit — a San Francisco-based writer known for Wanderlust, "A Field Guide to Getting Lost," and extensive urban writing — brought literary weight to what had been primarily a housing advocacy discourse.

Her central arguments: tech salaries were driving rental prices up 10-135% in some neighborhoods, displacing artists, teachers, service workers, and the cultural communities that made San Francisco distinctive. The private commuter buses that Google, Apple, and Facebook ran to their South Bay campuses were using public bus stops without contributing to public transit — a visible symbol of corporate infrastructure that bypassed civic systems. The tech industry was treating San Francisco as a consumption platform rather than a community they were part of.

Solnit's framing drew comparisons to historical resource extraction — Gold Rush, oil boom — where a boomtown swells with transient newcomers who prioritize extraction over community investment. The tech workers, in this reading, were not building San Francisco; they were depleting it, moving in with high incomes, bidding up housing, and moving out when the next opportunity arose.

## Key points

- Google buses (and Apple/Facebook equivalents) became the symbol of the tech gentrification critique: private infrastructure using public stops, serving a wealthy enclave that bypassed public transit
- Rental price increases of 10-135% across SF neighborhoods in 2012-2013 — driven primarily by tech salary concentration in a geographically constrained city
- Solnit's argument: San Francisco's cultural identity (refuge for dissidents, queers, artists, the unconventional) was being erased by monoculture wealth — not an inevitable market outcome but a political choice
- The Ellis Act eviction wave of 2013-2014 followed the pattern Solnit identified: landlords evicting long-term tenants to flip units at market rate
- The essay's publication in LRB gave the argument international reach — the San Francisco housing crisis became a global case study in tech-driven displacement

## Summary

This New England Journal of Medicine review by Rafael de Cabo and Mark Mattson (both NIH) synthesizes decades of animal research and growing clinical evidence for intermittent fasting (IF) as a distinct health intervention. The key reframe: most benefits aren't simply from calorie reduction or reduced oxidative stress — they stem from a specific metabolic switch that occurs when the body depletes liver glycogen and shifts to burning ketone bodies from fat. This switch happens daily or several times per week depending on the fasting protocol.

The metabolic switch triggers evolutionarily conserved cellular responses: improved insulin sensitivity, upregulation of autophagy (cellular cleanup), enhanced mitochondrial health, and activation of BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) pathways. These effects appear consistently across studies of time-restricted feeding, alternate-day fasting, and the 5:2 diet. Human clinical trials show reductions in blood pressure, inflammation markers, LDL cholesterol, and HbA1c — markers relevant to cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome.

The review also addresses longevity: animal studies in mice and non-human primates show consistent effects of caloric restriction on healthspan, and IF appears to capture many of these effects without requiring chronic restriction. Aging-related conditions like neurodegeneration, cancer susceptibility, and cardiovascular disease all show IF-related improvements in animal models, with early human evidence corroborating several of these findings.

## Key points

- Intermittent fasting works through a metabolic switch from glucose to ketone bodies — not merely from reduced calorie intake — activating stress-response pathways that improve cellular health.
- Core benefits include improved insulin sensitivity, enhanced autophagy, reduced inflammation, and increased BDNF — a neuroprotective factor with implications for cognitive decline and depression.
- Three major IF protocols (time-restricted feeding, alternate-day fasting, 5:2 diet) all produce similar mechanistic effects; protocol choice can be adapted to individual lifestyle.
- Human clinical evidence shows measurable improvements in blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, HbA1c, and inflammatory markers — relevant to major chronic disease risk.
- Long-term sustainability is the main open question: adherence is harder than caloric restriction and the optimal fasting window and protocol remain under investigation.

## Summary

Fast Company covered the emerging "data for good" movement in 2013 — practitioners applying data science skills to social sector problems: nonprofits, public health, urban planning, and humanitarian organizations that had data but lacked analytical capacity. The framing was mercenaries — people with valuable technical skills volunteering or contracting to work on social problems, bringing methods from tech industry to causes that couldn't compete for talent at market rates.

DataKind — founded by Jake Porterfield in 2012 — was the flagship example: connecting data scientists with nonprofits for project-based pro bono work. Similar organizations (Statistics Without Borders, university data science programs with community partners) were multiplying. The demand was real: most nonprofits collected substantial data but lacked the statistical and engineering skills to extract value from it.

The mercenary framing was slightly tongue-in-cheek — these were volunteers and low-paid social entrepreneurs, not hired guns. But the underlying idea was sound: data science had become a high-value SKILL set that could be applied outside its default commercial context (ad targeting, recommendation systems, fraud detection) to genuinely impactful problems. The data for good movement that followed — DSSG (Data Science for Social Good), Bloomberg Data for Good Exchange, city chief data officers — grew directly from this energy.

## Key points

- DataKind: the canonical data for good organization — data scientists working on nonprofit projects through weekend DataDives and longer engagements.
- Skill transfer challenge: what works in an ad-tech company doesn't always translate directly to nonprofit contexts — data quality, organizational capacity, and deployment infrastructure differ substantially.
- Open data movement: concurrent push for governments to release public data was complementary — more raw material for civic data science.
- Data Science for Social Good (DSSG): University of Chicago summer program training data scientists on civic and social problems — formalized the academic pipeline for data for good.
- The movement has matured: by 2020, most major cities had chief data officers; data for good had moved from volunteer projects to institutionalized civic tech.
- Tension: the "mercenary" model depends on volunteer time from practitioners who have day jobs — scaling impact required building institutional infrastructure, not just goodwill.

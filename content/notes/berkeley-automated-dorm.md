## Summary

Brad Kohlenberg, a UC Berkeley student, built what was described as one of the most automated dorm rooms ever created — automated lighting that responded to occupancy and time of day, temperature control, automated door lock/unlock via phone, music that played based on context, and various other sensor-driven features. The system apparently used a custom-built network of Raspberry Pi-class hardware (this was very early Raspberry Pi era), Arduino controllers, and custom software — a genuine small home automation system in a 12x12 foot dorm room.

The university sent officials to investigate after other students noticed the setup, and ultimately told Kohlenberg to remove parts of the system that they deemed safety concerns or policy violations. The story spread on Hacker News and tech media partly because of the obvious irony: the student body at UC Berkeley EECS included many of the world's most capable young hardware and software engineers, but the university was more comfortable with students doing nothing impressive in their rooms than doing something impressive that required bending the rules.

The story resonated as an example of the gap between institutional rules designed for typical users and what happens when unusually capable people encounter them. The dorm room automation project wasn't dangerous — it was, if anything, more careful than typical student wiring. But it violated the letter of policies designed to prevent typical students from burning down buildings. The maker culture vs. institutions friction this illustrated became a recurring theme as maker/hacker skills spread.

## Key points

- Automated lighting, temperature, door locks, music via phone — a complete home automation system in a dorm room.
- Built with early Arduino and similar microcontrollers; custom software integration.
- UC Berkeley officials forced removal of components citing safety/policy concerns.
- Became an HN story about institutions and their inability to distinguish between dangerous modification and impressive engineering.
- Context: 2012 was when home automation was moving from expensive custom installs toward DIY accessible systems.

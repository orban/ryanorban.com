## Summary

This GigaOm piece argued in early 2013 that enterprise IT was entering a genuinely interesting period — not just iterative improvement on SAP and Oracle incumbency, but a convergence of cloud computing, SaaS, mobile, and big data that was fundamentally reshaping how businesses operated their technology. The claim: enterprise IT was as interesting and fast-moving as consumer tech, and the wave would last.

The convergence of forces: Amazon Web Services had made infrastructure-as-a-service mainstream, allowing startups to build enterprise-grade products without data center capital. Salesforce had proven that SaaS could displace installed software even in conservative enterprise accounts. Mobile (iPhone and Android enterprise adoption accelerating in 2012-2013) meant the endpoint had changed — enterprise apps needed to work on devices IT didn't control. And Hadoop, NoSQL, and the emerging data science profession were giving enterprises analytical capabilities that would have required expensive vendor software a decade earlier.

The argument that it'll be here for a while was a response to the common dismissal that cloud/SaaS was a cyclical bubble. GigaOm's analysis (roughly correct, though they went bankrupt in 2015): the structural shift was durable because it was economic — cloud was genuinely cheaper, SaaS meant faster updates without IT project cycles, and mobile endpoint adoption was too rapid to resist. The companies that would win weren't the incumbents trying to cloud-wash existing products but new entrants like Workday, ServiceNow, and Box built cloud-native from the start.

## Key points

- Cloud economics: AWS commoditized infrastructure capital expenditure — enterprise software companies could launch without data center investment.
- SaaS disruption: Salesforce model proved large enterprises would use subscription software — faster updates, no IT project cycles.
- Mobile enterprise: BYOD and iPhone/Android adoption in enterprises changed the endpoint assumption — apps had to be mobile-first.
- Big data as enterprise capability: Hadoop, Redshift, and the data science role gave enterprises analytical power previously requiring expensive BI vendors.
- New entrants vs. incumbents: Workday, ServiceNow, Box, Veeva were cloud-native and would win against legacy players cloud-washing existing products.

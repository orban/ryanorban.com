## Summary

NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory released a series of retro-styled travel posters for exoplanets in early 2015 — rendered in the aesthetic of 1920s-1930s Works Progress Administration (WPA) national park posters. The posters imagined what tourism advertising might look like for planets like Kepler-186f, HD 40307g, and Kepler-16b (the land of two suns), each designed with the real physical characteristics of those worlds in mind.

The project is a standout example of science communication through design. Exoplanets had been discovered for two decades by 2015 but remained largely abstract to the public — distant statistics, not places. The WPA travel poster format, which carries emotional resonance from mid-century America, made them feel concrete and imaginable without distorting the science. The physical characteristics (surface gravity, star type, orbital period) were incorporated into each poster's visual and textual design.

The posters became one of NASA's most-shared content pieces, downloaded millions of times and widely printed. The lesson for science communication and data visualization more broadly: aesthetic framing changes what people engage with. The same information that reads as a dry astrophysics abstract becomes emotionally resonant when rendered through the vocabulary of desire (travel, adventure, discovery).

## Key points

- WPA travel poster aesthetic applied to exoplanet science — emotional resonance from familiar visual vocabulary.
- Each poster incorporates real physical characteristics: star type, surface conditions, gravity.
- One of NASA JPL's most successful science communication pieces — millions of downloads.
- Lesson: aesthetic framing determines engagement. Same science, radically different reach.
- Connects to data visualization principle: the frame affects what people feel, not just what they know.
- Free download: NASA made them available as high-resolution prints, which drove viral spread.

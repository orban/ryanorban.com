## Summary

This tweet from jdh (@jdh) is a compressed observation about personal growth and what changes when someone succeeds or improves: "I think, but I am not sure, that the only thing that changed was my sense of self importance?"

The framing is doing interesting work. The uncertainty (I think, but I am not sure) signals epistemic humility — this is offered as a hypothesis, not a conclusion. The observation itself cuts against common narratives about success: we tend to attribute improvement to SKILL development, mindset shifts, discipline, or external circumstances. But what if the primary change is simpler and more ego-adjacent — just feeling more important, more capable of acting, more permission-granting to yourself?

The implication: imposter syndrome and lack of confidence might be more fundamental blockers than actual skill gaps, and what "growth" really produces in many cases is a revised self-model that gives you permission to act differently. This is related to self-efficacy research (Bandura) — belief in your capability to perform a task affects performance more directly than actual capability level. The note the user added — "this <--" — suggests it resonated as a real observation about their own experience.

## Key points

- What if success primarily changes your sense of self-importance, not your actual capabilities?
- Epistemic humility in the framing — a hypothesis held loosely, not a confident claim.
- Related to self-efficacy: belief in capability affects performance independent of actual capability.
- The permission frame: confidence grants permission to act in ways you'd have hesitated to act before.
- Short, compressed format — the kind of observation that's easy to dismiss and hard to forget.

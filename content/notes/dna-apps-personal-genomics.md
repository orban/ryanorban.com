## Summary

In 2012, 23andMe and similar direct-to-consumer genomics companies were beginning to open their APIs to third-party developers. ReadWrite covered the emerging ecosystem of apps designed to build on top of personal genomic data — ancestry tools, health risk calculators, nutrition guidance, and even social networks organized around shared genetic variants.

The underlying bet was that consumer genomics would follow the smartphone app platform model: a data layer (23andMe, later AncestryDNA) would enable a developer ecosystem building specialized applications on top. The first wave of apps focused on things like pharmacogenomics (how your genetic variants affect drug metabolism), nutrigenomics (dietary recommendations based on MTHFR, APOE, and other variants), and ancestry composition visualizations.

What happened instead: the FDA's 2013 enforcement action against 23andMe shut down most health-related features for years, significantly dampening the third-party developer ecosystem. Personal genomics survived but evolved toward ancestry rather than health, with the health applications gradually returning under stricter regulatory frameworks. The app ecosystem predicted here mostly didn't materialize at the pace anticipated.

## Key points

- 23andMe opening developer APIs in 2012 created brief optimism for a personal genomics platform layer
- Early app categories: pharmacogenomics (drug response), nutrigenomics (diet/nutrition), ancestry visualization, social matching by shared SNPs
- Privacy concerns were nascent — genomic data is uniquely permanent and familially revealing, unlike passwords or credit cards
- FDA enforcement in 2013 froze 23andMe's health features, effectively killing the developer ecosystem being previewed here
- George Church and others were simultaneously building the Personal Genome Project — a public, consented genome database as research infrastructure

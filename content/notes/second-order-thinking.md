## Summary

Second-order thinking is the practice of tracing consequences beyond immediate effects — asking and then what? repeatedly to map how a decision ripples through interconnected systems over time. Farnam Street presents it as a foundational mental model for strategic decision-making, drawing primarily on investor Howard Marks (Oaktree Capital).

The distinction from first-order thinking is practical: first-order thinking stops at the obvious answer ("take the pain reliever, it stops the headache"). Second-order thinking asks what follows ("what if you take them every time you're uncomfortable? what does that do to pain tolerance over time?"). Most people stop at first-order because it's faster and sufficient for low-stakes decisions; second-order thinking becomes essential when decisions affect complex systems or have long time horizons.

The Farnam Street essay uses the example of a government funding moderate rebels to spark regime change, who later become adversaries — a classic second-order failure where the 10-year consequence wasn't considered alongside the immediate-term effect. The framework: map consequences at 10 minutes / 10 months / 10 years, and across all relevant stakeholders. Connects to systems thinking and unintended consequences.

## Key points

- Second-order thinking: trace consequences past immediate effects by repeatedly asking "and then what?"
- First-order thinking is fast and works for most decisions; second-order is necessary for complex systems.
- Howard Marks (Oaktree Capital) is credited with formalizing the concept for investment contexts.
- Map effects at three timeframes: 10 minutes / 10 months / 10 years simultaneously.
- Consider cascading effects across all stakeholder groups, not just direct consequences.
- Connects to systems thinking, feedback loops, and unintended consequences frameworks.

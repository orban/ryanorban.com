## Summary

Robert Read's guide covers the full range of what it takes to be a good programmer — not just technical SKILL, but the judgment, communication, and organizational savvy that separate effective engineers from merely competent ones. It was originally written for his colleagues and has circulated widely since as one of the better articulations of the non-technical dimension of software development.

The guide is organized around three areas: personal skills (how to work), team skills (how to collaborate), and judgment (how to make good decisions under uncertainty). The personal skills section covers practical debugging wisdom — how to think systematically about bugs, how to use binary search on code, how to ask for help effectively. The judgment sections are the most valuable: when to use which tool, when not to over-engineer, when to fight for something and when to accept constraints.

What makes it unusual is its directness about the political and human dimensions of engineering: how to work with difficult people, how to manage your own learning, how to evaluate managers. These are things junior programmers need to know but rarely learn formally, and the guide treats them as first-class concerns rather than soft skills footnotes.

## Key points

- Practical debugging methodology: systematic elimination, divide and conquer, reading error messages carefully, not assuming you understand the system.
- Communication skills as core engineering skill: asking good questions, writing clear documentation, explaining technical decisions to non-technical stakeholders.
- Judgment under uncertainty: when a problem is worth solving vs. working around, when to refactor vs. ship, how to recognize technical debt accumulating.
- Team dynamics: giving and receiving criticism, working with difficult people, understanding what managers actually need from engineers.
- The hardest problems in programming are often human, not technical — good software requires good judgment about both.

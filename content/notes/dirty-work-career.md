## Summary

Stay SaaSy's piece on the Dirty Work Theory: the lamentable, avoided, unglamorous tasks in an organization are consistently underpriced from a career perspective. Most people optimize for visible, interesting work. The dirty work — the support escalations, the migration nobody wants to do, the legacy system everyone complains about but avoids, the vendor relationship that's a mess — has low competition and high organizational value.

The argument is structural: when everyone avoids something, the counterfactual of doing it is large. The person who handles the painful on-call rotation, fixes the flaky test suite, or owns the relationship with the most difficult customer builds credibility and trust faster than someone doing the same level of work in a more pleasant domain. The career risk is low (it's already a mess), and the upside is outsized relative to effort.

This connects to contrarian investing logic applied to careers — find where the market is underweighting value due to aesthetic preferences. It also relates to Warren Buffett's preference for simple businesses in boring industries and Paul Graham's advice to do things that don't scale. The willingness to do dirty work is a moat because it's hard to fake and self-reinforcing: doing it once builds the credibility and tolerance to keep doing it.

## Key points

- Dirty work is structurally underpriced: low competition, high organizational value, large counterfactual impact.
- The avoided task (legacy migration, escalation ownership, painful vendor) is often the highest-leverage place to work.
- Career risk is low — it's already a problem; your involvement has limited downside.
- Builds trust and credibility faster than equally-skilled work in a more pleasant domain.
- Applies contrarian logic to career strategy: optimize for where taste preferences suppress competition.
- Willingness to do dirty work is durable — hard to fake, compounds over time.

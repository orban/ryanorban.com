## Summary

Fast Company's 2014 profile documents Sebastian Thrun openly acknowledging that Udacity's original MOOC model wasn't delivering on its promise. Despite enormous initial enrollment (160,000 students in the first AI course), completion rates were under 10% and learning outcomes were weak — Thrun called it "a lousy product." The pivot: away from university-style courses toward short, vocational Nanodegrees built in partnership with employers like Google and AT&T.

The story was unusually candid for a startup founder admitting failure mid-run. It landed at a moment when MOOC hype was peaking and the hard questions about completion rates, credential value, and who actually finishes were just starting to be asked publicly.

## Key points

- Udacity's first course (Introduction to AI with Thrun and Peter Norvig) enrolled 160,000 students — less than 10% completed it
- Low completion rates across MOOCs were a systemic problem, not a Udacity-specific one, but Thrun was the first to say so explicitly
- Pivot to Nanodegrees: shorter, vocationally focused, with employer-recognized credentials and mentorship components
- Employer partnerships (Google, AT&T, others) provided both curriculum input and hiring pipelines — making completion more instrumental
- The pivot acknowledged a real insight: unbundling university education works better for SKILL acquisition than for credentialing or community

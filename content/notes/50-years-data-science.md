## Summary

David Donoho's 2015 essay (later published in the *Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics*) makes the case that data science is a legitimate intellectual discipline, not just a rebranding of statistics or a label for people who use Python to run regressions. He traces the argument from John Tukey's 1962 paper "The Future of Data Analysis" through work by Leo Breiman, John Chambers, and Bill Cleveland, showing that the push toward empiricism, computation, and scale has been happening inside statistics for decades — just under the surface.

Donoho distinguishes "Greater Data Science" from classical statistics by its scope: it includes not just inference but data acquisition, transformation, exploration, modeling, communication, and the metatask of improving the practice itself. This is essentially the data science pipeline that practitioners recognize but statisticians often dismiss as engineering, not science.

The essay's sharpest point is about academic statistics failing to claim its own intellectual territory. While universities debated whether machine learning belonged in statistics or computer science departments, practitioners were building the tools and methods that actually mattered. Donoho argues this was a strategic and intellectual failure — and that data science as taught in bootcamps and practiced at tech companies is closer to Tukey's vision of the future of data analysis than anything produced by mainstream academic statistics departments.

## Key points

- Traces "data science" back to John Tukey's 1962 Future of Data Analysis — not a recent invention.
- Defines Greater Data Science as 6 divisions: data exploration, data representation, data transformation, data modeling, data visualization, and science about data science.
- Critiques academic statistics for ceding machine learning to computer science departments.
- Leo Breiman's "Two Cultures" (2001) is a key predecessor: predictive vs. inferential modeling.
- Argues the data science bootcamp/tech industry definition aligns better with Tukey's vision than academic stats.
- Still relevant: the tension between statistics-as-inference and statistics-as-prediction defines many institutional and methodological debates today.

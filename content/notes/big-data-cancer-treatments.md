## Summary

By 2013, a few major cancer centers — Memorial Sloan Kettering, MD Anderson, Mayo Clinic — were beginning systematic programs to apply big data methods to oncology. The basic approach: combine genomic sequencing of tumor samples with electronic medical records of treatment protocols and outcomes, then look for patterns between tumor genetic profiles and treatment responses. The hypothesis was that cancer isn't one disease but hundreds, defined by specific mutation patterns, and that matching treatments to molecular profiles rather than tissue of origin would improve outcomes.

IBM Watson for Oncology was the highest-profile attempt in this direction — trained on Memorial Sloan Kettering's treatment protocols, it was supposed to recommend treatment options to oncologists at hospitals that didn't have MSK-level expertise. The promise was compelling: give every patient access to the reasoning of the world's best cancer hospital. The reality, which wasn't fully apparent in 2013, was that Watson's training data was MSK's existing protocols (human-curated, not evidence-based), and its recommendations often didn't generalize or match clinical trial evidence.

The more durable 2013 developments were in genomic data infrastructure: The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) was compiling comprehensive molecular characterizations of 33 tumor types, and cancer genomics platforms like cBioPortal were making this data accessible to researchers. These open data initiatives created the foundation for the genuine precision oncology that emerged later — using genomic biomarkers to match patients to targeted therapies and clinical trials.

## Key points

- Precision oncology: matching treatment to tumor molecular profile rather than tissue of origin — the organizing principle of cancer big data in 2013.
- The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA): comprehensive genomic characterization of 33 tumor types — the foundational open dataset for cancer genomics research.
- IBM Watson for Oncology: high-profile but ultimately flawed attempt to distill MSK expertise into an AI recommendation system.
- Electronic health records integration: combining genomic data with treatment outcomes required integrating previously siloed clinical and laboratory systems.
- cBioPortal: open-source cancer genomics data portal making TCGA and other datasets accessible — a durable contribution to research infrastructure.

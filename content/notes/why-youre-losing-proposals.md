## Summary

Jeff Archibald runs a web agency and writes about the business of freelancing and consulting. This post diagnoses why proposals fail — the answer he arrives at is that most freelancers lose proposals not on price or quality of work, but on failure to demonstrate they understand the client's actual problem and risks.

The core argument: clients making a purchasing decision for a service are buying risk management, not just deliverables. They're worried the project will go over budget, run late, or not deliver what they need. A proposal that only lists deliverables and prices without explicitly addressing these fears leaves the client's anxious questions unanswered — and they'll choose someone who does answer them, even if that someone is more expensive or less technically skilled.

The practical implication: proposals should surface and address client concerns directly, not just outline scope and timeline. Show you understand what success looks like from the client's perspective, what could go wrong, and how you handle it when it does. This reframes the proposal from a quote document to a trust-building document.

Relevant to Zipfian Academy's context in 2014: graduates entering data science consulting roles or pitching data projects internally faced exactly this dynamic — technical people who could build excellent things but struggled to communicate value to non-technical decision makers.

## Key points

- Proposals fail most often on risk communication, not price or SKILL.
- Clients buying services are buying risk management — they want evidence you've anticipated what can go wrong.
- A proposal that only lists deliverables leaves client fears unaddressed.
- Show understanding of the client's definition of success, not just your own.
- Reframe the proposal as a trust document, not a quote document.
- The diagnosis applies equally to internal pitches: data scientists presenting projects to business stakeholders.

## Summary

Ivan Mazour (co-founder and CEO of Ometria, a UK retail marketing platform) argued in this post that the ability to speak well in public is the single most asymmetric SKILL investment available to ambitious people. His reasoning: public speaking is required in essentially every high-leverage situation — fundraising, hiring, selling, leading a team, influencing an industry — yet most people have significant anxiety about it and invest almost no deliberate effort in improving.

The unfair advantage framing captures the asymmetry: because most people avoid developing this skill, someone who becomes genuinely good at it gains leverage in every context where communication matters. The compounding effect is large — a founder who can communicate a vision compellingly raises more money, attracts better people, and closes larger customers than an equally smart founder who can't. The skill multiplies rather than adds.

Mazour's practical advice: join Toastmasters or equivalent, seek out every opportunity to speak (company all-hands, meetup talks, conference panels), and treat each one as deliberate practice rather than a performance. The anxiety response decreases with exposure; the skill builds faster than most people expect.

The argument connects to the broader idea in career development that improving meta-skills (communication, learning rapidly, building trust) has higher returns than improving domain skills directly, because meta-skills apply across all domains.

## Key points

- Public speaking as a meta-skill: required in fundraising, hiring, selling, leading — improves returns on every other capability
- Asymmetric investment: most people avoid it, so competence is relatively rare despite being learnable
- Toastmasters: structured deliberate practice environment for public speaking; Mazour's recommended path
- Anxiety decreases with exposure; the initial investment is uncomfortable but the acquisition curve is steep
- The "unfair advantage" framing: skills that compound across many contexts are worth disproportionate investment compared to domain-specific skills

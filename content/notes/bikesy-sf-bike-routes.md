## Summary

Bikesy was a San Francisco-specific bike routing tool that Ryan described as his new favorite website in 2012. Its key differentiator from Google Maps (which had only recently added basic bike directions) was that it explicitly optimized for elevation — routing cyclists around San Francisco's steep hills rather than just taking the shortest path, and showing elevation profiles for each route.

San Francisco's topography makes elevation a first-class concern for cyclists in a way that flat cities don't require. The city has over 40 named hills, many with grades exceeding 10-15%. The difference between a 1.5-mile route that climbs 300 feet and a 2-mile route that stays flat is significant for a daily commuter. Bikesy used OpenStreetMap data and elevation models to generate routes that balanced distance with climbing, with configurable preferences for how much hill-avoidance mattered to you.

This was a good example of civic hacking — a specialized tool built to serve a specific community (SF cyclists) using open data (OpenStreetMap, public elevation data) that the major mapping providers hadn't yet optimized for. Google Maps eventually improved its bike routing with elevation awareness around 2014-2015. By then, Bikesy had served as a proof of concept that specialist users were willing to leave the dominant platform for a tool that actually understood their use case.

## Key points

- Bikesy optimized bike routes for elevation, not just distance — crucial in San Francisco's hilly topography.
- Used OpenStreetMap data and elevation models; configurable hill-avoidance preferences.
- Built before Google Maps added meaningful elevation awareness to bike routing (~2014-2015).
- Example of civic hacking: open data + specialist need + community that major platforms underserved.
- San Francisco has 40+ named hills with grades to 15% — elevation routing matters far more than flat cities.
- Demonstrated demand for specialized routing tools that understood local geography better than general-purpose maps.

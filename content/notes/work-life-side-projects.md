## Summary

This 2012 Smashing Magazine piece addressed the challenge of sustaining side projects alongside a full-time job — a question that was increasingly relevant as the internet made independent creation more viable and the indie hacker and maker cultures were beginning to crystallize. The core argument: side projects matter not just for their potential to become businesses, but for the intrinsic value of working on something that's entirely yours, without the constraints of institutional approval or commercial viability.

The practical challenge is time and cognitive load. After a full day of professional work, the mental energy required for creative or technically demanding side projects is often depleted. Successful side project practitioners tend to either: (a) protect morning time before the work day begins, (b) use commute or transit time for planning even if not execution, or (c) treat project time with the same scheduling rigidity as professional commitments. The key insight is that waiting for free time that appears reliably produces no output — side projects require deliberately carved space.

A secondary challenge is scope management. Side projects are particularly vulnerable to what Smashing calls ambition inflation: the project expands to fill imagined possibility rather than remaining anchored to a concrete, achievable first version. The counterintuitive discipline is to artificially constrain scope — build the version that teaches you what you need to know, not the version you'd want to have eventually. Ship something, get feedback, iterate. This applies whether the goal is a SaaS product, a writing project, an open source library, or a physical making project. The article situates this in the context of web design and development, but the pattern generalizes.

## Key points

- Side projects provide intrinsic value beyond their commercial potential: autonomy, creative expression, technical exploration without institutional constraints.
- Time scarcity is the primary blocker — successful practitioners protect specific blocks (mornings, transit) rather than waiting for time to appear.
- Cognitive load after full-time work is real: complex technical or creative work requires mental energy that employment can deplete.
- Scope creep is the greatest threat to completion: artificially constraining scope to a first deliverable is necessary discipline.
- 2012 context: pre-Product Hunt, pre-Indie Hackers, but the cultural moment was emerging — making, shipping, and indie creation were becoming identity markers in tech.
- The advice is structurally similar to writing advice: time matters less than consistency; constraint is a feature, not a limitation.

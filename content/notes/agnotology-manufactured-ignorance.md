## Summary

Agnotology is a term coined by historian Robert Proctor to name the study of deliberate ignorance production — how doubt, uncertainty, and not-knowing are actively manufactured rather than simply existing as the absence of knowledge. The book *Agnotology: The Making and Unmaking of Ignorance*, edited by Proctor and Londa Schiebinger, collects essays examining cases where powerful interests invested in creating and maintaining ignorance as a strategic tool.

The paradigm case is the tobacco industry. For decades, cigarette companies knew that smoking caused lung cancer — their own internal research confirmed it. Rather than accept this finding, they funded counter-research, hired scientists to produce ambiguous studies, and ran a coordinated public relations campaign to manufacture doubt. The goal wasn't to prove cigarettes were safe — it was to maintain a state of public uncertainty sufficient to forestall regulation. Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway's later book *Merchants of Doubt* extended this analysis to climate change denial using many of the same actors and playbook.

Agnotology as a framework is more analytically useful than simply calling something misinformation because it focuses on the production process and the interests served by ignorance. Who benefits from people not knowing? Who funded the doubt? The concept applies broadly: fossil fuel industries, pharmaceutical companies suppressing negative trial data, and tech platforms optimizing for engagement over accuracy all have structural interests in certain kinds of ignorance.

## Key points

- Agnotology: the study of how ignorance is produced, not just how knowledge is acquired.
- The tobacco industry is the paradigm case: internal research confirmed cancer risk, but the companies manufactured public doubt.
- Ignorance is a resource that can be created, maintained, and deployed for political and commercial ends.
- Connects to Naomi Oreskes's *Merchants of Doubt* — same playbook used for climate change denial.
- The framework shifts analysis from who's lying to who benefits from uncertainty.
- Epistemic cowardice (deliberate vagueness to avoid controversy) and manufactured ignorance are related phenomena.

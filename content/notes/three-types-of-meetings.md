## Summary

Cam Daigle offers a three-type classification system for meetings: a survival mechanism, as he calls it, for staying sane in a meeting-dense work environment. The framework works as a forcing function — before scheduling or accepting a meeting, you categorize it, and the category determines how to prepare and what success looks like.

The three types roughly correspond to: (1) information sharing where one party has something to convey to another, (2) collaborative work where the output requires multiple people in the room together, and (3) decision-making where a choice needs to be made and documented. Each type has different failure modes: information-sharing meetings fail when they become discussion-heavy; collaborative meetings fail when a solo owner isn't designated; decision meetings fail when there's no documented outcome.

The underlying insight is that most meeting dysfunction comes from type mismatch — treating a decision meeting like an information share (no decision gets made) or treating a collaborative session like a check-in (the wrong people are in the room). Naming the type before scheduling surfaces the mismatch early.

## Key points

- Three types: information share, collaborative work, decision-making.
- Each type has distinct preparation, attendance, and success criteria.
- Type mismatch is the root cause of most meeting dysfunction.
- Works as a scheduling pre-mortem: naming the type reveals whether the meeting is necessary.
- Pairs with other meeting hygiene frameworks like Jeff Bezos's six-pager memo culture.
- Decision meetings need a documented outcome or they default to information-sharing mode.

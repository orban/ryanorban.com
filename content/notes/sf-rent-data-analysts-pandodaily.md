## Summary

In early 2014, San Francisco rent had become one of the most-discussed topics in the tech industry — a direct consequence of the city's second tech boom displacing longtime residents. PandoDaily covered data analysts who were applying predictive modeling to rental listing data from Craigslist, Zillow, and other sources to forecast where rents were heading.

The analysis reflects the era's enthusiasm for using publicly-accessible scraped web data as a proxy for real estate market dynamics. Rental listing databases are imperfect (not all rentals list publicly, listed price ≠ actual price paid) but frequent enough to provide signal. Time-series analysis of neighborhood-level median rents, combined with leading indicators like new building permits, tech company office openings, and commute patterns, gave data scientists tools to produce at least directional forecasts.

The San Francisco housing story in 2014 was simultaneously a policy debate, a tech culture critique, and a data problem. Projects like this occupied the intersection: the data science community had the tools, the Bay Area had the urgency, and platforms like Craigslist had the data. It anticipates later work by companies like Zillow, Redfin, and Opendoor that built full real estate pricing models as core products.

## Key points

- Craigslist and listing sites as data sources: rental price time series at neighborhood granularity — imperfect but actionable signal.
- Predictive modeling on real estate: trend extrapolation, leading indicators (permits, employment data), and neighborhood-level heterogeneity.
- San Francisco rent crisis context: 2014 median rent for a 1BR had passed $2,500, up 50%+ since 2010 — an urgent applied forecasting problem.
- Data quality challenges: listings don't represent all rental transactions, price points self-select, and actual signed rents are rarely public.
- Anticipates modern real estate data platforms: Zillow's Zestimate, Opendoor's offer algorithm, Redfin's market analysis tools.

## Summary

Nanomagnetic logic (NML) is a computing paradigm that uses the magnetic orientation of nanoscale magnets — rather than electron flow — to represent and process binary information. Unlike CMOS circuits where logic is fixed in silicon at fabrication, magnetic logic gates can be reconfigured after manufacture by applying a magnetic field to flip the state of individual magnetic domains. The Nature News piece covered research showing this could be made to work reliably at room temperature, a prerequisite for practical chips.

The mutable chips framing captured what was genuinely new: a chip whose logic function could be changed in the field, without replacing hardware. This is related to but distinct from field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs), which are reprogrammable in software but use static CMOS hardware underneath. NML would allow reconfigurability at the physical level, and it operates non-volatilely — the magnetic state persists without power.

The practical appeal in 2013 was partly security (a chip could be reconfigured to present a different attack surface) and partly energy (magnetic switching requires less energy than charging a CMOS capacitor). The research was early-stage; the systems were demonstrated in laboratory settings with strong external fields, nowhere near the density or speed of production CMOS.

## Key points

- Nanomagnetic logic: binary computation using magnetic domain orientation rather than electrical charge — logic state is physically encoded in the magnet
- Non-volatile computing: magnetic state persists without power, unlike DRAM or SRAM — important for low-power and edge computing
- Reconfigurable hardware without software abstraction: change the magnetic field, change the chip's function — distinct from FPGAs
- Room-temperature operation was the key 2013 milestone; prior work required cryogenic conditions
- Energy efficiency argument: magnetic switching avoids charging/discharging capacitors — potential for sub-threshold computing

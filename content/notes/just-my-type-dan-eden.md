## Summary

Dan Eden — a designer known for clean, considered work — wrote this essay on typography at a moment when the web was finally getting good type tools. Google Fonts had launched in 2010, `@font-face` was becoming reliable across browsers, and designers were no longer stuck with the handful of web-safe fonts. With that newfound freedom came the question: how do you choose well?

The essay addresses the craft of type selection and type pairing: understanding the difference between serif and sans-serif in different contexts, the personalities that different typefaces carry, and how whitespace and line-height shape readability as much as the font itself. Web typography in 2012 was a discipline being rediscovered — the rules from print didn't transfer directly, but they weren't irrelevant either.

CSS gave designers control over `font-size`, `line-height`, `letter-spacing`, and `font-weight`, but the decision-making — which font says what you want it to say — remained a matter of taste and education. Pieces like this one served as practical type education for a generation of developers-turned-designers working on the early web.

## Key points

- Type pairing is about contrast and harmony — combining a display face with a body face that don't fight each other.
- Line height and measure (line length) matter as much as font choice for readable body text.
- The shift to web fonts via Google Fonts and Typekit opened up vocabulary that was previously print-only.
- Serif typefaces work well for long-form reading; sans-serif tends to perform better at small sizes on screen.
- Typography is one of the highest-leverage design decisions — it sets tone before any content is read.

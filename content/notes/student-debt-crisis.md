## Summary

This BuzzFeed News analysis by Molly Hensley-Clancy challenges the dominant narrative about student debt: that the crisis is caused by rising tuition at four-year universities. The actual data tells a different story. The worst debt outcomes come from for-profit colleges and from students who borrowed to attend community colleges or four-year schools but never graduated — not from graduates of expensive elite universities.

The mechanism: graduates with high debt from expensive schools tend to have high earnings that make their debt manageable. The people in genuine financial distress are those who borrowed to attend a for-profit or community college, got inadequate job market preparation or never finished, and now carry debt with no corresponding credential or wage increase. The median debt load causing defaults is relatively small — often under $20,000 — not the six-figure loans that dominate media coverage.

This reframes the higher education policy debate significantly. Proposals focused on making elite universities cheaper (free community college, loan forgiveness tied to income) address the headline-grabbing cases, not the statistically common ones. The crisis is really about for-profit education predation and credential completion rates — problems that don't fit the "student loan = Silicon Valley grad paying off Stanford" narrative.

## Key points

- High-debt/high-default borrowers are concentrated in for-profit colleges, not elite universities.
- Many defaults involve borrowers who never completed degrees — debt without credential value.
- Median default debt is often under $20K — not the six-figure loans in media narratives.
- Elite university graduates have high debt but also high earnings, making repayment manageable.
- Policy implication: loan forgiveness targeted at income helps the wrong population; completion rates and for-profit regulation matter more.
- Connects to the broader education ROI debate: the value of a degree depends enormously on which institution and whether you finish.

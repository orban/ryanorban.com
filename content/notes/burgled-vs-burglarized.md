## Summary

This English Language and Usage Stack Exchange answer explores the British English vs American English divergence around the back-formation burgled and the longer form burglarized. In British English, "burgled" is standard — formed by back-formation from burglar, treating it like other agent nouns (you can be "burgled" the same way you can be mugged or robbed). In American English, burglarized became the standard form, which is unusual because it adds the -ize suffix to an already-complex word.

The interesting historical question, flagged in the tweet that prompted this save, is what happened in the 1960s. Google Ngram data shows a clear divergence point where "burglarized" began rising sharply in American usage while staying flat in British usage. The timing may correspond to the rise of crime reporting in American newspapers and police procedural language — law enforcement terminology tends to favor longer, more formal-sounding forms (burglarize, homicide, perpetrator) over their shorter equivalents.

The broader linguistics point is that back-formation (deriving a shorter word from a longer one, like edit from editor or donate from donation) competes with suffixation (adding endings to existing forms) as a word-formation strategy, and the two strategies can produce competing forms that divide along national or register lines.

## Key points

- Back-formation: burgle from burglar — same process as "edit" from editor, laze from "lazy"
- British English standard: "burgled" (back-formed); American English standard: "burglarized" (suffixed)
- The 1960s divergence in Google Ngrams suggests the American form was reinforced by formal/legal/police language in that era
- Both are grammatically valid; "burglarized" sounds formal and slightly verbose to British speakers; "burgled" sounds informal or quaint to American ones
- Register plays a role: formal writing and legal/police contexts favor "burglarized" even in the US

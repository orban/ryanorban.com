## Summary

A detailed genetic analysis of how the wizarding gene in the Harry Potter universe could be biologically consistent with a singular, autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, despite the existence of apparent exceptions like Muggle-born witches and wizards and Squibs (non-magical children of magical parents). The analysis was shared by Jimmy Wales (Wikipedia founder) on Twitter with the note I love the Internet.

The puzzle: if magical ability were simply dominant, you'd expect every child of a wizard to have magic (assuming the wizard has even one dominant allele). But Squibs exist — children of two magical parents who can't do magic. And Muggle-born witches like Hermione Granger appear from entirely non-magical families. The paper's resolution involved incomplete penetrance and expressivity — the magical allele is present in many "Muggles" but simply not expressed, explaining why magical children occasionally appear from apparently non-magical families (dormant alleles expressing after multiple generations).

The broader value of the piece was as science communication — it demonstrated that Mendelian genetics, penetrance, and expressivity concepts could be taught through a pop-culture lens that made the underlying biology memorable. The author's decision to actually write and send this to J.K. Rowling was a nice touch. The analysis also implicitly suggested that the wizard/Muggle distinction is fuzzier than the books imply — most "Muggles" may carry the dormant allele.

## Key points

- Singular autosomal dominant gene model can explain wizarding ability if incomplete penetrance is assumed.
- Squibs explained by the dominant allele failing to express (penetrance failure) in that individual.
- Muggle-born witches and wizards explained by dormant recessive alleles activating after multiple latent generations.
- Incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity are real genetic phenomena — not invented for the HP analysis.
- The Pure-blood vs Muggle-born distinction may be biologically meaningless — most Muggles may carry the dormant gene.
- Demonstrates the power of using familiar fictional universes as context for teaching population genetics.

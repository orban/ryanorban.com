## Summary

By March 2013, infographics had become a content marketing commodity. Every blog post was being turned into a visual flowchart; every statistic spawned a shareable image. HBR's critique was that the medium had become its own justification — infographics were being produced because they got shares, not because they communicated something better than text would have.

The piece diagnosed a real problem with data visualization: visual representation can make weak evidence look authoritative. A well-designed chart with clean typography and vibrant colors signals "this was researched" even when the underlying data is thin. Correlation arrows and percentage circles give a sense of precision that the source data may not support. The infographic boom of 2011–2013 produced a lot of visually compelling misinformation precisely because the format bypassed critical reading habits that text provoked.

The deeper critique was about the difference between informing and impressing. The best data visualization — Edward Tufte's famous examples, Florence Nightingale's coxcomb charts, John Snow's cholera map — revealed structure in data that was genuinely invisible in table form. Most 2013 infographics were the opposite: they turned simple facts into elaborate visual productions that were harder to read than a paragraph. The medium that was supposed to make data more accessible was making simple information more opaque.

## Key points

- Infographics proliferated as a content marketing tactic — shared widely, produced cheaply, but often conveying less information than equivalent text.
- The format's visual authority can make weak or misleading data look credible — a known failure mode in data visualization.
- Edward Tufte's principle of data-ink ratio: every ink mark should represent data, not decoration. Most 2013 infographics violated this badly.
- The critique still applies in 2024: dashboard culture, AI-generated charts, and social media data storytelling have the same pathology at larger scale.
- Good data visualization reveals structure invisible in table form; bad data visualization obscures simple facts behind visual complexity.

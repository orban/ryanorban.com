## Summary

This tweet by Glen Chiacchieri (@Glench) showed the result of a simple find-and-replace: swapping "recurrent neural network" with dog in ML research and journalism text. The resulting sentences remained semantically plausible — "we trained a dog to generate text," "the dog learned to recognize patterns" — which revealed something real about how loosely the term was being used.

It's a compact demonstration of what philosophers call the substitution test for meaning: if you can swap a technical term for something absurd and the sentence still parses, the term may not be doing much work. In 2015, neural network had become attached to almost any ML result that performed well, regardless of whether the architectural details were relevant to the finding being reported.

The joke circulated widely in the ML community partly because it landed on a genuine frustration: AI hype was already causing terminology inflation, making it harder to communicate actual technical results. When a headline saying "neural network beats humans at X" could equally mean anything from a 2-layer MLP to a carefully tuned 100-layer ResNet, something important is lost.

## Key points

- Find-and-replace recurrent neural network → "dog" and the text still reads coherently — indicating vague usage.
- 2015 early example of ML terminology inflation critique.
- The substitution test: if swapping a term for something absurd still parses, the term isn't load-bearing.
- Reflects the AI hype cycle beginning: technically-accurate-but-uninformative use of ML jargon in press coverage.

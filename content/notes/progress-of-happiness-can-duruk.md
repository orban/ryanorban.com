## Summary

Can Duruk (a Turkish-American software engineer and writer) explores the relationship between progress and happiness: the argument that happiness derives more reliably from the *experience of moving forward* than from achieving specific goals. The destination is less emotionally potent than the trajectory.

This connects to well-documented findings in positive psychology — hedonic adaptation means that achieved goals quickly become the new baseline, and the satisfaction they produce fades faster than anticipated. What sustains motivation and wellbeing is a sense of agency and forward movement, not arrival. Daniel Kahneman's work on the experiencing self vs. remembering self is adjacent here: the moment of progress is experienced richly, but the memory flattens.

For knowledge workers and founders in particular, Duruk's framing resonates because the work is never "done" — there's always more to build, more to learn. Framing this as a feature rather than a bug — the indefinite progress is the happiness, not an obstacle to it — is a useful reframe. Related to the growth mindset literature and Csikszentmihályi's flow work on intrinsic motivation.

## Key points

- Progress (moving forward) generates happiness more reliably than achievement (arriving) — the journey vs. destination distinction
- Hedonic adaptation: achieved goals become the new baseline quickly; the satisfaction fades
- For knowledge workers with open-ended work, framing ongoing work as progress is psychologically adaptive
- Connects to flow (Csikszentmihályi) — the experience of meaningful challenge is intrinsically rewarding
- Can Duruk later wrote for Margins and other tech-culture publications; this essay reflects his early blogging voice

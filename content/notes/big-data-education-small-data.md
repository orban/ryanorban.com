## Summary

This Washington Post piece by Valerie Strauss critiques the wave of big data investment in K-12 education during the 2010s — the proliferation of student tracking systems, predictive analytics tools, and data-driven accountability regimes — and argues that they have largely failed to improve learning outcomes while introducing new harms.

The argument runs on two tracks. First, the empirical track: the specific interventions that were sold on data-driven promises (prescriptive curricula adjusted by algorithmic tracking, early warning systems for dropout prediction, performance pay for teachers driven by student test metrics) have thin or negative evidence for impact. NCLB-era accountability, with its heavy reliance on standardized test scores as the primary data source, created perverse incentives (teaching to the test, gaming metrics) without improving the underlying thing being measured.

The second track is about what data can and can't capture. A teacher who knows 30 students individually has access to contextual, qualitative knowledge about each child that no database can hold: what happened at home this morning, why a student is disengaged today, what kind of feedback lands for a particular kid. The article calls this small data — not a technical term but a framing device for local, relational knowledge as opposed to population-level statistical tracking. The critique anticipates later arguments about the limits of algorithmic decision-making in high-stakes domains.

## Key points

- Big data in education promised personalized learning and accountability; delivered teaching to the test and surveillance without evidence of learning gains.
- Standardized test scores as primary data source create incentive misalignment: optimizing the metric decouples from optimizing learning.
- "Small data" framing: individual teacher knowledge of students is a rich, contextual dataset that algorithmic systems systematically fail to encode.
- Fits into a broader 2016 critique of algorithmic decision-making — same year as Cathy O'Neil's Weapons of Math Destruction.
- Still relevant: ed-tech investment hasn't slowed, and the algorithmic tracking vs. teacher autonomy tension is ongoing.

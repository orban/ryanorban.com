## Summary

[Max Hawkins](/notes/max-hawkins/) is an artist and software engineer based in Los Angeles whose work explores what happens when you hand decision-making over to algorithms. His most documented project was a years-long experiment in randomized living: a system that selected where he would move and live each month, based purely on computational chance. Rather than optimizing life choices, the project reversed the logic — treating unpredictability as valuable rather than something to eliminate.

His portfolio spans practical software and art installations in ways that blur the distinction. Dialup (which he co-founded) was a voice-chat app that connected strangers randomly, tapping into the same serendipity logic as his personal experiments. The Third Party was a bot that scraped Facebook events and sent him to random ones, leading to an NPR Invisibilia feature. QuarantineChat and PartyLine applied the same idea during COVID — connecting people across isolation and political difference through random pairing. The recurring theme is using randomness to break the filter bubble and encounter the genuinely unfamiliar.

The philosophical frame here isn't just novelty-seeking. Hawkins is interrogating how algorithmic optimization systems (recommendation engines, feed ranking, location apps) systematically narrow experience toward what's predicted to be preferred — and proposing inversion as a practice. This puts him in interesting conversation with writers like Jenny Odell (resisting the attention economy), though Hawkins' approach is more playful and less explicitly political. His work is creative coding as life practice.

## Key points

- Randomized living: algorithmic selection of where to live each month — a multi-year experiment in surrendering control.
- The Third Party: random Facebook event generator; ended up at hundreds of unexpected gatherings, covered by NPR.
- Dialup and QuarantineChat: serendipitous voice connection apps — strangers talking across normal social graphs.
- Central thesis: optimization systems narrow experience; randomness as a design principle forces genuine discovery.
- Shares conceptual space with Jenny Odell and the slow information diet movement, but via practice rather than critique.

## Summary

Wesley Tansey's post on startup idea generation was part of a genre of early-2012 writing aimed at aspiring founders who hadn't yet worked on anything. The core argument: the most reliable source of good ideas is your own experience of genuine friction. Problems you've personally experienced are more likely to be real, more likely to make you persistent through the difficulty of solving them, and more likely to give you insight into solutions that outsiders won't have.

This connects to Paul Graham's later (but more widely read) How to Get Startup Ideas — which makes essentially the same point but with more rigor. PG's framing: the best ideas come from living in the future (being an early adopter of technology that's just becoming feasible) and noticing what's missing. Tansey's framing is more practical: look at what bothers you daily, at work, in your current domain. The ideas you find by imagining markets to serve are weaker than the ideas you find by solving problems you're already living.

The post also addressed the common anxiety that all good ideas are taken. The counterargument: most markets are bigger than they appear from the outside, most existing solutions are worse than they appear from the inside, and the execution gap between "this idea exists" and "this idea works at scale" is enormous. The existence of a competitor is validation, not disqualification. In 2012, this was less established thinking than it is today — the ecosystem of writing about startups was still relatively young.

## Key points

- Best startup ideas come from problems you personally experience — you have more information and more persistence.
- Paul Graham's parallel framing: "live in the future, notice what's missing" — same underlying mechanism.
- The all good ideas are taken anxiety is almost always wrong — execution gap is the real differentiator.
- Competitor existence is validation of a market, not disqualification from entering.
- Related: Y Combinator's emphasis on make something people want as more reliable than find a gap in the market.

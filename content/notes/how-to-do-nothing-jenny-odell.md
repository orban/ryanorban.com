## Summary

This essay by Jenny Odell originated as a talk at the EYEO Festival in 2017 and later became the basis for her book "How to Do Nothing: Resisting the Attention Economy" (2019). The core argument is that the attention economy — platforms designed to capture and monetize your time and focus — has made doing nothing feel impossible and even shameful. Odell's counter-claim: doing nothing is a radical political act, not a failure of productivity.

The essay uses the rose garden in Morcom Amphitheater of Roses in Oakland as a recurring frame. There, Odell finds the practice of just watching — birds, flowers, light — without producing content, posting about it, or optimizing it. She draws on Herbert Marcuse's concept of "refusal" and positions presence, attention, and being-in-place as a resistance to the logic of constant extraction. This is not a tech-backlash argument about quitting social media; it's more philosophical, about what it means to simply exist without instrumentalizing existence.

Her argument connects to ideas from Hannah Arendt on the distinction between labor, work, and action — specifically, the kind of contemplative or unpurposive engagement that platforms are structurally designed to prevent. The essay was received as a manifesto by many in the tech industry who felt pulled between the tools they were building and what those tools were doing to their own capacity for stillness.

## Key points

- The attention economy commodifies presence and makes non-productive time feel like waste — the essay names this and argues it should be resisted.
- "Doing nothing" here means sustained, non-instrumental attention — birdwatching, wandering, contemplation without output.
- Draws on Herbert Marcuse's Great Refusal — refusing to participate in systems whose values you reject is itself a political stance.
- The EYEO Festival audience (creative technologists) made this especially charged: people who build the engagement systems reconsidering their own relationship to them.
- Later expanded into a book that brought in bioregionalism, ecology, and public space as additional frames for reclaiming attention.

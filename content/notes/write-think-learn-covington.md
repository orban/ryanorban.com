## Summary

Michael Covington's guide (University of Georgia AI Institute) makes a simple but powerful claim: clear writing, clear thinking, and learning are not separate skills but manifestations of the same underlying capacity. You don't really know what you think until you try to write it down, and if your writing is nonsense your thoughts probably are too.

The guide debunks myths about writing — that grammar is the main obstacle, that writing is for poets, that good writers are born not made. Most people's grammatical instincts are already adequate. The real challenge is having something to say and organizing it so the reader (not the writer) finds it easy. This unselfish perspective — doing what's easy for your reader — is the heart of good writing.

The core advice centers on what Covington calls amplifying intelligence: writing as thinking tool. Just as physical tools extend the body's capabilities, good writing practices extend cognitive capacity. Short sentences, concrete examples, active voice, and honest hedging all serve the reader's comprehension rather than the writer's ego.

## Key points

- Writing is the externalization of thought — unclear writing reveals unclear thinking, not just poor style
- The "unselfish perspective": good writing is about the reader's experience, not the writer's expression
- Grammar is rarely the bottleneck; structure and clarity of ideas matter more
- Amplifying intelligence through writing parallels how physical tools extend the body
- Strong advice: never write sentences you yourself don't understand, and never use jargon you can't define

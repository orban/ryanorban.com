## Summary

Alexander Etz's guide is aimed at scientists and statisticians trained in frequentist statistics who want to understand the Bayesian paradigm. The core shift is philosophical: instead of asking "what is the probability of this data given the null hypothesis?" (p-values, NHST), Bayesian inference asks "what is the probability of this hypothesis given the data?" This requires specifying prior distributions — beliefs before seeing data — which frequentists often find objectionable but Bayesians treat as a strength.

The eight steps walk through: understanding likelihoods, specifying priors, computing posterior distributions via Bayes' theorem, using Bayes factors instead of p-values, and learning to update beliefs incrementally as data accumulates. The Bayes factor comparison is particularly useful — it gives a direct measure of how much evidence shifts beliefs toward one hypothesis over another, rather than just a binary reject/don't-reject decision.

The post comes from the context of the 2015-2016 replication crisis in psychology, where NHST was under heavy criticism. Etz was part of the movement advocating Bayesian statistics as a more principled alternative for small-sample psychological research. The guide was influential for researchers looking to move beyond p-values without diving into full-length textbooks.

## Key points

- Core Bayesian shift: condition on observed data, put a distribution on hypotheses — the reverse of NHST.
- Prior distribution: quantifies knowledge before observing data; choice affects the posterior.
- Bayes factor: ratio of likelihoods under two hypotheses — a continuous measure of evidence, unlike binary p-value tests.
- Sequential updating: posterior from one experiment becomes prior for the next — Bayesian reasoning is naturally cumulative.
- Context: written during the replication crisis as a practical alternative to p-value-driven research.

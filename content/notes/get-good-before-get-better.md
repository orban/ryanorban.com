## Summary

Dan Shipper's early blog post (written when he was a college student) made a point about skill acquisition that resonated widely: the gap between taste and ability is real, persistent, and the only path through it is volume. Ira Glass had articulated the same idea about creative work — you know what good looks like before you can produce it, and that gap is uncomfortable. The post was applying this to writing and entrepreneurship specifically.

The argument: beginners often look for shortcuts because the gap between their output and what they know is good feels too large to close by grinding. They look for frameworks, techniques, better tools. But the gap is almost entirely a function of accumulated reps. Deliberate practice — the Anders Ericsson framework — requires doing the thing, getting feedback, and doing it again. There's no way around the bad early work. The only choice is to produce it faster.

This connects to Carol Dweck's growth mindset research: people who believe abilities are developed (rather than fixed) tolerate the bad-phase better and come out the other side. But the post's emphasis isn't on mindset — it's simpler. You don't get to skip from knowing what good looks like to producing it. The middle phase is the job. This is particularly relevant to writing, which is Shipper's framing, and to startup learning, where founders often want to learn from reading rather than doing.

## Key points

- Ira Glass gap: taste develops faster than ability; the early period of knowing good but not making it is unavoidable.
- Volume is the mechanism — more reps, faster feedback cycles, eventually the gap closes.
- Shortcuts (frameworks, better tools) optimize marginal steps but don't substitute for accumulated bad work.
- Anders Ericsson's deliberate practice research: the 10,000-hour idea isn't about time, it's about feedback-loop density.
- Dan Shipper later built and sold Firefly (iOS app), then founded Every (paid newsletter bundle) — this post is early-career writing.

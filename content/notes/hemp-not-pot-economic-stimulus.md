## Summary

Industrial hemp and marijuana are both varieties of *Cannabis sativa* but are functionally different plants: industrial hemp contains less than 0.3% THC (the psychoactive compound), while marijuana varieties are bred specifically for high THC content. Despite this, the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) treated them identically under the Controlled Substances Act, making it illegal to grow hemp in the US — a policy unique among developed nations.

The economic case against this prohibition: hemp has dozens of legitimate industrial applications — textiles, rope, food products (hemp seeds are nutritionally dense), building materials (hempcrete), biofuel, and paper — with established global markets. While the US banned domestic cultivation, Canada, France, and Germany grew hemp commercially. American companies could import hemp-derived products but couldn't grow the source material domestically.

Written in 2009 during the Obama administration's economic stimulus debate, the article frames hemp legalization as a green jobs opportunity: reviving domestic agricultural and manufacturing jobs in an environmentally sustainable crop that requires less water and pesticide than cotton. The political framing has since shifted; the 2018 Farm Bill eventually removed hemp from the Controlled Substances Act, legalizing commercial cultivation.

## Key points

- Industrial hemp has <0.3% THC — it's not psychoactive, but US law banned it as equivalent to marijuana.
- Applications: textiles, food (hemp seeds), building materials (hempcrete), biofuel, paper, rope.
- The US ban created a market for imports while blocking domestic production — economic policy incoherence.
- Canada, France, Germany grew hemp commercially while the US imported hemp products.
- The 2018 Farm Bill ultimately legalized hemp cultivation, vindicating the economic argument.
- A case study in drug policy spillover: prohibiting one thing incidentally banning another.

## Summary

Naveen Selvadurai — co-founder of Foursquare — wrote about the concept of a personal API: a structured endpoint that surfaces data about yourself (location history, calendar, activity, preferences) that you control and selectively share. The premise was that the data you generate across dozens of services should be accessible through a single, permissioned interface rather than siloed in incompatible platforms.

The idea connected to the quantified self movement that was active in 2013 — wearables like the Jawbone UP and Fitbit were generating personal health data, and services like Foursquare were logging location. The personal API would aggregate these streams and expose them to apps with your explicit permission: a restaurant app could query "what cuisines has this person liked recently?" rather than asking you to fill out a preference form.

In practice, personal APIs never became mainstream — the incentive structures push platforms toward lock-in rather than interoperability, and building and maintaining a personal API requires more technical effort than most people will invest. But the underlying desire resurfaced in subsequent movements: IndieWeb, data portability, GDPR's right of data access, and more recently Solid (Tim Berners-Lee's personal data pod project).

## Key points

- Personal API: structured endpoint exposing your own life data with granular access control
- 2013 context: Jawbone UP, Fitbit, Foursquare generating streams of personal data across incompatible silos
- Naveen Selvadurai was Foursquare's technical co-founder — had direct experience with the fragmentation problem
- Anticipated IndieWeb principles: own your data, publish to your own domain, syndicate to platforms
- GDPR (2018) made data portability a legal right in the EU — closest regulatory echo of the personal API idea
- Solid (Tim Berners-Lee) is the most serious technical implementation of this vision

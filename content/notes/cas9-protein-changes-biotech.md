## Summary

In March 2013, Matthew Herper at Forbes was among the first mainstream science journalists to flag Cas9 as a potential inflection point in biotechnology. The timing was remarkable: Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier had published their foundational Science paper showing that Cas9 — a bacterial immune system protein — could be reprogrammed to cut DNA at any desired sequence back in June 2012. Then in January 2013, Feng Zhang at the Broad Institute demonstrated that the system worked in human and mouse cells. Herper's piece appeared in the window between those two papers and the technology becoming widely understood.

CRISPR-Cas9 works by pairing the Cas9 endonuclease with a guide RNA sequence complementary to the target DNA site. When the guide RNA finds its match, Cas9 makes a double-strand break. The cell then repairs the break via non-homologous end joining (disabling the gene) or homology-directed repair (inserting a new sequence). The programmability — swapping the guide RNA to target a different site costs almost nothing — was what separated it from earlier genome editing tools like zinc finger nucleases and TALENs, which required re-engineering a protein for each new target.

What made this early coverage prescient was recognizing that Cas9 wasn't just another incremental improvement to molecular biology's toolbox. The speed and cheapness of the approach fundamentally changed what questions researchers could ask. Within two years, papers using CRISPR would cover everything from crop disease resistance to potential HIV therapy to the creation of animal models for human disease that would have taken years to produce by earlier methods.

## Key points

- Cas9 is a protein from the CRISPR adaptive immune system of bacteria — naturally evolved to cut foreign DNA at precise sequences; repurposed for genome editing by Jennifer Doudna, Emmanuelle Charpentier, and others
- The key breakthrough: guide RNAs are trivially programmable, making CRISPR-Cas9 orders of magnitude cheaper and faster than prior tools like zinc finger nucleases
- Feng Zhang's January 2013 paper in Science showed human-cell compatibility, opening the door to therapeutic applications
- Early therapeutic targets discussed included monogenic diseases (sickle cell disease, Duchenne muscular dystrophy) where a single-gene correction could be curative
- The downstream consequences — gene drives, base editing, prime editing — were not yet imagined; this article captures the moment the field saw the potential without knowing its full scope

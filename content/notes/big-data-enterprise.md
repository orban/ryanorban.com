## Summary

A ZDNet survey piece examining the actual state of big data adoption in enterprise organizations in mid-2012, during what was arguably the peak of Hadoop hype. The article found the familiar pattern of technology adoption curves: widespread experimentation and proof-of-concept projects, far fewer production systems delivering measurable business value, and significant confusion about what "big data" actually meant in practice.

The dominant theme of 2012 big data coverage was the collision between vendor excitement (every major IT vendor had a Hadoop strategy or a big data announcement) and the operational reality facing enterprise IT teams. Hadoop's complexity — configuring HDFS, tuning MapReduce jobs, managing ZooKeeper, understanding the dependency graph of the ecosystem — was not trivial. Most enterprises discovered that getting a Hadoop cluster running was the easy part; building data pipelines that reliably processed real data and delivered answers people trusted was much harder.

The big data adoption trajectory in 2012 foreshadowed what would happen with AI adoption in 2023–2024: massive hype, widespread experimentation, significant confusion about use cases, and a gap between what vendors promised and what enterprise IT teams could actually deliver in production. The enterprises that made big data work in this period were typically those that hired experienced data engineering teams rather than assuming the tools would handle the complexity.

## Key points

- Mid-2012: widespread big data experimentation in enterprise but few production systems delivering business value.
- Hadoop ecosystem complexity was the primary barrier — running the cluster is easy, running reliable data pipelines is hard.
- Vendor hype significantly outran practical enterprise adoption rates.
- Success correlated with experienced data engineering team investment, not just tool deployment.
- Pattern mirrors later AI adoption cycles: hype → experimentation → reality check → selective production deployment.
- ZDNet was tracking enterprise IT realities more skeptically than tech press, which was still mostly enthusiastic.

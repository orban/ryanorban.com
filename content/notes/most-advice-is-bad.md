## Summary

This essay from Sam's "All That Is Solid" Substack makes a case for epistemic skepticism toward advice as a category. The core argument: advice is almost always derived from someone's specific experience in specific circumstances, but when transmitted it loses that context and becomes a decontextualized rule that may not apply to your situation at all.

The failure modes are several. Survivorship bias: the people giving advice are those who succeeded, so their advice reflects what worked in their specific path through a skewed sample. Context collapse: work hard or follow your passion or find a mentor were true for a specific person in a specific environment, but the rule strips out the conditions that made them true. Attribution error: the advice-giver may not know what actually caused their success — the thing they recommend loudly might be incidental, while the real cause was circumstance, luck, or something they consider unremarkable.

The essay likely recommends: treat advice as a hypothesis to test rather than a prescription to follow, pay more attention to the circumstances under which advice was true than to the advice itself, and develop first-principles reasoning for your own situation rather than pattern-matching to others' conclusions. This is adjacent to thinking about base rates, reference class forecasting, and the general epistemic toolkit.

## Key points

- Survivorship bias in advice: we hear from people who succeeded; the failures who followed the same advice are invisible
- Context collapse: advice extracted from circumstance becomes a rule that applies far less broadly than stated
- Attribution error: advice-givers often don't know what actually caused their outcomes — confident but miscalibrated
- Better approach: treat advice as a hypothesis, seek out the original context, reason from first principles for your situation
- Adjacent to: base rates, Goodhart's Law (optimizing the metric isn't the same as achieving the goal), epistemic humility

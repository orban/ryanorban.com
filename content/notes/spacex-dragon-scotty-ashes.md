## Summary

SpaceX's Dragon capsule — on the first private commercial cargo mission to the International Space Station in May 2012 — carried the cremated remains of James Doohan, the actor who played Montgomery "Scotty" Scott in the original Star Trek series. The mission (CRS-1 test flight, CRS Demo Flight 2) was a milestone for commercial spaceflight: the first time a private company docked with the ISS and delivered cargo under a NASA contract.

Doohan had died in 2005. His family had previously attempted to get his ashes to space on a suborbital flight, but that rocket failed to reach space. This SpaceX mission finally succeeded — fitting that the fictional chief engineer of the Enterprise reached orbit on the first genuinely commercial spacecraft. The remains were part of a memorial spaceflight service run by Celestis, a company that has been launching small amounts of cremated remains into orbit since 1997.

The detail has a kind of historical resonance beyond the sentimental. Star Trek's transporter rooms and warp drives pulled generations of engineers toward aerospace and physics. Doohan himself had noted that being Scotty was the most meaningful thing he'd ever done specifically because of the engineers who told him the show made them want to go into engineering. The fact that SpaceX — a company built substantially by people who grew up on science fiction — carried him on a mission that marked a genuine inflection point in space commercialization has a certain symmetry.

## Key points

- James Doohan (Scotty, Star Trek TOS) died 2005; ashes launched by Celestis memorial spaceflight service.
- SpaceX CRS Demo Flight 2 (May 2012) was the first commercial cargo delivery to the ISS — historic for commercial spaceflight.
- Dragon capsule was the first private spacecraft to berth with the ISS and return.
- Celestis has offered orbital cremation services since 1997 — Gene Roddenberry was also flown in space.
- SpaceX's commercial success was predicated on NASA's Commercial Orbital Transportation Services (COTS) program, shifting from government-built rockets to contracted private launches.

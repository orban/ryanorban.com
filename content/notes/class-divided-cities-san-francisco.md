## Summary

Richard Florida and Sara Johnson analyzed San Francisco's economic stratification for The Atlantic Cities in April 2013, at the height of the first major tech-driven wealth concentration in the city. Their data showed SF as one of the most economically bifurcated cities in America — a pattern Florida called the winner-take-all urbanism of the knowledge economy: cities that attract high-skilled tech workers become enormously productive but also enormously unequal.

The mechanism: San Francisco's economy had bifurcated into two dominant sectors — the high-wage tech and finance sector, and the low-wage service sector (hospitality, food service, retail) that served the tech workers. The middle — manufacturing, blue-collar trades, mid-SKILL clerical work — had hollowed out as industries departed or automated. This created an hourglass income distribution rather than the bell curve of mid-century American cities.

The irony that Florida probably noted: the very factors that made SF attractive to tech workers (density, cultural amenities, educated population) were the same factors driving up housing costs, which then priced out the service workers whose labor made those amenities function. The tech boom was self-undermining for the middle class and working class who had built the city's appeal. This was a 2013 articulation of what became the dominant urban policy debate of the following decade — whether superstar cities were a net good for the people who lived in them or just for the knowledge-economy workers they attracted.

## Key points

- Richard Florida's winner-take-all urbanism: cities that attract knowledge workers become highly productive but highly unequal — the creative class thesis complicated by its consequences.
- Hourglass economy: tech-driven SF had high-wage knowledge work and low-wage service work, with the middle class shrinking as mid-skill jobs left.
- Housing affordability as the crux: service workers, teachers, and middle-income residents couldn't afford to live near the jobs that needed them.
- Inequality data for SF: Gini coefficient approaching those of developing-world cities — an unusual pattern for an American metro.
- The political consequence: SF in 2013 was already seeing organized resistance to tech-company practices (private buses, real estate conversions) from existing residents.

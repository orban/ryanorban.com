## Summary

John Arundel (bitfield consulting) makes a pointed observation: software engineers are professional planners — we write detailed specs, break down complex problems, design systems carefully — yet almost none of us apply that same thinking to career planning. The [career ending mistake](/notes/career-ending-mistake/) in the title is this neglect: treating your career as something that unfolds passively rather than as a system you intentionally design.

The article's core argument is that career strategy for engineers should be as deliberate as technical architecture. What are your goals? What's the current state? What's the gap? What's the roadmap? These are the questions engineers answer daily for software systems but rarely ask about their own work lives. The piece uses Go programming as its native context (Arundel runs a Go training business) but the advice is generalist.

The practical implication is that skills alone don't compound in a career the way deliberate positioning does. An engineer who thinks about where they want to be in 5 years and chooses projects, employers, and skills accordingly tends to outperform a more talented engineer who just takes whatever comes next. The article sits alongside Staff Engineer by Will Larson and similar resources on intentional engineering career development.

## Key points

- The irony: engineers plan software meticulously but their careers by accident.
- Career planning doesn't mean rigid 5-year plans — it means having a direction and making choices that point toward it.
- Choosing the right problems and the right companies matters more than individual SKILL level over a 10-year horizon.
- Regular self-review (quarterly, annually) brings the same feedback loop to a career that retrospectives bring to software.

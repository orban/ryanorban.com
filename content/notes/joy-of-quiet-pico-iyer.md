## Summary

Pico Iyer published this essay in the NYT at the start of 2012, when smartphone adoption was cresting and the always-on model of connectivity was becoming normalized. His argument is simple and uncomfortable: the ability to be quiet, to sit with oneself without input or output, is becoming a scarce good — and like all scarce goods it's increasingly stratified by wealth and status.

Iyer observes that the affluent were beginning to pay for silence in ways the poor couldn't afford: meditation retreats, digital detox weekends, vacation spots without cell service. The irony is that technology — which promised to democratize access and save time — was mostly consumed by those who could least afford to lose their attention. The knowledge workers generating the most economic value were also those most constantly interrupted.

The essay connects to a broader thread in attention economy criticism: Herbert Simon's point that information abundance creates attention scarcity applies here at the level of experience. What's rare isn't information or connection — it's the absence of them. Cal Newport's later work on deep work and digital minimalism extends this argument with more operational specifics, but Iyer gets the phenomenology right: the cost of constant connectivity is the loss of a kind of interior space that was previously involuntary and free.

## Key points

- Silence and stillness are becoming luxury goods — stratified by who can afford retreats, phone-free holidays, and the self-discipline to disconnect
- Attention scarcity (Herbert Simon): information abundance is the flip side of attention poverty; every app competes for the same finite resource
- The always-on model normalizes constant availability as professionalism, making disconnection feel like delinquency
- Deep work and contemplative states require extended uninterrupted time — which constant connectivity structurally prevents
- Iyer lived part-time in rural Japan — his personal experiment with reduced connectivity informed the empirical core of the essay

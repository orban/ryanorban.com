## Summary

This piece argues that data science practice depends heavily on skills that statistics and programming courses don't teach: communication, intellectual curiosity, domain knowledge, and the ability to frame problems before solving them. The technical skills are table stakes — most candidates who get interviews can code and run models. What separates practitioners who produce impact from those who produce reports nobody acts on is usually communication and problem framing.

The argument was increasingly common in 2014 as the first wave of data science hires showed mixed results. Teams that hired purely for technical depth often found analysts who could answer the wrong question very precisely. Domain knowledge meant knowing which questions were worth asking.

## Key points

- Problem framing is the highest-leverage SKILL: defining what to measure and what question to ask determines the ceiling on any analysis
- Translating statistical results for non-technical audiences is a distinct skill — knowing what to say and what to leave out
- Domain knowledge acts as a prior: it tells you which effects are plausible and which model outputs to distrust before seeing the data
- Curiosity drives the iteration cycle — staying with a confusing result until it makes sense, rather than accepting the first clean-looking answer
- The irony is that soft skills are what make hard skills useful — a brilliant model that nobody understands or trusts won't change any decisions

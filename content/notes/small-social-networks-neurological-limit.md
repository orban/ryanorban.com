## Summary

In 2012 a cluster of social networks launched around a deliberate constraint: limit the number of connections. Path capped its friend list at 150 (later expanded), invoking Dunbar's number — the anthropological claim that humans maintain stable social relationships with roughly 150 people at most. Familyleaf focused on family networks specifically. Pair (later Couple) was a two-person network for romantic partners only. The NYT piece framed these as a reaction to the ambient-broadcast model of Facebook and Twitter, where friend had become a meaningless unit.

The bookmarker's annotation — "new neurological limit — the maximum number of social networks the human brain can handle" — is a wry joke, but it points at a real tension. Dunbar's number research suggests our primate brains have rough limits on the number of relationships we can track with real attention. Facebook had by 2012 demonstrated that social software could vastly exceed those limits, but at the cost of relationship quality — the feed became noise because it was optimizing for engagement across hundreds of connections rather than depth across dozens.

Path was the most prominent of these. Designed by Dave Morin (former Facebook platform executive) and with investment from Shawn Fanning, it was beautifully designed and genuinely popular in tech circles, but struggled to achieve mass-market scale precisely because its constraint — the small friend list — meant it couldn't benefit from the viral network effects that drove Facebook's growth.

## Key points

- Path (150 connection cap), Familyleaf (family), and Pair (romantic couples) all launched around the same thesis: smaller is better.
- Dunbar's number (~150) is the cognitive limit on maintained social relationships — the biological constraint these products tried to honor.
- Facebook's model had commoditized "friend" to the point where the word meant almost nothing; these products tried to restore signal.
- The tension: network effects reward growth, but growth erodes the intimacy that made these products valuable in the first place.
- None of these achieved lasting scale; Path sold to Kakao in 2015 for an undisclosed amount after failing to monetize.

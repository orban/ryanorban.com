## Summary

UC Berkeley's School of Information (iSchool) ran a course in Spring 2012 that taught students to analyze large-scale Twitter data using Hadoop and related tools. This was early for academic programs in what would come to be called data science — most universities didn't have dedicated data science courses yet, and the iSchool was one of the few that bridged social media data with distributed computing infrastructure.

The course (I290) was part of a pattern at UC Berkeley that positioned the institution as the academic home of the emerging big data stack. The AMPLab (Algorithms, Machines, and People Lab) at Berkeley was simultaneously producing Apache Spark, Mesos, and other foundational infrastructure. Having a course that used Twitter's real data for teaching gave students access to production-scale problems — billions of tweets, real API rate limits, the messiness of real-world text data — rather than cleaned academic datasets.

Twitter itself was an active participant in academia during this period: they provided data access grants, gave talks, and in some cases funded research. Twitter's engineering blog and conference presentations were primary sources for how to do Hadoop-based analytics on real social data. The Hortonworks companion piece (Twitter presenting Hadoop at Berkeley) reflects this ecosystem of company-university knowledge transfer.

## Key points

- UC Berkeley iSchool's I290 was among the first academic courses explicitly teaching large-scale social data analysis with distributed computing tools.
- Used real Twitter data — giving students exposure to production data scale and messiness rather than cleaned academic datasets.
- UC Berkeley's AMPLab was simultaneously producing Apache Spark and Mesos — the course and the research lab were part of the same institutional moment.
- Twitter was an active academic partner in 2012–2013, providing data access and sending engineers to give talks at universities.
- Preceded the establishment of formal data science degree programs, which began appearing at universities around 2014–2015.

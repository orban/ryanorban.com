## Summary

BioCurious, the community biology lab in Sunnyvale, California, built a bioprinter from modified off-the-shelf inkjet printer components. The printer deposited living mammalian cells in precise patterns, allowing it to construct rudimentary three-dimensional tissue-like structures. The project was covered by Wired in January 2013 as an example of the maker culture ethos reaching into synthetic biology and tissue engineering — domains previously restricted to well-funded university and pharmaceutical labs.

The underlying technique is bioink printing: cells suspended in a hydrogel matrix are loaded into printer cartridges and deposited layer by layer. The challenge is keeping cells viable during and after printing — cells need nutrients, the right temperature, and the right extracellular environment to survive. Professional bioprinters from companies like Organovo cost hundreds of thousands of dollars; the BioCurious version was built for a few thousand.

The significance wasn't the quality of the output — it wasn't printing functional organs — but the democratization of access to the technique. This was the same dynamic playing out across biohacking labs: tools that cost $100,000 in 2005 were being replicated for $500 in 2013. The tweet that prompted this bookmark tagged Eri Gentry (BioCurious co-founder) and patrikd posing with the bioprinter.

## Key points

- BioCurious was one of the first community biology labs in the US — a hackerspace for biology, opened in 2010 in Sunnyvale
- Bioprinting technique: cells in hydrogel matrix deposited by a modified inkjet head, layer by layer, to build 3D cell arrangements
- Organovo was the leading professional bioprinting company at the time; BioCurious version was an order of magnitude cheaper
- The 2013 DIY biology scene included iGEM, BioCurious, and Genspace (NYC) — laying groundwork for what's now a broader citizen science infrastructure
- Cell viability after printing was the technical challenge; early structures were simple and not vascularized

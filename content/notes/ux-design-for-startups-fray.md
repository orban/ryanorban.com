## Summary

Fray (fray.it) was a small design agency blog that published practical UX articles for a product and startup audience. This post addressed how to apply user experience design methodology in startup contexts — where time, budget, and team size constraints make the full UX process impractical, but where getting the product experience right is critical for early traction.

The core tension the post addressed: academic UX practice involves extensive user research, usability testing, card sorting, persona development, and detailed wireframing before any design decisions are made. Startups can't afford six weeks of research before building. The guidance was about which elements of UX practice give the highest signal-to-noise ratio when compressed — which shortcuts are acceptable and which cuts create technical debt in the product experience.

The typical advice in this genre: start with at least 5 user interviews before designing (enough to identify patterns without requiring large samples), prioritize information architecture because it's expensive to change later, do guerrilla usability testing with low-fidelity prototypes early, and document decisions so the rationale survives team growth. The goal is to bring enough UX discipline to avoid the most expensive mistakes without slowing the core product iteration loop.

## Key points

- Lightweight user research: 5-7 user interviews reveal most usability patterns that matter; formal usability studies are rarely necessary for early-stage products
- Information architecture decisions (hierarchy, labeling, navigation structure) are the most expensive to change later — invest here early
- Low-fidelity prototyping: paper sketches or Balsamiq mockups get real feedback without the investment of high-fidelity design
- Usability testing doesn't require a lab: walking through a prototype with a colleague or a coffee shop stranger surfaces 80% of the critical issues
- 2013 context: UX as a discipline was just becoming standard in startups; most early-stage founders still thought of design as visual polish rather than user research

## Summary

Andy Baio's piece at Waxy.org documented the first wave of platform-level responses to AI-generated art in September 2022 — right as Stable Diffusion went public and flooded art communities with generated images. ArtStation, DeviantArt, Newgrounds, Fur Affinity, and others moved to restrict or ban AI-generated content. The bans varied: some prohibited AI art outright, others required tagging or restricted it to specific sections.

The core grievance from artists: Stable Diffusion and Midjourney were trained on images scraped from the web without consent, and artists found their styles being replicated at scale by people with no training. HaveIBeenTrained.com launched around this time to let artists check if their work was in the training set. The backlash was both ethical (consent and attribution) and economic (AI images competing directly with commissions).

DeviantArt's response was particularly fraught — they introduced DreamUp, their own AI image tool built on the same technology artists were protesting, while adding opt-out mechanisms for training data. The gap between platform rhetoric and action became a recurring theme. This moment marked the beginning of an ongoing conflict between generative AI developers and creative communities that extended to music, writing, and code with platforms like Soundcloud, Getty Images, and legal challenges like the Andersen v. Stability AI case.

## Key points

- ArtStation, DeviantArt, Newgrounds, and others banned or restricted AI-generated images in Sept 2022.
- Backlash driven by training data consent issues and economic competition with human artists.
- HaveIBeenTrained.com launched to let artists opt out of LAION training datasets.
- DeviantArt's DreamUp launch during the backlash illustrated the platform alignment problem.
- This was the first major public reckoning with Stable Diffusion's open release and its social consequences.
- Pattern repeated in music (Soundcloud), writing, and code — creative industry vs. generative AI training data.

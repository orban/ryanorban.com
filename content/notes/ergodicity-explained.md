## Summary

Ergodicity is a property of a system where the time average of an outcome (what one person experiences over time) equals the ensemble average (what a large group of people experience at one moment). For ergodic systems, the two are interchangeable. For non-ergodic systems — which includes most real-world economic decisions — they diverge dramatically, and using ensemble averages to guide individual decisions leads to wrong conclusions.

The classic example: a coin flip game where heads doubles your wealth and tails halves it. The ensemble average (across many people) shows positive expected value — the average gain is positive. But the time average (what happens to a single person playing many times) trends toward zero because of multiplicative dynamics: a sequence of doublings and halvings doesn't return you to where you started, and enough halvings drive you toward zero regardless of the doublings.

This critique, developed by Ole Peters at the London Mathematical Laboratory, has significant implications for standard expected utility theory in economics. Risk aversion in economic models is typically modeled as a personal preference (utility functions), but Peters argues it's actually the rational response to non-ergodic dynamics — a person playing a repeated game should be more conservative than ensemble averages suggest because ruin is absorbing (once broke, you stop playing).

## Key points

- Ergodicity: time average = ensemble average. Most financial decisions are non-ergodic — they're not interchangeable.
- Multiplicative dynamics: gains and losses compound, so average gains don't predict individual outcomes.
- Ole Peters's critique: risk aversion in economics is partly misattributed — it's rational non-ergodic behavior, not a preference.
- Kelly criterion follows naturally: size bets to maximize long-run growth rate, not expected value per bet.
- Practical implication: never bet more than you can afford to lose many times over, regardless of expected value.

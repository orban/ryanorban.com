## Summary

The promise of personalized medicine in oncology is that by sequencing a tumor's genome and targeting its specific mutations, we can treat cancer with much higher precision than blunt chemotherapy. The problem is that this model assumes a tumor is a uniform, static target — and it's not. Tumors are genetically heterogeneous mosaics: different regions of the same tumor carry different mutations. A single biopsy captures only about 55% of all mutations present. You're not sequencing "the cancer," you're sequencing one sample from a dynamically evolving population.

The deeper problem is tumor evolution. Cancer cells divide rapidly under therapeutic pressure and the ones that happen to carry resistance mutations survive and proliferate. This is natural selection at work within the body — the same process that makes bacteria evolve antibiotic resistance. Targeted therapy kills the sensitive subpopulation, which clears the way for the resistant subpopulation to expand. The therapy that worked last month stops working because the tumor has evolved in response.

Darwinian oncology — treating cancer as an evolutionary process rather than a genetic snapshot — offers a more realistic framework. Approaches include adaptive therapy (alternating treatments to prevent resistance from dominating) and targeting evolutionary constraints (mutations that cancer cells can't survive without, not just common mutations).

## Key points

- A single tumor biopsy captures only ~55% of its mutations — spatial heterogeneity makes any sample incomplete.
- Tumor evolution under therapy is analogous to antibiotic resistance: sensitive cells die, resistant subpopulations expand.
- Triple-negative breast cancer studies found no two tumor genomes alike — each patient has a distinct evolving disease.
- Personalized medicine as currently practiced targets a snapshot of a moving target.
- Adaptive therapy (using tumor evolution strategically) is a more sophisticated approach than find-and-kill.
- The fundamental challenge is evolutionary, not merely genomic — we need evolutionary biology as much as genomics.

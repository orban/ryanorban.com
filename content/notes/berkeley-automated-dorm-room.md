## Summary

In 2012, a UC Berkeley freshman named Brandon Stafford turned his dorm room into what TechCrunch called ridiculously automated — a network of Arduino controllers, sensors, and scripts that could respond to commands, set scenes, and (most notably) trigger an "Emergency Party" button that changed the lighting and music on demand. The project got attention not just for the execution but for what it represented: a maker movement moment where the barrier to building real-time physical control systems had dropped to student-project levels.

The technical foundation was Arduino microcontrollers connected to various relays, LED lighting strips, and a computer running scripts. This predates the consumer smart home era — before Nest, Hue, or Amazon Echo made home automation a retail product. The fact that a college freshman could build something this functional, with off-the-shelf hardware and open-source software, illustrated the inflection point the maker community was at in 2012.

The "Emergency Party" button was the detail that made it shareable: it collapsed a complex technical setup into a single relatable affordance, demonstrating something that home automation systems often miss — that the goal isn't control panels, it's outcomes. One button, one transformation. The Raspberry Pi had just launched the same year, and Kickstarter was funding hardware projects at unprecedented rates. The maker movement was producing a generation of engineers who expected their environments to be programmable.

## Key points

- Built on Arduino hardware — cheap, open-source microcontrollers that democratized physical computing in the early 2010s.
- Part of a broader maker movement wave that included Raspberry Pi (launched 2012), Makerbot, and the Maker Faire community.
- "Emergency Party" button was the killer feature — single-action scene control that made the complexity invisible.
- Predates consumer IoT: this was DIY home automation before smart home became a product category.
- Reflects the 2012 moment when Arduino and cheap sensors made dorm-room prototyping of physical systems realistic.
- UC Berkeley's engineering culture encouraged this kind of personal infrastructure hacking.

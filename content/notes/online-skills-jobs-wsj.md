## Summary

This Wall Street Journal piece examines the gap between online SKILL acquisition and employer recognition in late 2015, at the height of the MOOC boom. Platforms like Coursera, Udacity, and edX had attracted millions of learners promising career transformation, but the question of whether completing a Nanodegree or MOOC certificate actually moved the needle in job searches remained contested.

The pattern the article documented: employers and recruiters often filtered on traditional credentials first (university degree, recognized certification bodies), making online learning completions invisible in the initial resume screen. The value of online skills depended heavily on context — developers could demonstrate competence through GitHub portfolios and take-home coding tests, making the degree signal less important. But for career changers without a technical portfolio, the credential gap remained significant.

This was precisely the problem the coding bootcamp industry was positioned to solve: not just teaching skills but providing a credential that employers in a specific local market had agreed to recognize. Galvanize invested in employer relationships as much as curriculum. The article captures the moment before outcome-based and income-share models tried to align incentives — when the burden of proving online skill value was still entirely on the learner.

## Key points

- Employer filtering on traditional degrees meant MOOC completions often didn't reach hiring decision-makers.
- Developers could route around credential filtering via portfolios and GitHub — non-technical roles couldn't.
- Coding bootcamps positioned as the credential bridge that online courses couldn't provide alone.
- Tension: online learning's promise was individual upskilling; its limitation was employer signal recognition.
- Outcome-based and ISA models emerged later to force alignment between training and employment.

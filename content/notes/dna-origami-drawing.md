## Summary

DNA origami is a technique for folding long single-stranded DNA into arbitrary two-dimensional and three-dimensional shapes using short staple strands that bind at specific positions to hold the scaffold in place. Paul Rothemund at Caltech pioneered the method in 2006, demonstrating that you could program DNA's Watson-Crick base pairing rules to serve as a construction kit at the nanoscale. This 2012 Nature News piece covered refinements and new applications of the technique.

The old twist in the title refers to using established tools and principles from structural DNA nanotechnology — a field pioneered by Nadrian Seeman beginning in the 1980s — applied in newer ways. By 2012, DNA origami had produced nanoscale smiley faces, maps of the Western hemisphere, and molecular machines. The technique was attracting interest for potential applications in drug delivery (folding DNA cages that open in response to specific molecules), nanophotonics (arranging gold nanoparticles at precise distances), and molecular computing.

The underlying insight is that DNA is not just a genetic information carrier — its physical properties (predictable binding, controllable rigidity, precise dimensions of roughly 2nm width per double helix) make it an ideal structural material. A DNA strand will reliably bind to its complement. If you design the sequence, you design the structure.

## Key points

- DNA origami uses a long scaffold strand + short "staple" strands to fold DNA into designed 2D/3D nanostructures.
- Paul Rothemund (Caltech, 2006) demonstrated arbitrary 2D shapes — smiley faces, star patterns — at ~6nm resolution.
- Applications in 2012: drug delivery cages, nanophotonics (positioning gold nanoparticles), molecular machines.
- Builds on Nadrian Seeman's foundational work in structural DNA nanotechnology from the 1980s.
- DNA as a construction material: 2nm helix width, programmable binding, ~$0.01 per strand — cheaper than most alternatives at the time.

## Summary

On May 20, 2012, an annular solar eclipse crossed the western United States, sweeping from the Pacific Coast through Nevada, Utah, Colorado, and New Mexico before continuing across the Pacific to Asia. In the Bay Area, the eclipse reached approximately 94% coverage — not total, but enough to noticeably dim the sky. NASA's Science News coverage explained the mechanics and provided viewing guidelines.

An annular eclipse occurs when the Moon is near apogee (the far point of its elliptical orbit) and its apparent diameter is slightly smaller than the Sun's. Instead of completely blocking the Sun as in a total solar eclipse, the Moon leaves a bright annular ring visible — the ring of fire effect. The ring phase lasted about four minutes along the central path. Unlike a total eclipse, an annular eclipse can't be viewed with the naked eye even at maximum coverage — the exposed ring of the Sun is still intensely bright.

The 2012 annular eclipse was widely observed in the Bay Area, where clear skies and the late-afternoon timing (around 5:30 PM local time) made it accessible. Maker Faire 2012 was happening the same weekend in San Mateo, creating an unusual conjunction: the eclipse viewing happened while makers were showing off their projects. The eclipse prompted a small improvised gathering with welding glass for viewing (shade 14 glass blocks enough light to safely view the partial/annular phases).

## Key points

- Annular eclipse: Moon near apogee → apparent size smaller than Sun → "ring of fire" remains visible at peak.
- Path: Oregon/California coast → Nevada → Utah → Texas → Pacific → Japan/China/Southeast Asia.
- Bay Area coverage: ~94% at maximum, around 5:30 PM on May 20, 2012.
- Viewing safety: must use solar filters (shade 14 welding glass, eclipse glasses) — annular phase still burns retinas.
- Previous US annular eclipse: 1994; next was October 2023 across Nevada/New Mexico.

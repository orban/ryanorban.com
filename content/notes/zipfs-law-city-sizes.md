## Summary

Zipf's law for cities states that when you rank cities by population, the relationship between rank and size follows a power law: the largest city is roughly twice the size of the second-largest, three times the third-largest, and so on. George Zipf identified this pattern in 1949, but it was Xavier Gabaix who formalized it as a power law distribution and showed it holds across every country studied for over a century.

The empirical pattern is striking because it persists despite cities growing at vastly different rates and being subject to completely different economic and geographic conditions. Paul Krugman noted the paradox: "reality is startlingly neat and simple" even though our economic theory is too complex to produce this result cleanly. No one has a fully satisfying explanation for why this regularity appears.

Two important boundary conditions: the law breaks down for cities below ~100,000 people (smaller cities are too idiosyncratic), and it only applies within economically integrated systems. It works nation by nation but fails if you apply it to the EU as a whole — economic integration seems to be a necessary condition.

## Key points

- Zipf's law: city rank × population ≈ constant. The #1 city is ~2× #2, ~3× #3, etc. — a power law distribution.
- Holds empirically across all countries studied for the past century, despite different policies, geographies, and growth rates.
- Formalized as a power law by economist Xavier Gabaix.
- Fails for cities below ~100,000 and fails across non-integrated economic zones (e.g., all of EU combined).
- Related to Zipf's law for word frequencies (the original application) — suggests a universal mechanism for rank-frequency distributions in human systems.
- Paul Krugman acknowledged the pattern is cleaner than anything economic theory predicts.

## Summary

This post from Qunb makes the data visualization purist's case against most infographics: they sacrifice information density and accuracy for visual novelty, substituting decorative elements for clarity and replacing quantitative precision with rough visual impressions. The critique was aimed particularly at the genre of social media infographics that were extremely popular in 2013 — elaborate illustrated pieces with statistics embedded in pictorial contexts.

Edward Tufte's concept of "chartjunk" is the intellectual ancestor here: visual elements that don't encode data are noise that competes with signal. A 3D bar chart looks more impressive than a 2D one but the third dimension conveys nothing; it actively distorts area perception. Decorative illustrations around a statistic might make it more shareable but make it harder to compare values precisely.

The save note mentions Stamen Design and @fulgenteft (Nathan Yau, of FlowingData) — both were serious data visualization practitioners who occupied interesting positions on this debate. Stamen produced beautiful, carefully crafted visualizations that were aesthetically rich without being dishonest. Yau was consistently in the functional beauty camp: charts should be well-designed AND accurate.

## Key points

- Most infographics fail the basic test of data visualization: do they let the reader accurately read quantities and make comparisons?
- Chartjunk (Edward Tufte): visual elements that don't encode data add noise, not value — 3D charts, decorative illustrations, unnecessary color.
- The shareability trap: infographics optimized for social virality are usually optimized against clarity.
- The data visualization community (Tufte, Yau, Bostock) had a strong clarity over decoration position that this piece reflects.
- Connected to [cargo cult analytics](/notes/cargo-cult-analytics/): infographics that look like data communication without actually communicating data clearly.

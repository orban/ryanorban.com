## Summary

Liz Abinante (lizthedeveloper) wrote this during the peak of the coding bootcamp boom as a practitioner who'd seen both sides. Her argument isn't that bootcamps are better at everything — it's that for the specific goal of producing job-ready software developers, they beat universities on the dimensions that matter: tight feedback loops, direct mentorship, cohort-based peer learning, and immediate application of skills to real projects.

University CS programs optimize for theory and research preparation. The four-year arc made sense when software development required deep computer science foundations — compilers, operating systems, algorithms — before any production work was possible. But full-stack web development, data science, and most SaaS engineering work doesn't require that foundation first. Bootcamps figured out how to teach the practical layer directly, cutting out years of prerequisites.

The critique embedded in Abinante's piece applies beyond coding: that formal education has conflated "education" with credentialing, and that the job market signal of a credential can persist long after the actual learning value has been superseded by faster, cheaper alternatives. Galvanize, where this was saved from, was among the larger bootcamp networks running programs in data science and full-stack development at the time.

## Key points

- Coding bootcamps beat traditional CS programs on job-readiness through tight feedback cycles and immediate applied practice.
- University CS optimizes for theory and research prep — valuable for some careers, misaligned for most SaaS engineering roles.
- Cohort learning and direct mentorship are bootcamp structural advantages that lectures don't replicate.
- Critique: higher education conflates credentialing with actual SKILL transfer.
- Written at peak bootcamp era (2016) — the market subsequently corrected, with many bootcamps closing by 2020.

## Summary

William Deresiewicz delivered this as an address to the plebe class at West Point in 2010. The argument is counterintuitive for a military context: the most important quality for a leader isn't decisiveness or command presence — it's the ability to think alone. Solitude, uninterrupted and unmediated by external input, is how you develop genuine convictions rather than absorbed opinions.

Deresiewicz's critique targets the multitasking, hyper-connected professional culture that was already dominant by 2010. The problem isn't efficiency — it's that constant stimulation prevents the kind of slow, deep thinking where original thought actually happens. He cites Dwight Eisenhower writing long letters to no one in particular, Ulysses Grant in the field, and reading great literature as practices that build the mental discipline to hold a problem in mind long enough to actually think it through.

The essay connects to a broader theme in his work about elite education producing technically competent but intellectually conformist graduates — excellent sheep who can execute brilliantly on assigned tasks but lack the conviction to push back when the assigned task is wrong. Leadership, in his view, requires the prior capacity to form independent judgments, which requires solitude to develop.

## Key points

- Solitude is the prerequisite for independent thought — constant stimulation produces borrowed opinions, not conviction.
- Critique of multitasking culture: fragmented attention prevents the sustained thinking that generates original ideas.
- Leaders need to know their own mind before they can lead others — requires time without external input.
- Connects to Deresiewicz's broader critique in Excellent Sheep of elite university education.
- Delivered at West Point — the military audience makes the anti-action, pro-reflection argument more pointed.

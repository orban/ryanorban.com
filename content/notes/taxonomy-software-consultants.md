## Summary

Erik Dietrich (daedtech.com) has written extensively about the career evolution of software developers, and this taxonomy extends that work into the consulting space. The core insight: software consultant is a catch-all term that obscures radically different value propositions, market positions, and leverage structures. Collapsing them together hides what separates highly paid specialists from commoditized labor.

The taxonomy generally moves along two axes: generalism vs. specialization, and advisory vs. execution. Staff augmenters sit at the execution/generalist corner — they replace or supplement headcount, competing on availability and rate. Deep specialists sit at the advisory/specialized corner — they solve specific problems no one else can, charging for knowledge rather than time. Between these are various hybrids: practitioners who specialize but still execute, advisors who remain somewhat general, and productized experts who've packaged their knowledge into repeatable deliverables.

The stakes for this framing: if you're a software consultant thinking about your market position, what type of consultant am I? determines your pricing power, client relationship, and growth ceiling. Staff augmenters face commodity rate pressure; specialists with a proprietary niche can charge 5-10x more for the same hours because they're pricing on value, not time. Positioning is the mechanism — becoming the go-to person for a specific, painful, expensive problem.

## Key points

- "Software consultant" covers a spectrum from staff augmentation to strategic advisor — each has different economics.
- Specialization increases pricing power by reducing direct competition: fewer people can solve your specific problem.
- Productized consulting packages expertise into repeatable deliverables (courses, assessments, audits) that don't sell time for money.
- Positioning is how you escape the commodity trap: narrow focus on a specific problem/industry/technology.
- Related to Erik Dietrich's broader argument in "Developer Hegemony" about developers escaping the employee model.

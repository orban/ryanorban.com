## Summary

Dror Poleg's "The Ponzi Career" draws an analogy between modern knowledge worker careers and Ponzi schemes — not in the fraudulent sense, but in the structural sense that the value of credentials depends on continued demand from later entrants. An MBA from a top school was worth more when fewer people had one; as more people pursued the credential, the value per person declined. Those who got in early captured most of the returns.

The argument extends to professional networks, industry expertise, and geographic concentration (Silicon Valley, New York finance). The people who built networks in these spheres before they became crowded extracted outsized value; those arriving later get lower returns on the same investment of time and social capital. This is structurally similar to an asset bubble — early participants profit, late participants often don't.

Poleg connects this to broader labor market inequality: the winner-take-all dynamics in knowledge work concentrate returns among those who established positions early, often through luck of timing and access to the right institutions, rather than pure merit. The meritocracy framing obscures that the game is partly about position — being early to the right credential, network, or geography.

## Key points

- Credential value is path-dependent: MBA returns diminished as supply expanded, benefiting early holders.
- Network effects in careers: early entrants to the right professional networks capture disproportionate value.
- Geographic concentration (Silicon Valley, NYC finance) works the same way — early movers benefit most.
- Structural critique of meritocracy: timing and access matter as much as ability.
- By Dror Poleg — economist focused on the future of work and real estate.

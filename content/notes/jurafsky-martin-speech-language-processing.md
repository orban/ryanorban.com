## Summary

Speech and Language Processing by Dan Jurafsky and James H. Martin is the canonical textbook for natural language processing. The third edition draft is freely available at the Stanford URL and has been continuously updated to cover modern deep learning-based NLP methods. Starting from classical statistical methods, it builds through n-gram language models, hidden Markov models, and parsing, all the way to transformers, BERT, and neural language models.

What distinguishes this book from most ML texts is the combination of linguistic grounding and computational rigor. Jurafsky is both a computational linguist and an ML researcher, and the text doesn't just describe algorithms — it explains what language phenomena those algorithms are capturing. The chapter on coreference resolution, for example, grounds the computational problem in linguistic theory about discourse coherence. This dual perspective makes it valuable both as a practical engineering reference and as a way to understand why NLP problems are hard in ways that pure ML training doesn't expose.

The 3rd edition structure covers: regular expressions and finite automata, n-gram models, text classification (Naive Bayes, logistic regression), word vectors (word2vec, GloVe), recurrent models, transformer architecture, question answering, information retrieval, machine translation, summarization, dialogue systems, and sentiment analysis. It's comprehensive and free — an unusual combination at this quality level.

## Key points

- Canonical graduate-level NLP textbook by Dan Jurafsky (Stanford) and James H. Martin (University of Colorado).
- Third edition draft freely available online — updated continuously to include modern methods.
- Covers classical (HMM, CRF) through modern (transformer, BERT, neural language models) approaches.
- Linguistically grounded: explains what language phenomena algorithms address, not just how they work.
- Key topics: language modeling, named entity recognition, parsing, machine translation, dialogue systems.
- Widely used in graduate NLP courses; accessible to engineers with ML background.

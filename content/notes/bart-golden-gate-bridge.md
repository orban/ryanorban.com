## Summary

A Substack essay investigating the history of why BART (Bay Area Rapid Transit) never extended across the Golden Gate Bridge to Marin County. When BART was originally planned in the late 1950s and early 1960s, Marin County was included in the system — the plan included a lower rail deck on the Golden Gate Bridge for BART trains, which would have connected Marin to San Francisco and eventually to the East Bay.

The reasons for Marin dropping out of the BART system are layered: political opposition from Marin County residents who feared that rail access would bring urbanization and denser development (Marin was — and remains — a wealthy, low-density county with strong NIMBY politics), concerns about the cost sharing formula and property tax assessments, bridge authority politics, and ultimately a 1962 vote in which Marin withdrew from the BART district.

The consequences are still felt today. Marin has no rail transit connection to San Francisco — residents either drive across the Golden Gate Bridge (contributing to its chronic congestion) or take the Golden Gate Ferry. The Golden Gate Bridge has never carried rail, even though its original design included provisions for it. The story is a well-known example in urban planning and transit policy circles of how short-term political decisions create long-term infrastructure lock-in.

The counterfactual is interesting: with BART in Marin, development patterns in the county would have been substantially different — higher density around stations, less car-dependency, different demographics over time.

## Key points

- Marin County was included in the original BART plans, including a rail deck on the Golden Gate Bridge.
- Marin withdrew in 1962 due to opposition from residents fearing urbanization and density.
- The Golden Gate Bridge was structurally designed to support rail but has never carried it.
- Result: Marin has no rail transit to SF 60+ years later; cars and ferry are the only options.
- Classic transit policy lesson: short-term political decisions create very long-term infrastructure consequences.

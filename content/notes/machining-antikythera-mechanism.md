## Summary

The Antikythera Mechanism is a 2,000-year-old Greek astronomical calculator recovered from a shipwreck near Antikythera island in 1901. X-ray and CT scanning revealed it to be a complex gear system with at least 30 interlocking bronze gears that computed solar and lunar cycles, eclipse predictions, and the timing of the Panhellenic Games. It's often called the world's first analog computer — far more mechanically sophisticated than anything else known from the ancient world for another 1,000 years.

This YouTube playlist documents the process of machining a working replica using modern tools. The machining challenge is substantial: the original's gears have odd tooth counts (e.g., 127 teeth for the lunar anomaly gear) and the tolerances required for smooth operation are tight. CNC machining and manual lathe work, gear cutting, and precision fitting are all involved. The documentary approach captures not just the technical process but what the attempt reveals about the original makers' knowledge and SKILL.

The project sits at the intersection of history of technology, classical mechanics, and modern machining craft. UCL's Antikythera Research Team (and earlier Tony Freeth's work) have produced increasingly complete models of the mechanism's full function. Machining a physical replica — as opposed to a CGI reconstruction — produces visceral understanding of how the device actually worked that no paper or visualization achieves.

## Key points

- Antikythera Mechanism (c. 100 BCE): at least 30 bronze gears computing astronomical cycles — solar, lunar, eclipse prediction
- The 127-tooth gear is key: encodes the Metonic cycle (19-year lunar-solar alignment); odd prime tooth count makes it hard to manufacture
- Modern replica machining reveals tolerances and fit required — validates and deepens understanding of original construction
- Tony Freeth and UCL team produced the most complete 2021 reconstruction, proposing the full front panel layout
- Broader lesson: the gap between ancient and medieval technology is partly a material availability / institutional continuity problem

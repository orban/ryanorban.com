## Summary

Andrew Chen's post challenged the reflexive skepticism that greets consumer products without obvious business models. Written in 2012, when Twitter, Instagram, Pinterest, and Tumblr were all growing fast without clear paths to monetization, Chen argued that the question itself was poorly framed. Consumer tech products at scale almost always find revenue; the question at the early stage is whether the product is genuinely useful and growing.

The argument: monetization is solvable once you have a large, engaged user base. The companies that fail don't typically fail because they couldn't figure out how to charge for their product — they fail because they never built something enough people wanted. Inversely, companies that focus too early on monetization often prune the product features that users actually value (an early paywall, aggressive ads, selling data) and hollow out the user experience before they've achieved scale.

The examples in 2012 were instructive: Instagram had been acquired by Facebook for $1 billion with essentially no revenue but 30 million users. Twitter had been figuring out promoted tweets and Twitter ads since 2010. Pinterest was monetizing via affiliate links quietly while everyone asked but how do they make money? Google itself was technically a search company with no clear business model for years before AdWords proved the answer.

## Key points

- How will they make money? is premature for pre-scale consumer products — monetization is solvable; product-market fit isn't.
- Andrew Chen argues sequencing matters: build a product people love first, find revenue model second.
- Instagram sold for $1B with ~zero revenue in 2012 — the asset wasn't the revenue, it was 30M engaged users.
- Premature monetization can damage the product before it reaches the scale needed for attractive ad/subscription economics.
- The pattern (build users → solve monetization later) is now standard tech doctrine; in 2012 it still sparked debate.

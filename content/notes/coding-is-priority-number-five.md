## Summary

Benjamin Pollack (bitquabit) argues that for engineers working on startups, writing code is the fifth most important thing you can do — behind understanding your users, identifying the right problem, validating solutions before building them, and shipping frequently enough to learn. The post is a corrective to a specific failure mode in technical founders: building product before understanding users, because building is what they're comfortable with.

The hierarchy Pollack proposes (roughly): first, understand what problem you're actually solving. Second, understand who has that problem and how badly. Third, find the simplest possible solution that could address it. Fourth, get that solution in front of users as fast as possible. Fifth, write the actual code. The implication is that most coding done by engineers early in a startup is either solving the wrong problem or solving the right problem at the wrong scale.

This connects to the Lean Startup ideas popularized around the same time by Eric Ries — particularly the minimum viable product concept and the build-measure-learn loop. But Pollack's framing is more direct about the psychological difficulty: engineers derive satisfaction and confidence from building things, and that drive can become a form of avoidance when the harder work is talking to customers.

## Key points

- The instinct to build before validating is a psychological trap, not just a strategic mistake — building feels productive when customer conversations feel uncertain.
- The sequence matters: understanding the user beats understanding the problem beats understanding the solution beats building.
- Engineering quality matters eventually but is rarely the bottleneck in early startup failure — most failures are product-market fit failures.
- "Code is the thing you have left when you've eliminated every other way to solve the problem" — a deliberate reversal of the typical engineer's hierarchy.
- Connects to Kent Beck's "make it work, make it right, make it fast" — sequencing still matters even within the coding stage.

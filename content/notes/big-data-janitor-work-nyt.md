## Summary

This August 2014 New York Times article by Steve Lohr is the piece that brought the janitor work framing for data wrangling into mainstream consciousness. Based on interviews with data scientists at major companies, it reported that 50-80% of a data scientist's time is spent on data preparation — cleaning, collecting, organizing — rather than the modeling and analysis that the job is nominally about.

The article quotes multiple practitioners describing the gap between the public narrative of data science (sophisticated algorithms finding hidden insights) and the daily reality (fixing encoding errors, merging data sources with inconsistent schemas, handling missing values, tracking down the owner of a table that hasn't been updated in six months). This gap was well-known within the data science community but hadn't been articulated for a general audience.

The importance of the article: it shifted the conversation about what data science actually requires. Before this, the job was often described primarily as modeling and statistics. After this, data preparation became a recognized first-class SKILL, driving investment in tools for data engineering, data quality, and ETL automation. It also validated what bootcamps like Zipfian Academy were teaching — the practical messy side of data work, not just the algorithms.

## Key points

- 50-80% of data science time is data wrangling, not modeling — the "janitor work" statistic.
- Published August 2014 in the New York Times — brought this reality to mainstream awareness.
- Named the problem that drove investment in data engineering tools: dbt, Great Expectations, Apache Airflow.
- Common janitor tasks: encoding fixes, schema reconciliation, missing values, data provenance tracking.
- NYT framing legitimized what bootcamps were teaching — practical data work, not just algorithms.
- Led to the 2010s explosion of data quality and data reliability engineering as professional specialties.

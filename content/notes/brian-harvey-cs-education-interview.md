## Summary

The Daily Californian (Berkeley's student newspaper) interviewed Brian Harvey in November 2012, giving him a platform to discuss his work on CS education and the philosophy behind it. Harvey was both a prolific contributor to Berkeley's intro CS curriculum (CS61A, CS10) and the creator of Snap! (formerly BYOB) — a visual programming language extending Scratch with first-class procedures and higher-order functions.

Harvey's career at Berkeley spanned the transition from SICP-based teaching to more accessible entry points. He's a rare educator who both advocated for conceptual depth (SICP's abstraction-first approach) and built tools to make programming more accessible (Snap!'s visual blocks for beginners). His argument isn't a contradiction: different entry points are appropriate for different goals. Snap! is for students who've never programmed; SICP is for students committed to computer science as a discipline.

Snap! (used in Berkeley's CS10 Beauty and Joy of Computing course) extends Scratch by adding first-class functions, recursion, and proper lambda support — features that bring it closer to Scheme in expressive power while keeping the visual block interface that lowers the syntax barrier. Harvey's approach: meet students where they are while not hiding the depth underneath.

## Key points

- Brian Harvey dual contributions: SICP-based CS61A (depth-first) and Snap! visual language (accessibility-first)
- Snap! extends Scratch with first-class functions and higher-order functions — a gateway to real CS concepts
- CS10 "Beauty and Joy of Computing" used Snap! as its primary tool — accessible entry to CS for non-majors
- Harvey's philosophy: accessible doesn't mean shallow; tools should lower syntax barriers without hiding concepts
- The Daily Californian interview context: Berkeley CS curriculum debates were live news in 2012

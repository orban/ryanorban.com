## Summary

This essay by Michael Halligan, an infrastructure architect, made a simple and often-ignored argument in 2013: experienced engineers with families, mortgages, and financial obligations couldn't take the compensation risks that early-stage Y Combinator startups offered, regardless of the equity upside. Health insurance, 401k matching, paid time off, and predictable salary weren't perks — they were baseline requirements for anyone managing a household, and startups that treated them as optional were selecting for a specific demographic (young, mobile, without dependents) at the expense of depth of experience.

The subtext was also about the infrastructure engineering specifically: the kind of senior infrastructure architects who could design and build reliable distributed systems were disproportionately in the 35-50 age range, had hard-won experience from running production systems at scale, and had exactly the financial obligations (family health coverage, retirement savings, kids' college, mortgage) that made equity-heavy, benefits-light startup offers untenable. The implicit message: if you want that expertise, you have to pay for it like a grown-up business.

In 2013, the Valley startup culture mythology was at its peak — the "work for equity and change the world" pitch was ubiquitous and unquestioned in TechCrunch coverage. Halligan's post was a practitioner's rebuke: the people you need to build real infrastructure aren't 22-year-old engineers willing to trade salary for ping pong tables. The essay resonated because it stated plainly what many experienced engineers thought but rarely said.

## Key points

- Health insurance as non-negotiable: in the US pre-ACA individual market (2013), family health insurance at startup rates was often $1,000+/month — equity doesn't cover that.
- 401k matching and vesting: experienced engineers have retirement timelines; skipping employer matching is real compensation forgone, not a minor benefit.
- The demographic selection effect: benefits-light offers select for young, mobile, unencumbered engineers — structurally excluding experienced mid-career practitioners.
- Infrastructure depth requires experience: distributed systems, database reliability, networking at scale — these skills accrue over careers, not bootcamps.
- A critique of startup culture mythology: change the world narratives that treat adult financial obligations as unreasonable demands.

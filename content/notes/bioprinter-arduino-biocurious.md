## Summary

PatrikD (Patrik D'haeseleer, a bioinformatician and BioCurious member) posted a photo of a functional bioprinter built from two salvaged CD drive mechanisms, an Arduino microcontroller, and an Inkshield (an Arduino shield for controlling inkjet printer heads). The cost of components: likely under $100. The machine could deposit liquids in precise patterns — a functional, if crude, bioprinter.

This was a characteristic moment for BioCurious, the community biology lab in Sunnyvale, California. The lab attracted people who wanted to apply maker culture and hardware hacking sensibilities to biology. The CD drive tray mechanisms provided linear motion (the X/Y axes for a simple plotter), the Arduino controlled the movement, and the Inkshield drove the inkjet head to deposit fluid. The principle was identical to professional bioprinting equipment — just executed with hobbyist hardware.

This was the era when RepRap 3D printers were demonstrating that commercial manufacturing equipment could be replicated for a fraction of the cost by motivated hobbyists. The same instinct applied to biological equipment: centrifuges built from hard drives, PCR machines from thermoelectric coolers, bioprinters from CD drives. The OpenBio and Biohacking communities were building a toolkit for citizen biology.

## Key points

- CD drives provide ready-made linear actuators — the tray mechanism is a precise, controllable axis of motion.
- Arduino + Inkshield drives the inkjet head for fluid deposition — same functional principle as $100K professional bioprinters.
- BioCurious (Sunnyvale) was the locus of this DIY bio activity in the Bay Area circa 2012.
- Reflects RepRap-era thinking: any lab instrument can be replicated cheaply with maker hardware.
- Patrik D'haeseleer was part of the BioCurious founding community — a bioinformatician bringing computational biology into the community lab setting.

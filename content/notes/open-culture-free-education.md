## Summary

Open Culture (openculture.com) is a curated aggregator of free educational content across the web, covering online courses, e-books, audiobooks, language learning, documentaries, and cultural media. It was founded in 2006 by Dan Colman at Stanford, before MOOCs became a category, as a resource for anyone who wanted self-directed liberal education using what universities were already publishing freely online.

The model predates the MOOC wave. Open Culture didn't create content — it found and organized what already existed: MIT OpenCourseWare lectures, Yale Open Yale Courses, BBC documentaries, Project Gutenberg texts, and similar freely-available material. The value was curation and discovery. By 2012, when Coursera and edX launched, Open Culture already had thousands of organized resources and an audience that found curated discovery preferable to searching Google.

What distinguishes Open Culture is its humanities orientation. While most tech education resources skew technical, Open Culture covers literature, philosophy, history, music, and film alongside science and mathematics. The implicit argument is that the internet can deliver access to a classical education — not just vocational training — to anyone with a browser. This was a meaningful claim in 2006 and remains true: a motivated reader can cover more of the Western canon through Open Culture than through most formal education programs.

## Key points

- Aggregator model: Open Culture curates free content from universities, broadcasters, and archives rather than producing it — the value is discovery and organization.
- Pre-MOOC: launched 2006, before Coursera (2012), edX (2012), or Udacity (2012) — established the idea that university-level free content could be organized and discovered.
- Humanities-forward: unusual emphasis on literature, philosophy, history, and arts alongside STEM — an implicit argument for broad intellectual development.
- Audience: self-directed learners, lifelong education seekers, people who can't or don't want traditional university formats.
- Durability: still active and well-maintained in 2026 — one of the few web resources from the 2000s that survived and remained useful.
